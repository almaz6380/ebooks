## BPC-157

Hardly any peptide is searched for, recommended and sold online as often as BPC-157. In forums and videos, it is considered a miracle cure for tendons, ligaments, muscles, the gut and even the brain. Anyone who reads the scientific literature will indeed find well over two hundred publications. The picture changes, however, as soon as you ask how many of them studied humans: there are only a handful – small, uncontrolled and from a single private practice. This chapter puts into perspective what is proven, what has only been shown in rats and where the risks lie.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** synthetic peptide of 15 amino acids (pentadecapeptide) with the sequence GEPPPGKPADDAGLV (Cox 2017, PMID 28035768).
- **Origin:** derived from a larger protein described in human gastric juice in the 1990s ("Body Protection Compound," hence BPC). The BPC-157 sold is a laboratory product; whether the sequence occurs in this form in the body has not been independently confirmed.
- **Former development names:** PL 14736, PL-10, PLD-116 (Pliva company, Croatia), later "Bepecin" or PCO-02.
- **Mechanism of action (presumed, from animal studies):** promotion of new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) via VEGF receptor 2 and the nitric oxide system, effect on the migration of connective tissue cells.
- **Approval EU:** no. **Approval USA:** no; classified by the FDA in 2023 as a substance with safety concerns for compounding (Category 2).
- **WADA:** prohibited, group S0 "non-approved substances" (named explicitly).
- **Evidence level: C** – only three very small, uncontrolled investigations in humans with a combined total of around 30 people; no completed randomized trial.

### How it is supposed to work

The basic idea comes from gastric research. The gastric mucosa is exposed daily to acid, enzymes and medicines such as painkillers, yet repairs itself astonishingly quickly. In the 1990s, researchers in Zagreb suspected that protective substances in gastric juice mediate this ability and isolated a peptide they called "Body Protection Compound." BPC-157 is a short segment of it.

What is unusual is that BPC-157 remains stable in gastric juice. Most peptides are rapidly broken down there. From this, proponents infer that it could also work as a tablet – this has not been proven in humans.

Three mechanisms in particular are discussed for the claimed effect, all of which come from animal and cell experiments:

1. **New blood vessel formation:** In cultures of human vascular endothelial cells, BPC-157 increased the amount of VEGF receptor 2 (a central switch for the growth of new blood vessels) and activated the downstream Akt-eNOS signaling pathway; in rats with a ligated leg artery, blood flow returned more quickly (Hsieh 2017, PMID 27847966). A study from China described a further pathway via the proteins FBXO22 and BACH1 in cell experiments in 2026 (Zhang 2026, PMID 41606641).
2. **Cell migration and survival:** In cultures of rat tendon cells, BPC-157 accelerated the outgrowth of cells from tendon fragments, improved their survival under oxidative stress and promoted their migration; cell division itself was not directly increased (Chang 2011, PMID 21030672).
3. **Sensitivity to growth hormone:** In the same rat tendon cells, BPC-157 increased the number of growth hormone receptors (Chang 2014, PMID 25415472).

In addition, there are numerous other effects on nitric oxide, blood clotting and nerve messengers, described mainly in reviews by the Zagreb group (Sikiric 2025, PMID 40573323). To this day, no clear receptor to which BPC-157 binds is known.

A contradiction pointed out by a pharmaceutical review is striking: in animals, BPC-157 has a half-life of under 30 minutes, yet the described effects are said to last hours to days (Mateescu 2026, PMID 42198317). How this fits together is unclear.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** The automated search for this book found **no** study in PubMed meeting the filter criteria for a human study (`humanstudien_alle`: 0), and **4** entries in the ClinicalTrials.gov study registry. In total, PubMed lists around 230 publications on BPC-157 – almost exclusively animal experiments, laboratory work and review articles.

A targeted follow-up search found three small investigations in humans. All three come from the same first author from a private practice in Florida and appeared in a journal of alternative medicine. A systematic review published in 2026 found exactly these three papers with a combined total of 30 people (Poolton 2026, PMID 42885452).

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knee joint (Lee 2021), PMID 34324435 | 16 people with knee pain of various causes, retrospective chart review with telephone survey, no control group | Look-back 6–12 months | 14 of 16 reported pain relief; 12 received only BPC-157, 4 additionally thymosin beta-4 |
| Interstitial cystitis (Lee 2024), PMID 39325560 | 12 women with chronic bladder pain disorder, injections into the bladder wall during cystoscopy, no control group | one treatment | 10 of 12 reported complete, 2 predominant improvement; no side effects reported |
| Intravenous infusion (Lee 2025), PMID 40131143 | 2 people, safety test on two days | 3 days | no measurable changes in the blood values examined, no complaints |

#### How meaningful are these data?

Hardly at all. The knee investigation was not a planned study but a retrospective telephone survey of patients who had already paid for the treatment. There was no comparison group, no blinding, no measurement instruments for function or quality of life and no imaging. The causes of knee pain ranged from osteoarthritis to meniscus tears. According to the abstract, no standardized questionnaires on function were used (Lee 2021, PMID 34324435).

The cystitis paper recorded the outcome with a single global questionnaire. That all twelve women gave the best possible rating is unusual for a chronic, often difficult-to-treat condition and, without a control group, cannot be distinguished from a placebo effect or the natural fluctuation of symptoms. It is also noteworthy that the BPC-157 used came from a US compounding pharmacy (Lee 2024, PMID 39325560).

The infusion study included two people who had already received BPC-157 before. No conclusion about safety can be drawn from two people – rare or delayed side effects remain invisible this way (Lee 2025, PMID 40131143).

The systematic review rated the methodological quality of all three papers as poor and the certainty of the evidence according to the GRADE system as "very low." Its conclusion: BPC-157 should not be used in humans outside ethically approved, high-quality clinical trials (Poolton 2026, PMID 42885452). Another systematic review from sports orthopedics, which searched up to June 2024, found only a single clinical study among 36 included papers (Vasireddi 2025, PMID 40756949). Several current reviews from orthopedics and sports medicine reach the same conclusion: routine use cannot currently be recommended (Tham 2026, PMID 42937313; Tewari 2026, PMID 42578445).

#### What became of earlier company studies

In the 2000s, the Croatian pharmaceutical company Pliva developed BPC-157 under the code PL 14736 for chronic inflammatory bowel diseases. Animal studies from that time note that the substance was "currently being tested in clinical trials" (Staresinic 2003, PMID 14554208). A review by the Zagreb group mentions studies in ulcerative colitis and multiple sclerosis (Seiwerth 2021, PMID 34267654). Complete results of these studies cannot be found in PubMed as an original paper. In 2015, a Phase 1 trial with tablets ("Bepecin," PCO-02) in 42 healthy volunteers was also registered (NCT02637284); its status has been "unknown" for years, and results have not been published.

For readers, this means: when websites claim that BPC-157 was "safe in clinical trials," they are referring to studies whose data no one can independently verify.

### Animal and laboratory studies

The preclinical work is the reason BPC-157 became so well known. It is extensive, but unusually one-sided in its origin.

#### One research group dominates

Of the around 230 hits in PubMed (search October 2026), about 175 name the Zagreb pharmacologist Predrag Sikirić as an author – that is, around three quarters. A review on tendon and muscle healing noted that in two decades only a handful of research groups have studied BPC-157 in depth (Gwyer 2019, PMID 30915550). This is important for the assessment: scientific results are only considered robust once independent groups replicate them. A critical 2026 review on circulatory disorders in rodents criticizes short observation periods, single-dose experiments and incompletely described risks of bias, and calls for independent, blinded replications (Demirtaş 2026, PMID 42794771). A short commentary in the journal Inflammopharmacology in 2025 specifically addressed a long review by this group (Whitehouse 2025, PMID 40759852).

Independent work does exist – especially from Taiwan (tendon cells, blood vessels), South Korea and China – but it is considerably rarer and predominantly laboratory or cell experiments.

In addition, there are papers on the brain, eye, heart, bladder, poisonings and metabolic derangements – in rats and mice. Proponents read this enormous range as a strength. From a methodological point of view, it is more of a warning sign: a substance that improves almost every disorder in almost every model tested requires particularly careful, independent verification.

#### Pharmacokinetics and toxicology

A Chinese research group studied BPC-157 according to the usual rules of drug development. In rats and beagle dogs, the half-life of the unchanged peptide was under 30 minutes; after injection into the muscle, about 14 to 19 percent reached the bloodstream in rats and 45 to 51 percent in dogs. BPC-157 was rapidly broken down into small fragments and amino acids and excreted via urine and bile (He 2022, PMID 36588717). In toxicity tests in mice, rats, rabbits and dogs, the same researchers found no serious damage, no genetic damage and no harm to embryos; in dogs, the creatinine level fell temporarily at the highest dose (Xu 2020, PMID 32334036). Such animal data are a prerequisite for human studies but do not replace them.

### Risks and side effects

#### Documented

There are practically no documented side effects in humans – but not because BPC-157 has been shown to be safe, but because hardly anyone has systematically looked for them. The three pilot investigations with a combined total of around 30 people reported no side effects (Poolton 2026, PMID 42885452). With 30 people, only common, immediately occurring problems can be detected. A side effect affecting one in a thousand people would remain invisible.

#### Theoretical: new blood vessel formation and tumor growth

The most frequently discussed theoretical risk follows directly from the presumed mechanism of action. If BPC-157 promotes the formation of new blood vessels, it could – so the concern goes – also improve the blood supply of existing, perhaps still undetected tumors. Tumors of a few millimeters or more need their own blood vessels to keep growing. This is why inhibitors of the VEGF pathway are established cancer drugs. A substance that upregulates VEGF receptor 2 in cell cultures (Hsieh 2017, PMID 27847966) warrants caution here. Several reviews explicitly name pathological new blood vessel formation and possible cancer promotion as an unresolved risk (Moiz 2026, PMID 42757290).

The Zagreb group disagrees: BPC-157 regulates blood vessel formation rather than blindly boosting it, prevents, for example, pathological vessel ingrowth in the cornea of the eye and even shows anti-tumor effects in animal and cell experiments (Sikiric 2025, PMID 40573323). These data, again, come predominantly from the same group. Long-term studies on cancer development (carcinogenicity studies), as are customary for approved medicines, have not been published for BPC-157. The question is therefore open – neither confirmed nor refuted. Part IV of this book deals with the topic of growth signals and cancer risk in detail.

#### Gray-market risks

The greatest practical risk probably lies not in the substance itself but in the products. BPC-157 is mostly sold as a "research chemical" without pharmaceutical quality. Reviews name contaminants, incorrect concentrations, lack of sterility and fluctuating manufacturing quality as concrete dangers (Tham 2026, PMID 42937313; Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639). As early as 2017, an anti-doping laboratory described BPC-157 from seized vials (Cox 2017, PMID 28035768). With injections, there are additional infection risks from non-sterile solutions. Chapter I.5 and Part IV deal with laboratory analyses of gray-market peptides in detail.

#### Further open questions

- **Interactions:** In animal experiments, BPC-157 influenced blood clotting, blood pressure and nervous system messengers (Sikiric 2025, PMID 40573323). Whether this is relevant in humans for people on anticoagulants, with cardiovascular disease or on psychiatric medication, no one knows.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding, children and adolescents:** no human data.
- **Placebo effect:** A sports medicine review points out that social media can amplify the placebo effect with such peptides (Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639). Personal success stories are therefore no proof of efficacy.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU:** BPC-157 is not approved as a medicine in any member state. A substance offered for the treatment of diseases in humans is legally considered a medicine; without approval it may not be placed on the market. The notice "for research purposes only" does not change this if the product is evidently intended for use in humans.
- **Germany and Austria:** no approval, and no basis for pharmacy compounding either. Supplying it for use in humans is impermissible under the respective medicines act. In sport, anti-doping rules additionally apply.
- **USA:** no FDA approval. In 2023, the FDA assigned BPC-157 to Category 2 among the bulk substances for compounding – substances for which there are significant safety concerns. In the agency's view, compounding pharmacies may therefore not process it. The classification of peptides has since been debated controversially in the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) and in professional circles (Willis 2026, PMID 42752426); changes are possible.
- **WADA:** BPC-157 has been listed by name in group S0 (non-approved substances) of the Prohibited List since 2022 and is thus prohibited in and out of competition. A 2025 review states, to the contrary, that BPC-157 is currently not listed (Józwiak 2025, PMID 40005999) – to the author's knowledge, this is not correct for the current list. Anti-doping laboratories have developed detection methods in urine (Tian 2023, PMID 37959764).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

As of October 2026, four trials with BPC-157 are entered on ClinicalTrials.gov:

| Study | Design | Status | Topic |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCT07437547 | Phase 2, randomized, double-blind, placebo, 120 people planned | according to registry recruiting since February 2026; authenticity questionable (see text) | acute hamstring strain; time to return to sport, MRI findings |
| NCT07803250 | Phase 1, randomized, 30 people planned | not yet recruiting, start planned 2027 | healing after rotator cuff surgery (shoulder) |
| NCT07752381 | single group without control, 40 people | completed 2025, no results in registry | a manufacturer's gummies; inflammatory markers and self-assessment |
| NCT02637284 | Phase 1, tablets, 42 healthy people planned | status unknown since 2016 | safety and pharmacokinetics |

Little is **confirmed**. On paper, the Phase 2 trial on hamstring strains (NCT07437547) would be the first randomized, placebo-controlled trial with an objective endpoint (MRI) and adequate size. When checked for this book, however, it was noticed that the same sponsor ("Hudson Biotech"), with an identical study site and identical contact person, has created a second entry on TB-500 (NCT07487363) which, in its own description, explicitly calls itself a "fictitious" example of a registry entry. Whether the BPC-157 trial is actually being conducted therefore cannot be stated reliably. ClinicalTrials.gov checks entries only formally, not for content – registration alone does not prove that a trial is taking place.

The shoulder trial at the University of Arkansas (NCT07803250) is randomized and blinded, but small and has not yet begun. A manufacturer's gummy trial (NCT07752381) has no control group and mainly measures self-reports; it will not answer the open questions.

**Author's assessment:** No robust efficacy data in humans are to be expected before 2028. Even a positive result from a small pilot study would only be a first indication – not yet a basis for approval, which requires several larger trials and long-term safety data. A negative result would probably do little to change the peptide's online reputation. A pharmaceutical review emphasizes that to this day there is no standardized, validated pharmaceutical form (Mateescu 2026, PMID 42198317) – this, too, must be resolved before approval.

### Conclusion

BPC-157 is a peptide with an unusually large number of animal studies, almost all of which report positive results – predominantly from a single Zagreb research group and without sufficient independent replication. In humans, there are only three small, uncontrolled investigations with a combined total of around 30 people, whose quality is rated as very low (Poolton 2026, PMID 42885452). Long-term safety, in particular the theoretical question of promotion of tumor growth via new blood vessel formation, has not been studied, and gray-market products bring their own risks. Randomized trials are only registered or planned so far (NCT07803250); until robust results are available, BPC-157 remains an experimental substance whose benefit in humans has not been proven. For athletes, it is prohibited under WADA rules.
