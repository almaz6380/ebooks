## Glutathione

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** tripeptide of three amino acids – glutamic acid, cysteine and glycine (γ-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine, abbreviated GSH). The reduced form (GSH) is the active one; in the defense against oxidation, two molecules are joined to form oxidized glutathione (GSSG).
- **Origin:** produced by virtually all body cells themselves, in particularly large amounts in the liver. As a preparation, it is usually produced by fermentation with yeasts.
- **Mechanism of action:** the most important endogenous antioxidant in cells; involved in the detoxification of medications and pollutants (for example in the breakdown of acetaminophen) and in the regulation of immune cells. In the skin, it shifts pigment production from dark to lighter melanin.
- **EU approval:** widely marketed as a dietary supplement; as an injectable medicine only in individual countries for narrowly defined indications, not for skin lightening (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **US approval:** no FDA-approved glutathione medicine; available as a dietary supplement and as a pharmacy compounded preparation.
- **WADA:** glutathione itself is not prohibited. However, infusions or injections of more than 100 mL within twelve hours are covered as a prohibited method (M2), except in the context of hospital treatment or clinical investigation (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – numerous randomized, mostly small studies with varying results; no approval based on large outcome trials.

### How it is supposed to work

Glutathione is an entirely ordinary component of every cell. It scavenges reactive oxygen species, helps enzymes with detoxification and keeps other antioxidants such as vitamins C and E in their active form. In many diseases – such as diabetes, liver disease, HIV, Parkinson's – and with increasing age, glutathione levels in blood or tissue are lower. This gave rise to the obvious idea of supplying glutathione from outside.

This is more difficult than it sounds, for three reasons:

1. **Digestion:** As a peptide, glutathione is partially broken down in the gut; for a long time, it was therefore considered barely effective when swallowed.
2. **Uptake into cells:** Glutathione is mainly produced *inside* cells; it enters them from the blood only to a limited extent.
3. **Short residence time:** Infused glutathione is rapidly broken down in the blood.

Various dosage forms are therefore offered: capsules and tablets, **liposomal** glutathione (packaged in lipid vesicles), lozenges and films for the oral mucosa, nasal spray, creams – and **infusions** into a vein. There are also precursors such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC) or glycine plus NAC, which the body uses for its own production; they are not the subject of this chapter.

For skin lightening, a further mechanism is assumed: in laboratory experiments, glutathione inhibits the enzyme tyrosinase and redirects pigment production from dark eumelanin to lighter pheomelanin.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 161 publications classified as human studies, plus 15 reviews and meta-analyses. On ClinicalTrials.gov, 273 studies were registered. Both figures considerably overstate the direct evidence: many hits only measure glutathione as a laboratory value (for example with selenium, vitamins, exercise or NAC) or concern the enzyme glutathione S-transferase. Studies in which glutathione itself was administered are a minority – but still several dozen.

#### Does swallowed glutathione even get there?

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allen 2011, PMID 21875351 | 40 healthy people, capsules vs. placebo, double-blind | 4 weeks | no change in glutathione levels or oxidation markers |
| Richie 2015, PMID 24791752 | 54 healthy people, two doses vs. placebo, double-blind | 6 months | levels in blood cells and plasma 30–35% higher (high dose); back to baseline after stopping |
| Sinha 2018, PMID 28853742 | 12 healthy people, liposomal glutathione, no placebo | 4 weeks | levels in whole blood up to 40% higher, in immune cells up to 100% |
| Schmitt 2015, PMID 26262996 | 20 people with metabolic syndrome, crossover: capsule, sublingual form, NAC | 3 weeks each | sublingual form raised plasma levels more than capsules |
| Jung 2026, PMID 42392376 | healthy people, orally dissolving film vs. tablet | single dose and 4 weeks | both forms raised blood levels; no change in oxidation markers |

The question was long disputed. A short study with 40 healthy people found no change whatsoever after four weeks (Allen 2011, PMID 21875351). A longer, likewise double-blind study with 54 healthy people, by contrast, found dose-dependently higher glutathione levels in blood, blood cells and oral mucosa after six months; one month after stopping, the values were back to baseline (Richie 2015, PMID 24791752). A small pilot study without a placebo group reported increases with liposomal glutathione after just one to two weeks (Sinha 2018, PMID 28853742).

In summary: swallowed glutathione can measurably increase blood levels, presumably depending on dose, duration and dosage form. Whether higher blood levels also lead to a health benefit is another question.

#### Disease-related studies

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leal 2014, PMID 24619793 | 185 cancer patients on paclitaxel/carboplatin, infusion vs. placebo, phase 3 | duration of chemotherapy | **no** reduction in chemotherapy-induced nerve damage |
| Hauser 2009, PMID 19230029 | 21 people with Parkinson's, infusion vs. placebo | 4 weeks | no significant difference on the Parkinson's scale |
| Mischley 2017, PMID 28436395 | 45 people with Parkinson's, nasal spray at two doses vs. placebo | 3 months | all groups improved, glutathione not better than placebo |
| Bozic 2020, PMID 32960827 | 58 children with cystic fibrosis, capsules vs. placebo | 24 weeks | no effect on growth and inflammatory markers |
| Søndergård 2021, PMID 33740389 | 20 overweight men with and without type 2 diabetes, vs. placebo | 3 weeks | insulin sensitivity improved; oxidation markers unchanged |
| Honda 2017, PMID 28789631 | 34 people with fatty liver, open-label, no control group | 4 months | liver enzyme ALT decreased |
| Arrivi 2023, PMID 36083043 | 100 people with heart attack, infusion vs. placebo (secondary analysis) | hospital stay | hospital stay 8.6 instead of 10.8 days |

The picture is sobering: the largest and methodologically strongest study – a phase 3 trial with 185 cancer patients – found no protection against chemotherapy-induced nerve damage; on some secondary endpoints, placebo even performed better (Leal 2014, PMID 24619793). In Parkinson's, glutathione was better than placebo neither as an infusion (Hauser 2009, PMID 19230029) nor as a nasal spray; in the nasal spray study, one person on the higher dose developed a heart muscle disease (Mischley 2017, PMID 28436395). In children with cystic fibrosis, growth was unaffected (Bozic 2020, PMID 32960827).

Positive signals come from small studies: in 20 overweight men, insulin sensitivity improved after three weeks (Søndergård 2021, PMID 33740389); in fatty liver, liver values decreased in an uncontrolled study (Honda 2017, PMID 28789631); after heart attack, the length of hospital stay was shorter in a secondary analysis (Arrivi 2023, PMID 36083043). None of these findings has yet been confirmed in a larger study.

#### Skin lightening: oral, topical, intravenous

In parts of Asia, Africa and increasingly also in Europe, glutathione is marketed mainly as a "skin lightener" – in capsules, creams and in the form of infusions in beauty clinics.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arjinpathana 2012, PMID 20524875 | 60 medical students in Thailand, capsules vs. placebo | 4 weeks | melanin index lowered more at 2 of 6 measurement sites |
| Weschawalit 2017, PMID 28490897 | healthy women, reduced or oxidized glutathione vs. placebo | 12 weeks | melanin index tended to be lower; in some cases fewer wrinkles |
| Handog 2016, PMID 26148180 | 30 women in the Philippines, lozenge, no control group | 8 weeks | melanin index decreased |
| Wahab 2021, PMID 33871071 | 46 people, combination of cream and capsule vs. placebo | 8 weeks | combination possibly more effective than single use |

**Oral and topical:** Several small randomized studies show slight lightening, especially at sun-exposed sites. In the first study, the effect was statistically significant at only two of six measurement sites (Arjinpathana 2012, PMID 20524875). A 2019 systematic review concluded that, because of study quality and contradictory findings, the evidence was "not yet conclusive" (Dilokthornsakul 2019, PMID 30895708). A more recent review rates creams and capsules as moderately effective, with effects that do not persist after stopping (Sarkar 2025, PMID 39444151).

**Intravenous:** According to the same review, there is only one placebo-controlled study of infusions for skin lightening, and it found no significant difference (37.5% versus 18.7%, p = 0.054). The authors consider intravenous glutathione for skin lightening **contraindicated**, that is, not justifiable, because of lack of efficacy and side effects (Sarkar 2025, PMID 39444151). A South African review found not a single study on the safety of repeated infusions for any use (Davids 2016, PMID 27499402). In the British Medical Journal, the practice was criticized as "unethical bleaching" (Dadzie 2016, PMID 27581922).

### Animal and laboratory studies

Laboratory research on glutathione is enormously extensive and touches almost every area of cell biology. Relevant for this book is above all that in cell and animal experiments, glutathione or its precursors were able to protect against oxidative damage, drug toxicity and inflammatory reactions – effects that are found only partially and mostly weakly in the clinical studies in humans.

### Risks and side effects

**Oral intake:** well tolerated in the studies; occasional gastrointestinal complaints are reported. Long-term data covering more than six months are largely lacking.

**Nasal spray:** In a Parkinson's study, one person on the higher dose developed a heart muscle disease; a connection has not been established (Mischley 2017, PMID 28436395).

**Infusions – the real risks:**

- **Contaminated compounded preparations:** In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) warned pharmacies against using glutathione from a particular wholesaler for infusions after patients developed nausea, vomiting and shortness of breath following infusions and elevated endotoxin levels (bacterial components that can cause fever and shock) were measured. In August 2026, the FDA again urged pharmacies not to use dietary-supplement-grade glutathione for injections; infusions from a particular batch had led to fever, chills, shock-like conditions and hospital admissions.
- **Regulatory warnings on skin lightening:** The Philippine drug regulatory authority warned against glutathione injections for skin lightening in 2011 and 2019; it cited side effects ranging from skin rashes to the life-threatening skin reactions Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, as well as risks from improper injections such as sepsis and transmission of infections. The US FDA also warned consumers that no injectable products for skin lightening are approved and that such products may contain unknown, harmful ingredients.
- **Skin cancer (theoretical):** The shift from dark to light melanin could reduce the skin's natural sun protection (Davids 2016, PMID 27499402).

**Interactions:** In a cancer study, glutathione was given before chemotherapy; the time to disease progression there did not differ from placebo (Leal 2014, PMID 24619793). In general, it is debated whether antioxidants could weaken the effect of some cancer therapies. Anyone undergoing cancer treatment should take glutathione only after consulting their treatment team.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** Glutathione is sold as a dietary supplement in capsules and powders; health claims in advertising are not permitted without authorized health claims. Infusions and injections are medicines; preparations for skin lightening by infusion are not approved. In individual countries (to our knowledge, for example, Italy) there are approved glutathione injections for specific medical uses; please refer to the respective prescribing information for details (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **USA:** no approved glutathione medicine; dietary supplements are permitted, and infusions are produced as pharmacy compounded preparations and are subject to FDA requirements for sterile preparations.
- **Sport:** glutathione is not prohibited; infusions of more than 100 mL in twelve hours outside of hospital treatment fall under WADA method M2 (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established (registered studies, as of October 2026):**

- A phase 1 trial in China is testing glutathione in addition to immunotherapy and chemotherapy in advanced lung cancer (NCT06896422, 80 planned participants).
- A study on glutathione-assisted microneedling in melasma (pigment patches) is registered (NCT07448415).
- Several small studies are examining new dosage forms (liposomal, micellar) and recovery from exercise, frequently funded by manufacturers.

**Assessment:** The most important open question is no longer whether glutathione is absorbed, but whether higher levels have any clinical benefit. The large studies to date tend to argue against it. For infusions for skin lightening or as a "wellness" drip, the risks outweigh an unproven benefit.

### Conclusion

Glutathione is the body's most important protective peptide against oxidation; taken as a capsule, it can measurably raise blood levels with longer-term intake. Clinically, however, the larger studies showed no benefit – neither against chemotherapy-induced nerve damage nor in Parkinson's or cystic fibrosis; positive indications come from small studies. Capsules and creams lightened the skin slightly and temporarily in small studies. Infusions for skin lightening have not been shown to be effective, are not approved and have been the subject of explicit warnings by several authorities because of serious side effects and contaminated preparations.
