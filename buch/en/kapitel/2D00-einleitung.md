# Part II · D – Brain, Sleep and Longevity

Hardly any group of peptides is promoted on the internet as promisingly as this one: agents for better concentration and memory ("nootropics"), for deeper sleep, for longer telomeres and "younger mitochondria." What the twelve substances in this section have in common is that they target the brain, cellular aging or energy metabolism – and that the gap between promise and evidence is particularly wide here.

### What these peptides have in common

The group can be roughly divided into three families:

- **Russian neuropeptides and "bioregulators":** Semax, Selank, DSIP, Epitalon and Pinealon come from Soviet and Russian research. Cerebrolysin, a peptide mixture from pig brain, comes from Austria but is used mainly in Eastern Europe and Asia.
- **Mitochondrial peptides:** MOTS-c and humanin are endogenous signaling molecules encoded in the genome of the mitochondria. SS-31 (elamipretide) is an artificial peptide that binds specifically to the mitochondrial membrane.
- **Experimental "anti-aging" molecules:** Dihexa (a derivative of angiotensin IV) and FOXO4-DRI (a senolytic intended to eliminate aged cells) have never been studied in humans. Glutathione, finally, is an endogenous tripeptide sold as a dietary supplement and as an infusion.

### The nootropics and longevity scene

Anyone searching the internet for these substances quickly ends up in forums, with influencers and at "longevity clinics." Three fallacies are typical, and you will encounter them again and again in the following chapters:

1. **From animal to human:** The fact that a peptide improves memory or lifespan in mice says little about whether it works in humans. In stroke and Alzheimer's research, hundreds of agents that were successful in animals have failed in humans.
2. **From level to injection:** The fact that people with low MOTS-c or humanin levels are more often ill does not prove that supplementing them makes people healthy.
3. **From mechanism to benefit:** A coherent mechanism – "protects the mitochondria," "lengthens telomeres" – is not efficacy. SS-31 is the best example of this: it was tested in several large trials and missed its primary endpoints in heart attack, heart failure and mitochondrial myopathy.

On top of this, almost all of these substances are sold on the gray market as "research peptides" – without quality control (see Part I and Part IV).

### Russian peptide research and its publication record

A large part of the literature on Semax, Selank, DSIP, Epitalon and Pinealon comes from a few institutes in Moscow and St. Petersburg. That is no reason to reject the research wholesale – but it is a reason for caution:

- **Approval without international standards:** Some peptides are registered as medicines in Russia without large, blinded, preregistered trials having been published of the kind required by the EU or the USA.
- **Small, uncontrolled studies:** Many clinical papers have no placebo group, no randomization, or give no figures in the abstract.
- **Language and access:** Many papers appear only in Russian, often in journals in which the author groups themselves frequently publish.
- **Lack of replication:** Independent groups outside Russia have rarely re-examined the results – and when they have, often with sobering results, as with DSIP.
- **No registry entries:** For Semax, Selank, DSIP and Pinealon, not a single study can be found on ClinicalTrials.gov.

### Reading guide

The chapters follow the uniform structure of this book. In each chapter, pay particular attention to the section "Human studies" and, within it, to the scope of the evidence: for nine of the twelve peptides, there is either no study in which humans received the substance or only very small, methodologically weak studies. The legal situation and FDA status changed several times in 2026; the information reflects the situation as of October 2026 and should be checked for updates.
