## Lactotripeptides (VPP and IPP)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** two tripeptides of three amino acids each: valine-proline-proline (VPP) and isoleucine-proline-proline (IPP). The double proline at the end makes them comparatively resistant to digestive enzymes.
- **Origin:** fragments of the milk protein casein. They are formed when milk is fermented with certain bacteria (above all *Lactobacillus helveticus*), or by enzymatic cleavage of casein with enzymes from the mold *Aspergillus oryzae*. Well-known products were a Japanese sour milk drink (Calpis) and a Finnish dairy product (Evolus from Valio).
- **Mechanism of action:** supposed to inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) – the same enzyme that blood pressure-lowering ACE inhibitors such as ramipril or enalapril block, only many times more weakly.
- **Approval EU:** no approval as a medicine; used as a food ingredient or in food supplements. No authorized health claim regarding blood pressure.
- **Approval USA:** no approval as a drug; available as a dietary supplement.
- **WADA:** not listed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – around 30 randomized studies with a total of several thousand participants, but each small, with contradictory results and without hard endpoints.

### How it is supposed to work

Blood pressure is controlled, among other things, by the renin-angiotensin system. A central step is the conversion of inactive angiotensin I into vasoconstricting angiotensin II by the enzyme ACE. The same enzyme also breaks down bradykinin, a substance that dilates blood vessels. If ACE is inhibited, less angiotensin II and more bradykinin are formed – blood pressure falls. ACE inhibitors, one of the most important groups of blood pressure medications, are based on this principle. Of historical interest: the first ACE inhibitor, captopril, was developed from a peptide in snake venom.

In the 1990s, Japanese researchers isolated two tripeptides from sour milk that inhibit ACE in the test tube. In rats with congenital high blood pressure, a single dose of the sour milk or of the isolated peptides VPP and IPP lowered systolic blood pressure for a few hours; in rats with normal blood pressure, nothing changed (Nakamura 1995, PMID 7673515). This gave rise to the idea of a "blood pressure-lowering food."

For this to work, the peptides must reach the bloodstream intact. A small study showed that IPP is indeed detectable intact in the blood after drinking an enriched yogurt drink – but at concentrations in the picomole-per-liter range (Foltz 2007, PMID 17374660). A Swiss study with twelve healthy men then tested directly whether the peptides inhibit ACE in the body: after seven days of fermented milk, markers of ACE activity and renin remained unchanged, whereas a single tablet of captopril lowered plasma ACE activity by 82%. IPP rose only briefly and slightly in the blood, and VPP was below the detection limit (Wuerzner 2009, PMID 19506528).

This means: the mechanism that is plausible in the laboratory could not be demonstrated in humans at the amounts tested. Other pathways are therefore also being discussed, such as improved function of the inner vessel wall via nitric oxide. So far, these come mainly from cell culture and animal experiments.

### Human studies

The PubMed search evaluated for this book (lactotripeptides or valyl-prolyl-proline, search date October 2026) yielded 21 human studies; on ClinicalTrials.gov, the search "lactotripeptides" found no registry entry. Most studies were conducted before registration became mandatory for nutrition studies. The meta-analyses refer to up to 30 randomized studies, including many Japanese studies that are not fully captured in the PubMed search.

#### The Early Positive Studies

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hata 1996, PMID 8901799 | 30 older people with high blood pressure (mostly on medication), sour milk vs. acidified milk | 8 weeks | Systolic −14.1 mmHg vs. baseline in the sour milk group; placebo unchanged |
| Seppo 2003, PMID 12540390 | 39 people with high blood pressure, fermented milk vs. control | 21 weeks | Difference systolic 6.7 mmHg (p = 0.03), diastolic 3.6 mmHg (not significant) |
| Tuomilehto 2004, PMID 15175633 | 60 people with mild high blood pressure, crossover | 2 × phases | Overall analysis: systolic difference 2.6 mmHg, not significant |
| Jauhiainen 2005, PMID 16364832 | 94 untreated people with high blood pressure, fermented milk vs. control, 24-hour monitoring | 10 weeks | Systolic −4.1 mmHg, diastolic −1.8 mmHg vs. control |

Hata's early study was small, most participants were already taking blood pressure medication, and the comparison was mainly with baseline. Jauhiainen's study is methodologically stronger because it used 24-hour blood pressure monitoring, which is less susceptible to the "white coat effect" at the doctor's office.

#### The Negative Studies from the Netherlands

From 2008 onward, several larger, carefully conducted studies from European countries appeared that found no effect:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engberink 2008, PMID 18086944 | 135 Dutch people with elevated systolic pressure, three production methods of LTP vs. placebo | 8 weeks | No difference from placebo in office, home and 24-hour blood pressure, ACE activity or angiotensin II |
| van der Zander 2008, PMID 19064533 | 275 Dutch people with high blood pressure, enzymatically produced LTP in a yogurt drink vs. placebo | 8 weeks | No difference systolic (p = 0.66) or diastolic (p = 0.72) |
| van Mierlo 2009, PMID 19106238 | 2 crossover studies with 69 and 93 untreated adults, 24-hour monitoring | 4 weeks each | No difference in 24-hour blood pressure |
| Usinger 2010, PMID 20147968 | 94 people with high-normal or borderline blood pressure, 150 or 300 ml fermented milk vs. placebo | 8 weeks | No significant difference in 24-hour blood pressure |

What is remarkable about the Engberink study: it tested lactotripeptides from three sources – from concentrated fermented milk, enzymatically produced and chemically synthesized. None of the three variants lowered blood pressure or measurably inhibited ACE. A review summarized six such studies with 780 participants from the United Kingdom and the Netherlands and concluded that the newer data do not support a role for lactotripeptides in blood pressure regulation (Geleijnse 2010, PMID 19884823).

#### Later Studies

Even after 2010, the results remained mixed. In Finland, a dose of 50 mg of lactotripeptides per day lowered 24-hour blood pressure compared with baseline, but not significantly compared with placebo (−2.6 mmHg systolic); 5 mg per day had no effect (Jauhiainen 2012, PMID 22617279). A spread containing IPP, VPP and plant sterols lowered systolic blood pressure measured at home more than placebo (−4.1 versus −0.5 mmHg), but not the office or 24-hour value (Turpeinen 2012, PMID 22398753). Italian studies reported favorable effects on office blood pressure and arterial stiffness (Cicero 2011, PMID 21753776; Cicero 2016, PMID 26683986), one of them without an effect on 24-hour blood pressure. In the USA, in a study with 91 people with stage 1 and 2 high blood pressure, systolic daytime blood pressure fell by 3.6 mmHg, but not under placebo (Germino 2010, PMID 20433527).

In addition, there are small studies on vascular function, cerebral blood flow, muscle fatigue and cognitive performance, mostly with 15 to 50 participants (e.g. Akazawa 2018, PMID 29703414). They should be read as indications, not as evidence.

#### Meta-Analyses: Small Effect, Strong Influence of Origin

| Meta-analysis | Scope | Effect on systolic blood pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Cicero 2011, PMID 20811398 | 18 studies | −3.73 mmHg overall; Asia −6.93 mmHg; Europe −1.17 mmHg (not significant) |
| Turpeinen 2013, PMID 23305234 | 19 studies, approx. 1,500 people | −4.0 mmHg |
| Qin 2013, PMID 23566677 | 28 study arms, 1,919 people | −1.66 mmHg; in 24-hour blood pressure −1.30 mmHg |
| Cicero 2013, PMID 23382495 | 14 European studies, 1,306 people | −1.28 mmHg |
| Fekete 2015, PMID 25608938 | 30 studies | −2.95 mmHg; 24-hour blood pressure not significant |
| Chanson-Rolle 2015, PMID 26536628 | 18 Japanese studies, 1,194 people | −5.63 mmHg |

Three patterns run through all the analyses:

1. **The effect is small.** Pooled, it is mostly between 1 and 4 mmHg systolic – considerably less than approved blood pressure medications achieve.
2. **Asian, especially Japanese, studies show considerably larger effects than European ones.** Whether this is due to genetic differences, to diet (such as higher salt intake) or to methodological differences is unclear.
3. **Indications of publication bias.** The Fekete meta-analysis found both publication bias and a "small-study effect": small studies showed larger effects. After correction, the systolic reduction shrank and the diastolic one was no longer significant. In 24-hour blood pressure – the most reliable measurement method – the effect was not significant (Fekete 2015, PMID 25608938).

There is no study showing that lactotripeptides prevent heart attacks, strokes or deaths. Given the small effect, such a study is not to be expected either.

### Animal and laboratory studies

In rats with congenital high blood pressure (SHR rats), VPP and IPP lowered blood pressure in a dose-dependent manner; in rats with normal blood pressure, they did not (Nakamura 1995, PMID 7673515). In cell cultures, VPP and IPP stimulated the formation of nitric oxide in vascular cells at concentrations above 100 nanomoles per liter, and in isolated rat aortas they triggered vasodilation (Hirota 2011, PMID 21221598). For comparison: in human blood, less than 1 nanomole per liter of IPP was measured after drinking an enriched beverage (Foltz 2007, PMID 17374660). X-ray structure analyses show that both peptides preferentially bind to one of the two active sites of the ACE molecule (Gregory 2024, PMID 37904282). In rats and rabbits, toxicity studies – including investigations of kidney development in the offspring, because ACE inhibitors can harm the unborn child during pregnancy – found no adverse effects at the highest doses tested (Dent 2007, PMID 17383063). A milk protein hydrolysate containing IPP was not mutagenic in genetic tests; the highest dose tested in the 90-day study in rats was about 141 times the expected maximum intake (Ponstein-Simarro Doorten 2009, PMID 18955104).

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** In the human studies, no more side effects occurred under lactotripeptides than under placebo (e.g. Jauhiainen 2005, PMID 16364832). The peptides come from milk protein, which has always been eaten; the amounts in the products are in the milligram range.

**Theoretical or worth noting:**

- **Milk allergy:** Milk- or casein-based products are unsuitable for people with cow's milk protein allergy.
- **Combination with blood pressure medications:** A relevant enhancement is unlikely given the weak effect, but has not been systematically studied.
- **Pregnancy:** ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in pregnancy. For lactotripeptides, no corresponding harm was found in animal experiments; human data are lacking.
- **The most important risk:** Anyone who treats high blood pressure that requires treatment with a milk drink instead of effective measures forgoes protection against heart attack and stroke. Weight reduction, less salt, exercise and – if necessary – medication demonstrably lower blood pressure more.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** Lactotripeptides are food ingredients. Health claims regarding blood pressure are **not authorized** under the Health Claims Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006). The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) assessed applications concerning lactotripeptides and the maintenance of normal blood pressure and concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship has not been sufficiently established. Decisive factors included the contradictory European studies. Products may therefore not be advertised in the EU with "lowers blood pressure" or "contributes to normal blood pressure."
- **Japan:** There, such beverages were marketed as "Foods for Specified Health Uses" (FOSHU) with an officially approved blood pressure claim. This partly explains why a large proportion of the studies come from Japan.
- **USA:** sold as dietary supplements; disease-related claims are not permitted.
- **Sport:** not on the WADA Prohibited List (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established:** On ClinicalTrials.gov, no ongoing study is registered under the term "lactotripeptides." Under related terms, there is a completed study on IPP and vascular function in coronary heart disease with 54 participants (NCT00924157). Research on lactotripeptides has declined markedly since about 2015; new work mainly examines in general why people respond differently to food peptides.

**Assessment:** Lactotripeptides are a textbook example of how a mechanism that is convincing in the laboratory and early positive studies can shrink in larger, independent investigations. A small effect on blood pressure is possible – especially in Asian study populations – but has not been convincingly demonstrated for Europe. A renaissance is not in sight.

### Conclusion

Lactotripeptides are two small milk peptides that inhibit the blood pressure-regulating enzyme ACE in the test tube. In early Japanese and Finnish studies they lowered blood pressure, but in several larger Dutch studies they did not, and measurable ACE inhibition could not be demonstrated in humans. Meta-analyses show a reduction in systolic blood pressure of about 1 to 4 mmHg, with indications of publication bias and a considerably smaller effect in Europe. EFSA has rejected a blood pressure claim. They are harmless, but no substitute for blood pressure treatment.
