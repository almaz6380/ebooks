## GHRP-6

GHRP-6 is the ancestor of all growth hormone–releasing peptides. In 1984, the US endocrinologist Cyril Bowers described an artificial hexapeptide that selectively releases growth hormone – 15 years before its natural counterpart, ghrelin, was known. In research, GHRP-6 became the standard tool for testing the growth hormone axis. In bodybuilding circles, by contrast, it is regarded as an inexpensive GH booster with the side effect of "ravenous hunger." It never became a medicine.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** synthetic hexapeptide His-D-Trp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH2 with two non-natural D-amino acids (Bowers 1984, PMID 6714155).
- **Origin:** Tulane University (USA), early 1980s.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a) in the pituitary gland and hypothalamus; releases growth hormone as well as ACTH, cortisol and prolactin; increases appetite.
- **EU approval:** no. **US approval:** no. Not approved as a medicine anywhere else either (as of 2026).
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 – GHRP-6 is explicitly named as a growth hormone–releasing peptide.
- **Evidence level: C** – many small studies on hormone physiology and diagnostics, each with 10 to 250 people, but not a single controlled trial of a therapeutic benefit.

### How it is supposed to work

In 1984, Bowers and colleagues showed that the hexapeptide releases growth hormone within minutes in rats, monkeys, lambs and calves without affecting other pituitary hormones such as LH, FSH or TSH; rats that received it repeatedly gained more weight (Bowers 1984, PMID 6714155). It later turned out that GHRP-6 acts at the same receptor as the stomach hormone ghrelin (Kojima 1999, PMID 10604470).

Like GHRP-2, GHRP-6 acts most strongly when the body's own GHRH from the hypothalamus is involved. When both are combined, the release of growth hormone is greater than the sum of the individual effects. It was precisely this property that made GHRP-6 interesting as a diagnostic agent. Unlike ipamorelin, GHRP-6 also activates the stress hormone axis (ACTH and cortisol).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **40** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **no** entry on ClinicalTrials.gov. Almost all papers use GHRP-6 as a single-dose test substance to study hormone regulation in diseases such as Cushing's syndrome, hyperthyroidism, overweight or in old age. One study on this list was conducted in dogs.

#### Diagnosis of growth hormone deficiency

The best-known study was published in 2000 in the journal Lancet: 125 adults with diseases of the pituitary gland and 125 healthy people received GHRH plus GHRP-6 as a combination test. The mean GH peak was 59.2 µg/L in the healthy people and 4.1 µg/L in those affected; a cutoff of 15 µg/L reliably separated the two groups. No side effects occurred, and age, sex and body fat did not influence the result (Popovic 2000, PMID 11030292). In a smaller study of 20 people, the test correctly identified all cases of growth hormone deficiency; the only side effect observed was a feeling of warmth (flushing) (Petersenn 2002, PMID 11980622). Of 21 adults in whom a deficiency had previously been diagnosed with conventional tests, about 40% did respond clearly to the combination after all (Leal-Cerro 1995, PMID 7788011) – an indication that different tests answer different questions.

As a test for underactivity of the adrenal axis, however, GHRP-6 was of only limited use: in 49 patients, compared with the insulin test, it achieved a sensitivity of 71.0% and a specificity of 77.8% (Alaioubi 2010, PMID 19946832).

#### Pharmacology, sleep, age

- **Pharmacokinetics:** In nine healthy men, the elimination half-life after intravenous administration averaged 2.5 hours (Cabrales 2013, PMID 23099431).
- **Sleep:** In healthy men, GHRP-6 increased growth hormone, ACTH and cortisol at night and prolonged light sleep (stage 2), but not deep sleep (Frieboes 1995, PMID 7617137).
- **Age:** In nine young and nine older adults, the pituitary gland in older age responded more weakly to GHRH, but not to GHRP-6 (Micic 1995, PMID 7734029).
- **Children:** As a tablet, GHRP-6 triggered a GH rise in children of short stature without hormone deficiency similar to that after GHRH injection (Bellone 1995, PMID 7581965).

### Animal and laboratory studies

A large part of more recent research concerns the protection of organs:

- **Heart (rats):** In rats receiving the cardiotoxic cancer drug doxorubicin, simultaneous administration of GHRP-6 prevented dilation of the heart chambers and preserved pumping function (Berlanga-Acosta 2024, PMID 38873418).
- **Multiple organ failure (rats, cell cultures):** After temporary interruption of blood flow to the liver, GHRP-6 reduced liver and intestinal damage; in cell cultures it accelerated the migration of intestinal cells (Cibrián 2006, PMID 16417467).
- **Nervous system (rats, mice):** In an animal model of multiple sclerosis, only the combination of GHRP-6 with the growth factor EGF improved the course of the disease, not GHRP-6 alone (del Barco 2011, PMID 21697595).

None of these approaches has so far led to a published controlled trial in humans.

### Risks and side effects

**Established from the short-term studies:** flushing (Petersenn 2002, PMID 11980622); rise in ACTH, cortisol and prolactin (Frieboes 1995, PMID 7617137); hunger as a known ghrelin effect. Otherwise, no relevant side effects occurred in the single-dose diagnostic studies.

**Unknown:** There are no human studies on repeated use over weeks or months. With continuous use, the known consequences of elevated growth hormone levels and repeated cortisol stimulation are conceivable, such as impaired blood glucose regulation. A case report describes a bodybuilder who used growth hormone and releasers – including a combination of sermorelin and GHRP-6 – for decades and developed a large bone spur on the cervical spine; a causal relationship has not been proven (Perez 2026, PMID 42465868). On the cancer question, see the introduction to this group and Part IV.

**Gray market:** "Gly-GHRP-6," an undeclared modification with an additional amino acid, has been detected in seized products (Krug 2018, PMID 29864719).

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria, USA:** no approval as a medicine; supplying it for use in humans is prohibited. In research, GHRP-6 is used within approved studies.
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2). GHRP-6 is largely excreted unchanged in urine and, after administration as a nasal spray to a volunteer, was detectable for 23 hours (Semenistaya 2015, PMID 25869809).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

As of October 2026, no study with GHRP-6 is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. *Author's assessment:* The animal work on organ protection is the only recognizable line of development; whether it will lead to human studies is open. For diagnostics, GHRP-6 has largely been replaced by other tests.

### Conclusion

GHRP-6 was the first artificial peptide to selectively release growth hormone and contributed decisively to the discovery of the ghrelin system. In humans, it has been studied almost exclusively as a single-dose test substance, including in a large Lancet study on the diagnosis of growth hormone deficiency. There is no proven therapeutic benefit, and data on repeated use, as promoted on the gray market, are completely lacking.
