## Patents, Generics, and Prices

Whether a drug works is decided by studies. Whether it reaches people is decided by patents, prices, supply chains, and health insurers. With the GLP-1 drugs, this second part of the story is almost as dramatic as the first: supply shortages, a market for pharmacy copies in the US, counterfeits worldwide – and in 2026 the expiry of the first semaglutide patents in large countries.

**An important note up front:** prices, patent terms, and reimbursement rules change quickly and are only partly documented in scientific publications. Where this chapter gives information without a study reference, it is marked "as of 2026, please check." Specific prices in euros are deliberately not given, because they differ depending on country, package, and point in time.

### Patents, generics, and biosimilars: the terms

A **patent** gives the manufacturer the exclusive right to sell an active ingredient for a limited period. In the EU and the US, the basic term is 20 years from filing; because development takes many years, it can be extended for drugs by up to five years through a supplementary protection certificate. In addition to the patent on the active ingredient itself, there are often further patents on manufacturing processes, dosage forms, or pens (as of 2026, please check).

After protection expires, other manufacturers may offer copies:

- **Generics** are copies of chemically manufactured active ingredients. They must show that they contain the same active ingredient and are equally available in the body; new large efficacy studies are not required.
- **Biosimilars** are follow-on versions of biologically manufactured active ingredients, such as antibodies or fusion proteins. Because biological manufacturing never yields completely identical molecules, their approval is more complex.

Short peptides such as liraglutide or semaglutide are in between: they can be manufactured by genetic engineering or chemically. This has practical consequences. A laboratory analysis compared 16 injectable and 8 oral semaglutide copies as well as 2 liraglutide copies with the originals. It found new impurities, considerably less active ingredient than stated in several semaglutide tablets, and a greater tendency to aggregate with liraglutide (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). Whether the differences mean anything clinically is unknown, according to the authors. But it shows why authorities look closely at peptide copies.

### Liraglutide: the first GLP-1 generic

Liraglutide is the first GLP-1 agent whose protection has expired in large markets. In the US, generics of the diabetes form (Victoza) have been on the market since 2024, and of the obesity form (Saxenda) since 2025. In Europe, copies are gradually being added; the situation differs from country to country (see the chapter Liraglutide; as of 2026, please check).

For access to care, this has two sides. On the one hand, an agent with proven cardiovascular benefit in type 2 diabetes is becoming cheaper. On the other hand, liraglutide has to be injected daily and is considerably less effective for weight than semaglutide: in STEP 8, weight fell by an average of 15.8% with weekly semaglutide and by 6.4% with daily liraglutide; 38.5% versus 6.0% lost at least a fifth of their weight (Rubino 2022, PMID 35015037). For people who cannot afford the newer drug, an inexpensive older drug may nevertheless be a better choice than none at all.

### Semaglutide: 2026 as a turning point

For semaglutide, the patents expire in 2026 in several large countries outside the EU and the US, including, according to the chapter Semaglutide, Canada, China, India, and Brazil. An analysis of patent databases arrived at the following picture (Levi 2026, PMID 42437874):

- In twelve countries, patent protection ends in 2026. They account for 47% of people with obesity and 49% of people with type 2 diabetes worldwide.
- In 150 further countries, the authors found no patent filing at all.
- By the end of 2026, injectable semaglutide copies could thus be available in 162 countries, home to 69% of people with type 2 diabetes and 84% of people with clinical obesity.
- From data on active ingredient shipments from India, they estimated production costs: one year of injectable semaglutide could be offered as a generic for 28 to 140 US dollars per person, the tablet for 186 to 380 US dollars. The pen accounted for a disproportionately large share of the costs.

The authors themselves emphasize that pen costs, further patents, and weak health systems may nevertheless limit access. How strongly generics can push down prices is shown by an analysis from India: there, prices for SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists fell by about 60 to 90% after patent expiry. Between Indian generic prices and US list prices, the authors found differences of 15- to 65-fold. They also point out, however, that generics from different manufacturers can differ in quality and absorption in the body (Mohan 2026, PMID 42747945).

**In the EU and the US**, semaglutide generics are not expected until around 2031/2032 (as of 2026, please check). For readers in Austria and Germany, this means: a semaglutide generic legally sold in Canada or India is not an approved drug here. The rules on importing for personal use are narrow (see the chapter "Legal Framework" in Part I), and online offers that refer to such generics are a typical gateway for counterfeits (see the chapter "The Gray Market").

**A political signal:** in September 2025, the World Health Organization added semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, and tirzepatide to its List of Essential Medicines. One commentary sees this as a step intended to facilitate price negotiations and the development of generics in low- and middle-income countries; at the same time, it names lack of funds, weak infrastructure, and the risk of uncritical use as hurdles (Mani 2025, PMID 41358329). In its first guideline on GLP-1 therapies for obesity, the WHO issues a conditional recommendation for long-term treatment together with intensive behavioral therapy. As reasons for the qualification, it cites, among other things, limited long-term data, costs, and the question of equitable access (Celletti 2026, PMID 41324410).

### Tirzepatide and the successors

For tirzepatide and all newer agents – retatrutide, CagriSema, amycretin, maridebart cafraglutide, orforglipron – patent expiry is not to be expected this decade. For tirzepatide, according to industry information, generics are expected in the second half of the 2030s at the earliest (as of 2026, please check). This results in a foreseeable pattern: the older agents will become cheaper, the most potent will remain expensive.

### The end of compounding in the US

During the official supply shortages, US pharmacies were allowed to prepare semaglutide and tirzepatide themselves ("compounding," see Part I). This gave rise to a large market. A study in the US state of Colorado found, in spring 2024, 93 companies with 188 locations advertising such preparations – mostly as medical spas or weight-loss services. Forty-one of these websites referred to FDA approval in their product descriptions, although the preparations had none. One provider advertised a mixture with BPC-157, a substance that the FDA has classified as unsuitable for compounding (DiStefano 2025, PMID 39776466).

After the FDA declared the shortages over – for tirzepatide at the end of 2024, for semaglutide at the beginning of 2025 – the transition periods for copies of the approved products expired over the course of 2025 (as of 2026, please check). Since then, only preparations that differ from the approved products have been permitted. One analysis found 33 such products, including mixtures with vitamin B12, glycine, or a laxative, as well as drops and orally disintegrating tablets whose benefit and safety are largely unknown (Belcourt 2026, PMID 41689811). A laboratory found in tirzepatide–B12 mixtures from various providers a previously unknown impurity that arises from a chemical reaction between tirzepatide and certain forms of B12; what consequences it has for health is unknown (Jordan 2026, PMID 42010938).

Compounding has thus not disappeared but has shifted into a niche in which "personalized" additives provide the legal basis. In Europe, there is no comparable system.

### Supply shortages

From 2022 onward, demand for semaglutide and later tirzepatide exceeded production. People with type 2 diabetes were at times unable to obtain their medication. In Germany and Austria, authorities and professional societies called for Ozempic to be reserved for people with type 2 diabetes and not to be prescribed purely for weight loss (as of 2026, please check).

How large was the proportion of off-label prescriptions? A nationwide registry study from Denmark provides an answer. The number of new Ozempic users rose sharply from 2022, to a peak of 10 per 1,000 adults per year at the beginning of 2023. In 2022, a third of new users had no documented type 2 diabetes; in 2023 this proportion fell again. The authors conclude that prescriptions for weight loss explain only a smaller part of the shortage (Mailhac 2024, PMID 38685990).

Shortages have direct consequences for treatment. In a US study of 288 people who stopped semaglutide or tirzepatide for weight loss within the first year, 11.8% cited supply shortages as the main reason. Most common, however, were cost or insurance problems (47.6%), followed by side effects (14.6%); 2.4% switched to a compounded product (Gasoyan 2025, PMID 41039650).

The manufacturers have since greatly expanded their production capacity, and the official shortages are considered over in the US. In Europe, the situation varies by product and country (as of 2026, please check).

### Prices: What is a weight-loss drug worth?

In the US, list prices for GLP-1 drugs are considerably higher than in Europe. Health economic studies from the US therefore ask whether the benefit justifies the price:

- A modeling study for US adults found that tirzepatide and semaglutide would prevent many cases of diabetes and cardiovascular disease over a lifetime, but were not cost-effective at the net prices at the time. To reach a common threshold, prices would have had to fall by a further 30.5% (tirzepatide) and 81.9% (semaglutide), respectively (Hwang 2025, PMID 40085108).
- A meta-analysis of nine health economic studies from high-income countries concluded that GLP-1 receptor agonists for the treatment of obesity without diabetes are generally not cost-effective from the payer's perspective; this may be different in subgroups and over longer time horizons (Dhippayom 2026, PMID 41365841).
- For people without diabetes who had already had a heart attack or stroke, a modeling study came out more favorably: at the price that self-paying patients in the US paid at the time of the analysis, semaglutide would be cost-effective in this group (Hennessy 2026, PMID 41637062).

Such calculations depend heavily on price assumptions, and prices are moving. In the US, the manufacturers have introduced considerably cheaper direct offers for self-paying patients since 2025 and have reached price agreements with the government; semaglutide is also the subject of government price negotiations for the Medicare program for older adults (as of 2026, please check). How strongly coverage influences use is shown by a US analysis: after Wegovy was approved in March 2024 for reducing cardiovascular risk and covered by Medicare for this use, the number of Wegovy packages dispensed through Medicare rose by a relative 598.1% (Scannell 2026, PMID 42640207).

**Author's assessment:** prices for GLP-1-based drugs will fall in the coming years – through generics of older agents, through competition between manufacturers, through tablets made from small molecules, and through political pressure. This will be felt most strongly in countries with early patent expiry. In the EU, the big price drop is likely to come only with semaglutide generics at the beginning of the 2030s.

### Reimbursement in Germany and Austria

**Germany:** For type 2 diabetes, statutory health insurance covers the costs of approved GLP-1 drugs such as Ozempic, Rybelsus, Trulicity, or Mounjaro within the scope of their approval. Drugs that serve predominantly for weight reduction, by contrast, are excluded from reimbursement under § 34 SGB V; people with statutory insurance therefore usually have to pay for Wegovy, Saxenda, and Mounjaro for weight management themselves. Whether this rule will be changed for people with high cardiovascular risk is being discussed politically and within the self-governing bodies of the health system. Private health insurers decide according to their own policy terms (as of 2026, please check).

**Austria:** For type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 drugs are reimbursable, but coverage is in some cases tied to conditions or to approval by the health insurance fund. For weight management alone, costs are as a rule not covered, or only in exceptional cases (as of 2026, please check).

In both countries: whether a treatment is reimbursed depends on the indication, the clinical findings, and the current legal situation. Your treating physician and your health insurer are the right people to ask. A prescription for weight loss with a product approved only for diabetes is off-label use and is not reimbursed.

### What this means for patients

- **Cheaper does not mean gray market.** With patent expiry in other countries, offers referring to "generics" will increase. For Austria and Germany, only products approved here and obtained from pharmacies are tested drugs.
- **Stopping for cost reasons is common – and has consequences.** In the STEP 1 extension, participants regained on average two-thirds of their weight loss one year after stopping semaglutide, and the improvements in blood pressure and blood values largely reversed (Wilding 2022, PMID 35441470). Anyone considering stopping for cost reasons should discuss this with a physician.
- **Older agents are not worthless.** Liraglutide or dulaglutide are well studied and have a proven cardiovascular benefit in type 2 diabetes. Which agent is suitable in an individual case is a medical decision.

### Key points in brief

- In 2026, patent protection for semaglutide ends in countries where almost half of the people with obesity live. Generics there could cost a fraction of today's prices; in the EU and the US they are not expected until around 2031/2032 (please check).
- Generics already exist for liraglutide; tirzepatide and all newer agents are expected to remain patent-protected until well into the 2030s.
- US compounding of copies of approved products has largely ended since the shortages ended; what remains are modified preparations with untested additives.
- In studies, costs are the most common reason for stopping treatment. In Germany, weight-loss drugs are excluded from reimbursement; in Austria, reimbursement is only limited (as of 2026, please check).
