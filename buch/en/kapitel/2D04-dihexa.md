## Dihexa

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** chemically modified mini-peptide derived from angiotensin IV, a fragment of the blood pressure–regulating hormone system. The core consists of two amino acids (tyrosine and isoleucine), with fatty acid–like chains attached at both ends; chemical name N-hexanoyl-Tyr-Ile-(6)-aminohexanoic amide, research code PNB-0408.
- **Origin:** developed around 2012 at Washington State University (USA).
- **Mechanism of action:** said to amplify the growth factor system HGF/c-Met (HGF = hepatocyte growth factor, c-Met = its receptor) and thereby stimulate the formation of new nerve connections (synapses).
- **EU approval:** no.
- **US approval:** no. From 2023, Dihexa was in FDA "Category 2" for compounding substances with safety concerns; in April 2026 it was removed from that category, and an evaluation by the advisory committee PCAC has been announced for a meeting by February 2027 (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **WADA:** not listed by name; as a substance not approved anywhere, Dihexa falls in our assessment under the catch-all category S0 "non-approved substances" (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **D** – not a single published study in humans.

### How it is supposed to work

Angiotensin IV is a short peptide formed in the body from the blood pressure hormone angiotensin II. Since the 1990s, researchers have observed that angiotensin IV and related molecules improve learning and memory in rats when injected directly into the brain. For a medicine this was useless: the peptides were rapidly broken down and passed neither from the gut into the blood nor across the blood-brain barrier.

The research group led by Joseph Harding and John Wright in Washington therefore shortened the molecule to its active core and added chemical protecting groups. The result was Dihexa: stable against breakdown, orally active in rats and able to enter the brain. In rats with drug-induced memory impairment and in old rats, it improved performance in the water maze and promoted synapse formation in the hippocampus (McCoy 2013, PMID 23055539).

Later, the same group found the presumed mechanism of action: Dihexa binds to the growth factor HGF and amplifies its signal at the c-Met receptor. When the HGF system in the brains of rats was blocked, the memory-enhancing effect disappeared (Benoist 2014, PMID 25187433). This paper was retracted by the journal in 2025 (retraction notice: PMID 40312093), as was another foundational paper by the same group on angiotensin IV derivatives as HGF/c-Met modulators (retraction notice: PMID 40312092). The described mechanism of action is therefore no longer reliably supported; the PubMed entries do not state the reasons for the retraction.

On the internet, Dihexa is often described as a substance many times more potent than the nerve growth factor BDNF. Such comparisons come from cell culture experiments and say nothing about whether and how Dihexa works in humans.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded **no** human study with Dihexa; in total, PubMed lists only 18 papers for the search term, several of them unrelated to the substance. No study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. A targeted follow-up search for case reports, poisonings or analyses of gray-market products also found nothing.

There are thus no data on efficacy, tolerability, duration of action or appropriate amounts in humans. Everything reported about effects in humans comes from anecdotal reports in forums.

#### A relative in the clinic: fosgonimeton

The fate of another agent that emerged from the same research approach – amplification of the HGF/c-Met system – is revealing. The company Athira Pharma developed fosgonimeton (ATH-1017), a chemically different molecule that is likewise intended to positively influence HGF/c-Met. Unlike Dihexa, fosgonimeton was tested in regular clinical trials:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 (Hua 2022), PMID 35180125 | 88 people (healthy people and 11 people with Alzheimer's), double-blind vs. placebo | up to 9 days | well tolerated; EEG changes as an indication of brain penetration |
| LIFT-AD (Porsteinsson 2025), PMID 41393340 | 287 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease in the primary analysis, phase 2/3 vs. placebo | 26 weeks | primary and secondary endpoints missed; more discontinuations due to side effects (14.2% vs. 4.6%) |

In LIFT-AD (NCT04488419), fosgonimeton did not differ significantly from placebo in either the combined primary endpoint or memory or daily-living scales. Serious adverse events were balanced (4.2% versus 6.9%); treatment discontinuations due to side effects – mainly injection site reactions – were more frequent on fosgonimeton (Porsteinsson 2025, PMID 41393340). A trial in Parkinson's disease dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies was terminated early (NCT04831281).

This does not prove that Dihexa would be ineffective – it is a different substance. But it shows how poorly memory improvements in rats translate to people with dementia.

### Animal and laboratory studies

- In rats with scopolamine-induced memory impairment and in old rats, Dihexa improved spatial learning and promoted synapse formation (McCoy 2013, PMID 23055539).
- In Alzheimer's model mice (APP/PS1), Dihexa improved learning in the water maze and reduced signs of inflammation in the brain (Sun 2021, PMID 34827486).
- In a rat model of Huntington's disease, Dihexa did **not** protect against the deficits (Wells 2024, PMID 38489193).
- In zebrafish larvae, Dihexa protected sensory hair cells from damage by certain antibiotics (Uribe 2015, PMID 25674052).
- In stem cell research, Dihexa is used as an inexpensive substitute for growth factors in the production of liver-like cells (Pan 2022, PMID 35410439) – an indication of how broadly the HGF system acts in the body.

A systematic review of angiotensin IV derivatives found favorable effects in eight of nine animal studies on memory impairment, but emphasizes that the substances worked best when given directly into the brain (Ho 2018, PMID 29733881).

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** There are no published safety data in humans – neither from studies nor from case reports.

**Theoretical – and to be taken seriously:** The HGF/c-Met system is not only important for nerve cells. Activation of c-Met stimulates cell division, cell migration and tissue remodeling (Wright 2015, PMID 25649658). In cancer research, c-Met is known as a driver of the growth and spread of various tumors; several approved cancer drugs block precisely this receptor. A substance that persistently amplifies this signal could theoretically favor the growth of undetected tumors. Whether this is the case with Dihexa has never been studied – there are no long-term toxicity studies in publicly accessible form. (More on growth signals and cancer risk in Part IV.)

**Gray market:** Dihexa is sold online as powder, capsules or solution, some for oral use, some for application to the skin. Content and purity are unverified.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved. As a substance with a pharmacological effect, Dihexa is considered a medicine; selling it for use in humans is not permitted. Labeling it "for research purposes only" does not change this (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **USA:** not approved. No longer in FDA "Category 2" since April 2026; as of October 2026, the advisory committee PCAC had not yet deliberated on possible inclusion in the list of permitted compounding substances (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **Sport:** as a non-approved substance, falls in our assessment under WADA category S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established:** No clinical trial is registered for Dihexa (search October 2026). The related approach fosgonimeton failed in the Alzheimer's trial LIFT-AD.

**Assessment:** Without toxicity data and a first study in humans, Dihexa remains a laboratory compound. Whether the announced PCAC deliberation in the USA will change anything is open – at most it would concern pharmacy compounding, not the missing proof of efficacy.

### Conclusion

Dihexa is a chemically stabilized derivative of angiotensin IV that promoted learning and synapse formation in rats and mice, mediated via the growth factor system HGF/c-Met. It has never been studied in humans; a related agent (fosgonimeton) missed all endpoints in an Alzheimer's trial with 287 people. Because c-Met also drives tumor growth, there is a plausible but untested cancer concern. The data are not sufficient to assess any benefit or safety in humans.
