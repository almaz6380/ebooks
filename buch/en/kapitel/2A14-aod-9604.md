## AOD-9604

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** synthetic peptide from the rear end (C-terminus) of human growth hormone: amino acids 177 to 191, supplemented by an additional tyrosine at the beginning (Cox 2015, PMID 25208511). Often offered online under names such as "hGH Fragment 176-191" or "Frag 176-191." The name stands for "Anti-Obesity Drug 9604."
- **Origin:** developed in the 1990s at Monash University in Melbourne and pursued as a weight-loss drug by the Australian company Metabolic Pharmaceuticals.
- **Mechanism of action (hypothesis):** intended to mimic the fat-burning effect of growth hormone without its effects on blood glucose and growth.
- **Approval EU / USA:** no. Development as a medicine was discontinued after Phase 2 trials.
- **WADA:** prohibited – growth hormone fragments such as AOD-9604 are explicitly named on the Prohibited List in class S2 (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **D** – no published results of human studies retrievable in PubMed; data from animal and laboratory studies.

### How it is supposed to work

Growth hormone (hGH) has many effects: it promotes longitudinal growth, acts on muscles and bones, stimulates fat burning – and raises blood glucose by weakening the effect of insulin. As early as the 1970s and 1980s, researchers investigated in animals whether individual segments of the hormone carry different partial effects. Notably, in a study in rats, chemically modified versions of exactly this segment (amino acids 177 to 191) triggered elevated blood glucose and insulin resistance (Wade 1982, PMID 6751009) – precisely the effect that AOD-9604 was later meant to avoid. The results evidently depend strongly on the exact chemical form of the peptide.

The idea behind AOD-9604: a short piece from the end of the hormone should retain only the fat-burning effect. Laboratory studies showed that AOD-9604 does not bind to the growth hormone receptor and does not stimulate cell division there either (Heffernan 2001, PMID 11673763). It would therefore not be a "mini growth hormone" but a molecule with its own, not yet fully understood mechanism of action. In experiments in mice lacking the beta-3 adrenergic receptor (an important receptor for fat breakdown), the long-term weight-lowering effect was absent; the authors concluded from this that the receptor is involved but is not the direct target (Heffernan 2001, PMID 11713213).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded **no** published human study. On ClinicalTrials.gov, no trial is registered under "AOD9604" or "AOD-9604."

This does not mean that AOD-9604 was never tested in humans. Reviews from 2004 to 2006 report that AOD-9604 was in Phase 2 trials for the treatment of obesity (Wilding 2004, PMID 15134286; Halford 2006, PMID 16625817). However, the results of these trials were not published in a peer-reviewed journal indexed in PubMed. According to publicly available information, the developer discontinued the weight-loss program in the second half of the 2000s because no sufficient effect was seen. Since this information does not come from a verifiable study publication, this book gives no figures on it.

For readers, this means: if you are offered AOD-9604 today as a weight-loss or "fat burner" peptide, it cannot be backed by any published human study demonstrating an effect.

### Animal and laboratory studies

All of the following results come from animal experiments or laboratory studies:

- **In obese rats** (Zucker rats), AOD-9604 given in the feed over 19 days reduced weight gain by more than half; adipose tissue showed higher fat-breakdown activity. Unlike with complete growth hormone, insulin sensitivity did not worsen (Ng 2000, PMID 11146367).
- **In obese mice**, both growth hormone and AOD-9604 reduced weight gain over 14 days and increased fat burning. In contrast to growth hormone, AOD-9604 did not cause elevated blood glucose (Heffernan 2001, PMID 11673763).
- **Earlier preliminary work** in mice with the related peptide hGH 177–191 showed a smaller increase in body weight and adipose tissue (Natera 1994, PMID 7987248).
- **In rabbits** with artificially induced knee osteoarthritis, injections of AOD-9604 into the joint – alone or together with hyaluronic acid – improved the condition of the cartilage (Kwon 2015, PMID 26275694). This finding is frequently cited online as evidence of a "joint-regenerating" effect in humans; no such human study exists.

The animal studies are small, partly older and predominantly from the developers' circle.

### Risks and side effects

**Documented:** In the absence of published human studies, there are no robust data on side effects in humans.

**From analyses of gray-market products and doping control:**

- AOD-9604 has been offered via websites for years and has been found in seized vials, among others in the USA (Cox 2015, PMID 25208511) and in Belgium, where authorities examined unknown peptide preparations (Vanhee 2014, PMID 24976118).
- Reviews on peptides in sport and on the gray market emphasize that hardly any robust human safety data exist for AOD-9604 and similar substances and that the composition, dose and purity of the products are uncertain (Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639; Dominikowski 2026, PMID 42395176). Another review points to mislabeled or contaminated products in unregulated trade (Coutinho 2026, PMID 41880199).

**Theoretical:** With any injectable product from uncontrolled manufacturing, there are risks from contaminants, bacteria or incorrect ingredients (see Part I and Part IV). Whether AOD-9604 – like growth hormone – affects metabolism or cell growth in the long term has not been studied in humans. The laboratory data argue against a direct effect at the growth hormone receptor (Heffernan 2001, PMID 11673763).

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved as a medicine. Products offered for use in humans may not be marketed under pharmaceutical law; "for research use only" notices do not change this.
- **USA:** not approved as a medicine. The FDA has placed AOD-9604 on the list of substances that, because of safety concerns, may not readily be compounded into medicines by pharmacies (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Sport:** explicitly prohibited (WADA Prohibited List, class S2 – peptide hormones, growth factors, related substances; growth hormone fragments). AOD-9604 became known to a wider public through the doping scandal surrounding the Australian football club Essendon in 2013. Specific detection methods in urine have been developed for doping control (Cox 2015, PMID 25208511). The usual growth hormone test is not affected by AOD-9604 (Orlovius 2013, PMID 24124033) – the fragment therefore has to be specifically looked for. As of 2026, please check for updates.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed:** No ongoing trial on AOD-9604 is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (search October 2026).

**Assessment:** There is no discernible path by which AOD-9604 could become an approved medicine in the foreseeable future. With the GLP-1-based agents, weight-loss drugs with large, well-documented efficacy exist today; a peptide whose own development as a weight-loss agent was not successful is unlikely to attract new investor interest. AOD-9604 will therefore probably remain a gray-market product.

### Conclusion

AOD-9604 is a short piece of growth hormone that stimulated fat breakdown in rats and mice without raising blood glucose. Although human studies were conducted in the 2000s, their results have not been published in peer-reviewed journals, and development as a weight-loss drug was discontinued. Robust data on efficacy and safety in humans are therefore completely lacking. AOD-9604 is not approved as a medicine anywhere and is explicitly prohibited in sport.
