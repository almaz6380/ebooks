# Part II · A – Metabolism and Weight

No group of peptides has changed medicine in recent years as much as the drugs in this section. They were originally developed for type 2 diabetes, then became the most effective weight-loss medications ever, and are now being studied in heart failure, sleep apnea, fatty liver disease, and kidney disease. At the same time, hardly any area is as heavily affected by the gray market: drugs that are still in clinical trials are sold on the internet as "research peptides."

### What These Drugs Have in Common: Hormones from the Gut and Pancreas

Almost all the drugs in this group mimic hormones that the body releases around a meal. You should know four of them:

- **GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1)** is released by cells in the small and large intestine as soon as food arrives. It enhances insulin release – but only when blood sugar is elevated – slows gastric emptying, and signals satiety to the brain. The body's own GLP-1 is broken down within a few minutes; the medications are modified so that they act for one day to one week.
- **GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide)** comes from the upper small intestine and likewise enhances insulin release. Its role in appetite and body weight is contradictory: tirzepatide activates the GIP receptor, maridebart cafraglutide blocks it – and both lead to weight loss.
- **Glucagon** is the counter-regulatory hormone to insulin and comes from the pancreas. It raises blood sugar but also increases energy expenditure and fat breakdown in the liver. Newer drugs make targeted use of this second side.
- **Amylin** is released together with insulin from the beta cells of the pancreas. It acts in the brainstem to promote satiety and slows gastric emptying.

GLP-1 and GIP are called **incretins** (derived from "INtestinal seCRETion of INsulin," i.e., insulin release triggered by the gut): they explain why sugar that is eaten releases more insulin than the same amount of sugar given directly into a vein. The development can be read as a sequence of stages: first pure GLP-1 agonists, then combinations with GIP, glucagon, or amylin in a single molecule, and finally tablets that are no longer peptides at all.

Two drugs in this group do not fit this pattern: **setmelanotide** acts deeper in the brain, at the melanocortin-4 receptor, and is intended only for rare genetic forms of obesity. **AOD-9604** is a fragment of growth hormone that is marketed as a "fat burner" without any published human studies.

### Reading Guide

The chapters roughly follow the historical development: from the established GLP-1 agonists (exenatide to semaglutide) via the combination drugs (tirzepatide to maridebart cafraglutide) to the special cases (pramlintide, setmelanotide, AOD-9604) and finally to orforglipron, which is included as a non-peptide for comparison.

If you want a quick overview, start with semaglutide and tirzepatide – all others are measured against them. Direct comparisons of weight loss, blood sugar reduction, cardiovascular outcomes, and side effects across all drugs can be found in Part III. Class risks such as gallbladder and pancreatic problems, muscle loss, and questions about the thyroid are covered in detail in Part IV.

Two principles apply to the entire group: first, the chapters describe study results, not treatment instructions – for approved medications, the prescribing information and medical supervision are authoritative. Second, for almost all drugs, a large part of the weight lost returns when treatment ends. These medications work as long as they are taken; they are not a cure.
