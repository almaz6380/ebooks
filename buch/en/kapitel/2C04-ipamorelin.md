## Ipamorelin

In fitness and anti-aging circles, ipamorelin is regarded as the "clean" growth hormone releaser: it is said to release growth hormone selectively without driving up stress hormones and appetite. This property comes from animal experiments in the 1990s. Ipamorelin has in fact been clinically tested in humans – but not for muscle building or rejuvenation, rather for something entirely different: bowel paralysis after abdominal surgery. There it showed no demonstrable benefit.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** synthetic pentapeptide (5 amino acids, some non-natural: Aib-His-D-2-Nal-D-Phe-Lys-NH2) (Raun 1998, PMID 9849822).
- **Origin:** developed by Novo Nordisk (Denmark) from the family of growth hormone–releasing peptides (GHRP); later developed further by Helsinn for the gastrointestinal field.
- **Mechanism of action:** binds to the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a) in the pituitary gland and hypothalamus as well as in the gastrointestinal tract; triggers a short burst of growth hormone and is said to stimulate bowel movement.
- **EU approval:** no. **US approval:** no.
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 (growth hormone secretagogues; ipamorelin is explicitly named).
- **Evidence level: B** – one randomized phase 2 trial with 117 people (without proof of efficacy) and a small pharmacological study in healthy people; a second phase 2 trial has been completed but is unpublished.

### How it is supposed to work

The ghrelin receptor is the second major control lever for growth hormone secretion, alongside the GHRH receptor. Its natural key is ghrelin, a hormone from the stomach discovered in 1999 (Kojima 1999, PMID 10604470). Ghrelin increases not only growth hormone but also hunger, and it stimulates the movements of the stomach and intestines.

Older synthetic releasers such as GHRP-6 or GHRP-2 release not only growth hormone but also the stress hormone system (ACTH and cortisol) and prolactin. Ipamorelin was specifically developed so that this side effect is absent: in pigs, it released growth hormone about as strongly as GHRP-6, whereas ACTH and cortisol did not rise more than under GHRH even at doses more than 200 times higher (Raun 1998, PMID 9849822). Whether this selectivity applies to the same extent in humans has not been systematically studied.

Clinically, interest focused mainly on its effect on the gut: after abdominal surgery, bowel activity often comes to a standstill for days (postoperative ileus), which prolongs the hospital stay. A ghrelin mimetic that can be given intravenously seemed an elegant solution.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **2** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **3** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov. One of these registry entries (NCT07717866) concerns an observational study on psychedelics in veterans and does not study ipamorelin as an investigational drug; it is merely listed there as one of several components of a "physiological supplementation" (alongside testosterone, fish oil and vitamins). Clinically relevant, therefore, are two trials on postoperative ileus.

**Pharmacology in healthy people:** In an ascending-dose study, eight healthy men per dose level each received a 15-minute infusion. Ipamorelin had a short half-life of about 2 hours and triggered a single GH pulse at all doses, which peaked after about 40 minutes and then fell rapidly (Gobburu 1999, PMID 10496658).

**Postoperative ileus, first phase 2 trial:** In a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 117 adults received ipamorelin or placebo as an infusion twice daily for up to seven days after small or large bowel resection. The mean time to the first tolerated solid meal was 25.3 hours on ipamorelin and 32.6 hours on placebo – a difference that was not statistically significant (p = 0.15). The other efficacy endpoints did not differ either. Side effects occurred in 87.5% on ipamorelin and 94.8% on placebo, which is not unusual after major abdominal surgery (Beck 2014, PMID 25331030).

**Second phase 2 trial:** A larger follow-up trial with 320 participants on the recovery of gastrointestinal function was completed in 2014 (NCT01280344). I have not found a publication of the results in PubMed, and the registry entry contains no results. Development was not continued thereafter.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pharmacology (Gobburu 1999), PMID 10496658 | healthy men, 5 dose levels of 8 people each | single infusion | half-life approx. 2 h; one GH pulse per dose |
| Ileus trial 1 (Beck 2014), PMID 25331030 | 117 adults after bowel resection, RCT vs. placebo | up to 7 days | 25.3 vs. 32.6 h to first meal, not significant |
| Ileus trial 2, NCT01280344 | 320 adults, phase 2 vs. placebo | – | completed 2014, results not published |

There is **no** published study that has examined ipamorelin in healthy adults, athletes or older people with regard to muscle mass, fat loss, sleep, skin or performance.

### Animal and laboratory studies

- **Gut (rats):** In rats with surgically induced bowel paralysis, a single dose of ipamorelin shortened the time to the first bowel movement; repeated doses increased stool output, food intake and weight gain (Venkova 2009, PMID 19289567). These results were the basis for the ileus trials in humans – which did not confirm the effect.
- **Bone (rats):** In adult female rats, ipamorelin dose-dependently increased longitudinal bone growth and weight gain over 15 days without measurably raising IGF-1 (Johansen 1999, PMID 10373343).
- **Selectivity (pigs, rat cells):** see above (Raun 1998, PMID 9849822).

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** In the ileus trial, ipamorelin was described as well tolerated over up to seven days, with no excess of side effects compared with placebo (Beck 2014, PMID 25331030). This was short-term use in hospital with intravenous administration.

**Unknown:** There are no data on repeated use over weeks or months, as described on the internet. Theoretically, the effects of elevated GH levels would be expected (fluid retention, joint complaints, reduced insulin sensitivity), as well as increased appetite via the ghrelin receptor. On the question of a cancer risk from persistently elevated IGF-1, see the introduction to this group and Part IV.

**Gray market:** Laboratory analyses of seized products found "Gly-ipamorelin," that is, ipamorelin with an additional, undeclared amino acid at the beginning – presumably to evade doping tests (Krug 2018, PMID 29864719; Gajda 2019, PMID 30136411). How such modifications behave in the body is unknown.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** no approval; supplying it for use in humans is prohibited.
- **USA:** no approval. The FDA has classified ipamorelin among the bulk drug substances for compounding pharmacies as a substance with safety concerns (Category 2); the classification of peptides has since been debated controversially (Willis 2026, PMID 42752426).
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2), in and out of competition. Ipamorelin and its metabolites can be detected in urine, including after use as a nasal spray (Semenistaya 2015, PMID 25869809).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

As of October 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov lists no ongoing study investigating ipamorelin as an investigational drug. *Author's assessment:* After the negative result in postoperative ileus, there is no company developing the substance further. New data are most likely to come from case reports on gray-market use.

### Conclusion

Ipamorelin is a purpose-designed ghrelin receptor agonist that, in animals, releases growth hormone without appreciable stress hormone release. The only published efficacy trial in humans – 117 patients after bowel surgery – found no significant benefit; a larger follow-up trial remained unpublished. For the uses promoted on the internet (muscle building, fat loss, sleep, anti-aging), there are no human studies, and gray-market products have been shown to contain modified molecules as well.
