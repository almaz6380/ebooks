## Hexarelin

Hexarelin is a close relative of GHRP-6 – and one of the most potent artificial growth hormone releasers of all. In the 1990s it was studied intensively in humans, especially in Italy. In the process, something unexpected emerged: hexarelin appears to act directly on the heart, independently of growth hormone. Even so, no approval followed. One reason lay in a typical weakness of this class of substances: with continuous use, the effect wears off.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** synthetic hexapeptide His-D-2-methyl-Trp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH2; it differs from GHRP-6 by an additional methyl group on the second building block (Imbimbo 1994, PMID 7957536).
- **Origin:** developed in the early 1990s as a modification of GHRP-6; a large part of the clinical research comes from Italian university centers.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a); releases growth hormone, ACTH, cortisol and prolactin. In addition, hexarelin binds to the receptor CD36 in heart tissue, through which GH-independent cardiac effects are suspected (Mao 2014, PMID 25278975).
- **EU approval:** no. **US approval:** no.
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 – hexarelin is explicitly named as a growth hormone–releasing peptide.
- **Evidence level: C** – numerous small studies, many of them randomized, but almost exclusively with single doses and hormonal or circulatory measures; no study of a clinical endpoint.

### How it is supposed to work

Like GHRP-6 and GHRP-2, hexarelin mimics the stomach hormone ghrelin and thereby triggers growth hormone secretion. Its effect depends on an intact connection between the hypothalamus and the pituitary gland: in children and adults in whom this connection was anatomically interrupted, the GH response was absent or greatly weakened, whereas in idiopathic deficiency it was strong (Loche 1995, PMID 7673411).

The second, independent idea concerns the heart. Ghrelin receptors and the scavenger molecule CD36 are also present in heart muscle. Hexarelin could therefore directly increase the pumping force of the heart – a possible approach in heart failure, independent of growth hormone.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **58** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **no** entry on ClinicalTrials.gov. Most studies date from 1994 to 2004 and use single doses to examine how the hormone response depends on age, sex, nutrition, medications or diseases. Some entries on the list deal mainly with ghrelin or GHRP-6 and use hexarelin only as a comparator.

#### Hormonal effect and dose

In a double-blind, placebo-controlled study with twelve healthy men, growth hormone rose dose-dependently after intravenous administration, peaking after about 30 minutes and returning to baseline within four hours (Imbimbo 1994, PMID 7957536). Hexarelin also released prolactin and cortisol dose-dependently; combined with GHRH, a low dose of hexarelin was sufficient for a massive GH release without a rise in cortisol (Massoud 1996, PMID 8954038). Activation of the stress hormone axis apparently runs via the hypothalamus; in a study with 15 young men, hexarelin also briefly increased appetite (Korbonits 1999, PMID 10404825).

#### Long-term administration: the effect wears off

What matters for a therapy is whether the effect persists with repeated administration. In adults who injected hexarelin subcutaneously twice daily for 16 weeks, the GH response declined markedly and recovered only four weeks after stopping. IGF-1, body fat, muscle mass and bone density did not change significantly (Rahim 1998, PMID 9589671). The cortisol response also weakened temporarily in the same study (Rahim 1999, PMID 10341859).

In children of short stature, the picture was somewhat more favorable: with a nasal spray, growth velocity in eight children rose from 5.3 to 8.3 cm per year, and IGF-1 increased (Laron 1995, PMID 8548949). A follow-up study with seven children showed that the GH response of the pituitary gland was already roughly halved after seven days, yet the faster growth nevertheless continued (Klinger 1996, PMID 8766941).

#### Cardiac effects

Several small studies by an Italian research group examined the heart:

- In seven men with growth hormone deficiency and nine healthy controls, the left ventricular ejection fraction (the proportion of blood the left ventricle ejects per beat) rose after a single dose of hexarelin in both groups, even though those affected released hardly any growth hormone. The authors conclude that there is a GH-independent effect (Bisi 1999, PMID 10528131).
- In patients with severe dilated heart muscle disease (dilated cardiomyopathy), however, this effect was absent (Broglio 2001, PMID 11322491).
- In 24 men with coronary artery disease during bypass surgery, hexarelin increased ejection fraction and cardiac output for up to 90 minutes, whereas GHRH, growth hormone and placebo had no effect (Broglio 2002, PMID 12144941).

### Animal and laboratory studies

- **Heart attack (rats, mice):** In rats four weeks after an experimentally induced heart attack, hexarelin improved stroke volume and lowered vascular resistance over two weeks, similarly to growth hormone (Tivesten 2000, PMID 10614623). In mice after a heart attack, it preserved pumping function and reduced scarring of the heart muscle (McDonald 2018, PMID 29756411).
- **Mechanism:** A review describes CD36 as a specific cardiac receptor for hexarelin that is thought to mediate the protective effects (Mao 2014, PMID 25278975).

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** rise in cortisol, ACTH and prolactin (Massoud 1996, PMID 8954038; Korbonits 1999, PMID 10404825); briefly increased appetite (Korbonits 1999, PMID 10404825); waning effect with continuous administration (Rahim 1998, PMID 9589671; Klinger 1996, PMID 8766941). No serious side effects were reported in the small studies; in children, the authors found no adverse effects (Laron 1995, PMID 8548949).

**Unknown:** Long-term safety data, especially regarding the heart, are lacking. The fact that a substance acutely increases pumping force says nothing about whether it helps or harms in the long term in heart failure – that could only be clarified in dedicated long-term studies with clinical endpoints, which do not exist. On the question of a cancer risk with persistently elevated growth hormone, see the introduction to this group and Part IV.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria, USA:** no approval as a medicine; supplying it for use in humans is prohibited.
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2). Hexarelin is extensively metabolized in the body; metabolites remain detectable in urine after the parent substance has disappeared (Semenistaya 2015, PMID 25869809).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

As of October 2026, no study with hexarelin is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. *Author's assessment:* Cardiac research continues, but only in animal models. Clinical development would require a company that holds the rights and funds trials – no such company is currently apparent.

### Conclusion

In humans, hexarelin strongly releases growth hormone, but also stress hormones and prolactin, and with continuous administration the effect wears off markedly; IGF-1 and body composition did not change in a 16-week study. Of interest are small studies in which a single dose increased the pumping force of the heart, apparently independently of growth hormone. Whether this will ever lead to a benefit for people with heart disease is completely open; for muscle building or rejuvenation, there is no evidence.
