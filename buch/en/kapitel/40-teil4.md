# Part IV – Risks

In Part II, each peptide has its own section "Risks and side effects." This part does not repeat those sections. It places them side by side and asks across all agents: Which organs are affected? Which warning signs appear with several peptides? How robust is what we know? If you are looking for details on a specific substance, each point refers you to the relevant chapter.

Before we begin, it is worth taking a brief look at how risks can be assessed in the first place. Three distinctions run through all of the following chapters.

**Proven, observed, or theoretical.** A risk is best established when it occurred more frequently with the agent than with placebo in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Observational studies using insurance or registry data, by contrast, capture very large numbers of people and rare events, but cannot reliably separate cause from coincidental circumstance. Adverse event reporting databases show only signals: what is well known and much discussed gets reported more often. Finally, theoretical risks are derived from the mechanism of action, from animal experiments, or from cell cultures. They are not proof of harm – but for substances that have never been systematically studied in humans, they are often the only information available.

**Missing data are not safety.** If no treatment-related side effects were noticed in three small studies with a total of 30 people (as with BPC-157, Poolton 2026, PMID 42885452), this only means that no very common, immediately occurring problems arose. A side effect that affects one in a thousand people cannot be detected by such a small study at all. For many peptides in Groups B, C, and D, this is the starting point.

**Relative and absolute risk.** Headlines usually give relative figures: "three times as common." What matters for your own weighing of risks, however, is how many people are actually affected. An example from this part: in a Danish-Norwegian registry study, a certain circulatory disorder of the optic nerve was almost three times as common with semaglutide as with a comparator drug (hazard ratio 2.81). In absolute terms, this meant around 1.4 additional cases per 10,000 person-years (Simonsen 2025, PMID 40098249). Both are correct – and both belong to the information.

Finally, the balance of benefit and risk counts. A drug for type 2 diabetes that reduces heart attacks and strokes in large trials may have rare side effects that would be unacceptable for a tanning agent or a "longevity peptide" with no proven benefit. Someone who uses an approved drug with medical supervision and a clear indication is in a completely different situation from someone who injects an untested powder from the internet. How to read studies is explained in Part I in the chapter "Reading Studies Correctly."

Part IV is structured as follows: this chapter organizes side effects by organ system. It is followed by chapters on cancer risk and growth signals, on immune reactions, on laboratory analyses of gray-market products, and on interactions and particularly vulnerable groups of people.

The following overview summarizes which organ systems are most frequently affected by which peptides. ### Gastrointestinal tract

No other organ system is affected as frequently. Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation are the typical side effects of all drugs that act on the GLP-1 receptor – from the older exenatide through semaglutide and tirzepatide to the new multi-agonists. A review that summarized 60 meta-analyses with a combined 1,751 randomized trials found the most reliable signals precisely here: the odds of nausea with GLP-1 receptor agonists were about two and a half times as high as in the comparison groups (odds ratio 2.47), almost three times as high for vomiting (2.78), and about twice as high for diarrhea (1.94) (Yang 2026, PMID 41915388).

How frequent the complaints are depends strongly on the agent, the dose, and the pace of dose escalation. A network meta-analysis of 39 trials with 33,354 people with overweight without diabetes found an increased risk of nausea for all agents studied, most pronounced for orforglipron, followed by exenatide, tirzepatide, semaglutide, and liraglutide (Ismaiel 2025, PMID 40804463). Rates are particularly high for agents with an additional glucagon component: in the fatty liver study of survodutide, 66% had nausea compared with 23% on placebo (Sanyal 2024, PMID 38847460). Details are in the chapters Survodutide, Mazdutide, and Retatrutide.

Nausea is also common outside the GLP-1 group. With bremelanotide, it occurred in 40.0% versus 1.3% on placebo in the phase 3 trials and was the most common reason for discontinuation (Clayton 2022, PMID 35147466). With pramlintide, 63% reported nausea, 36% on placebo (Edelman 2006, PMID 17003291). With setmelanotide, the figure was 36% in a meta-analysis of seven studies (Sridharan 2026, PMID 39924461).

More important than the frequency of nausea is the question of **serious** gastrointestinal events. A 2023 database analysis had reported an increased risk of pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and gastroparesis (stomach paralysis) for GLP-1 receptor agonists used for weight loss (Sodhi 2023, PMID 37796527). A meta-analysis of 55 placebo-controlled trials with 106,395 participants did not confirm this for most of these events. Gallstones were increased (relative risk 1.46; two additional cases per 1,000 treated), and probably reflux disease (relative risk 2.19; four additional cases per 1,000). For bowel obstruction, gastroparesis, ulcers, or bleeding, no clear difference was found (Chiang 2025, PMID 40499738). In a cohort study using routine data, tirzepatide and semaglutide hardly differed with regard to serious gastrointestinal events (Crisafulli 2026, PMID 41183330).

Of practical importance is that persistent vomiting and diarrhea can lead to fluid loss. In the studies, the complaints occur mainly during the dose escalation phase; this is why the prescribing information for the approved drugs provides for a stepwise approach that should be supervised by a physician. With gray-market products whose contents are unknown, such a schedule cannot be reliably followed (see the chapter "Gray-Market Products").

### Gallbladder and pancreas

For the gallbladder, the risk is now well established. A meta-analysis of 76 randomized trials with 103,371 participants found a 37% increased relative risk of gallbladder and biliary disease with GLP-1 receptor agonists. In weight-loss trials, the relative risk of 2.29 was considerably higher than in diabetes trials (He 2022, PMID 35344001). Part of the effect is likely due to the rapid weight loss itself, which is known to promote gallstones. In STEP TEENS, 5 of 133 adolescents on semaglutide developed gallstones, none on placebo (Weghuber 2022, PMID 36322838).

For the pancreas, the situation is less clear-cut. A meta-analysis of 62 trials with 66,232 participants found an overall slightly increased risk of pancreatitis (relative risk 1.44). When the studies were separated according to whether participants were also taking other medications, the difference was no longer statistically significant in either group (Wen 2025, PMID 40988099). The absolute frequency is low; in the large outcome trials, pancreatitis occurred rarely. The prescribing information lists it as a rare side effect. Severe, persistent upper abdominal pain, often radiating to the back, should always be evaluated by a physician with these drugs. For the question of pancreatic cancer, see the following chapter.

### Thyroid

The concern about the thyroid comes from animal experiments: in rats and mice, GLP-1 receptor agonists led to growths and tumors of the C cells, which produce the hormone calcitonin. In humans, the data are inconsistent. A direct indication of C-cell stimulation would be a rise in calcitonin in the blood. In the LEADER trial with 9,340 participants, calcitonin levels after 36 months did not differ between liraglutide and placebo, and no medullary thyroid carcinoma occurred with liraglutide (Hegedüs 2018, PMID 29279300). How the cancer question should be assessed overall is covered in the chapter "Cancer Risk and Growth Signals." Independently of this, the prescribing information lists a contraindication for a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma and for MEN 2 syndrome.

### Eyes: retina and optic nerve

There are two different questions here.

**Diabetic retinopathy.** In SUSTAIN 6, a cardiovascular trial with 3,297 people with type 2 diabetes, complications of diabetic retinal disease occurred more frequently with semaglutide than with placebo (hazard ratio 1.76) (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186). It is suspected that a very rapid lowering of blood sugar temporarily worsens pre-existing retinopathy – a phenomenon also known from insulin. A meta-analysis of 78 trials with 73,640 participants found no overall increased risk of diabetic retinopathy (odds ratio 1.04) or of eye disease in general (Natividade 2025, PMID 40810985). For people with known retinopathy, ophthalmological follow-up nevertheless remains advisable.

**NAION.** Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy is a circulatory disorder at the head of the optic nerve that usually leads painlessly to sudden vision loss in one eye. Since a first study from a neuro-ophthalmology clinic (Hathaway 2024, PMID 38958939), many further studies have been published, with varying results:

- The Danish-Norwegian registry study found an increased relative risk (hazard ratio 2.81), in absolute terms around 1.4 additional cases per 10,000 person-years (Simonsen 2025, PMID 40098249).
- In a US cohort of 159,398 people with type 2 diabetes, 0.04% on semaglutide or tirzepatide and 0.02% in the comparison group developed the condition over two years (hazard ratio 1.76) (Wang 2025, PMID 40788646).
- An international database study with almost 300,000 people found no established association (Chou 2025, PMID 39491755).
- A meta-analysis saw the signal only in observational studies in diabetes, not in randomized trials and not in obesity (Liu 2026, PMID 41475544).

In summary: a small increased risk is possible, the absolute frequency very low. In 2025, the European Medicines Agency added NAION to the prescribing information as a very rare side effect of semaglutide (as of 2026, please check for current status). Sudden vision loss is an emergency in any case. Details in the chapter Semaglutide.

### Cardiovascular system and fluid retention

For the GLP-1 drugs, according to current knowledge, the benefit for the heart and blood vessels predominates: in SUSTAIN 6, major cardiovascular events occurred less frequently with semaglutide (6.6% versus 8.9%) (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186). A slight rise in resting heart rate, however, is a class property and is in some cases more pronounced with the new multi-agonists. With retatrutide in TRIUMPH-2, low blood pressure also occurred in 1 to 6%, compared with less than 1% on placebo (Bellido 2026, PMID 42810372). See the chapters Retatrutide and Survodutide.

The **growth hormone axis** shows an entirely different pattern. Growth hormone causes the body to retain water. In a review of studies in healthy older people, swelling of tissues, joint pain, carpal tunnel syndrome, and breast enlargement in men occurred more frequently with growth hormone (Liu 2007, PMID 17227934). A similar pattern is to be expected for the secretagogues, which stimulate the body's own release. It was most evident with oral ibutamoren: a study in older people after hip fracture was stopped early because of a signal for heart failure (Adunsky 2011, PMID 21067829). A heart that is already weakened copes poorly with additional fluid.

Further individual findings:

- **Bremelanotide** temporarily raised systolic blood pressure after administration by 2.4 to 3.2 mmHg; the peaks usually lasted less than 15 minutes, while heart rate fell at the same time (White 2017, PMID 27977473).
- **Macimorelin**, in a deliberately excessive single dose, prolonged the QT interval on the ECG by an average of up to 9.61 milliseconds (Lissy 2021, PMID 32961034) – relevant for people taking other QT-prolonging medications.
- **Oxytocin** is chemically similar to the fluid-balance hormone vasopressin. One case of increased intracranial pressure with oxytocin nasal spray combined with very high fluid intake has been described (Mayer-Hubner 1996, PMID 8596357).
- **Melanotan II:** case reports of muscle breakdown with kidney damage (Nelson 2012, PMID 23121206) and a kidney infarction; see the chapter Melanotan II.

### Mental health and suicidality

In 2023, individual reports of suicidal thoughts with semaglutide and liraglutide triggered regulatory reviews in Europe and the US. The data are now considerably better and predominantly reassuring. A meta-analysis of 86 randomized trials with 133,378 participants found no increased risk with GLP-1 receptor agonists for mental illness overall (relative risk 0.96), for depression (0.88), suicide (0.90), or anxiety disorders (0.92) (Han 2026, PMID 41852258). In a Scandinavian registry study of 124,517 people on GLP-1 receptor agonists, the risk of suicide was not increased compared with another diabetes drug; the upper limit of the confidence interval corresponded to at most 0.16 additional deaths per 1,000 person-years (Ueda 2024, PMID 39226030). In the SURMOUNT trials, 0.6% each on tirzepatide and placebo reported suicidal thoughts (Wadden 2026, PMID 41537305). In adolescents, too, a meta-analysis found no increase in suicidal behavior or depression (Silva 2026, PMID 42115753).

One limitation remains: people with severe mental illness were excluded from many studies. For them and for people with eating disorders, separate considerations apply (see the chapter "Interactions and Special Risk Groups").

Other peptides act more directly on the brain. The prescribing information for setmelanotide contains warnings about depression and suicidal thoughts (chapter Setmelanotide). For Semax, a seizure-like EEG change has been described in individual people with pre-existing brain damage (chapter Semax). For most of the "nootropic peptides" in Group D, there are simply no systematic data on psychiatric side effects.

### Skin and pigment

The most conspicuous skin changes are caused by peptides that act on the **melanocortin 1 receptor** of the pigment cells. With setmelanotide, which is actually supposed to act on the melanocortin 4 receptor in the brain, the skin darkened in 62% of those treated in a meta-analysis (Sridharan 2026, PMID 39924461). In an observation over up to 46 months, lips and moles also darkened, and red hair turned brown; no malignant skin changes occurred (Kanti 2021, PMID 34058738). Bremelanotide, when given on up to 16 consecutive days, caused localized dark patches in more than a third of participants (Clayton 2022, PMID 35147466).

With the unapproved Melanotan II, newly appearing, sometimes atypical moles are added to this. A review of all published cases of suddenly appearing moles, with 179 affected people, named Melanotan among the triggers; 16% of all cases had at least one histologically atypical (dysplastic) mole (Burian 2019, PMID 31119650). Whether this results in a higher melanoma risk is covered in the next chapter.

With the GLP-1 drugs, other skin findings are in the foreground. A systematic review names injection-site reactions as most common with tirzepatide (2 to 7%) and abnormal skin sensations (dysesthesia) mainly with semaglutide: at about 5% with standard doses and up to 20% with the high obesity doses (Gupta 2026, PMID 42930571). With retatrutide in TRIUMPH-2, dysesthesia occurred dose-dependently in 4 to 7%, compared with 1% on placebo (Bellido 2026, PMID 42810372). Injection-site reactions and allergies are summarized in the chapter "Immune Reactions and Antibodies."

LL-37 is a special case: after injections into skin metastases, a patient developed numerous wart-like skin changes, initially thought to be cancer, which regressed after the treatment was stopped (Dolkar 2018, PMID 29665030). Authorities have repeatedly warned about glutathione injections for skin lightening (chapter Glutathione).

### Endocrine system

Many peptides are themselves hormones or mimic hormones. It is therefore not surprising that they intervene in regulatory circuits beyond their actual target.

- **Stress hormones and prolactin:** The ghrelin mimetics GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin, and macimorelin release not only growth hormone but also ACTH and cortisol, and some additionally prolactin. With GHRP-2, this effect is so reliable that it has been studied as a test of the adrenal axis (Arimura 2016, PMID 27020037). What repeated stimulation over months does has not been studied (chapters GHRP-2, GHRP-6, Hexarelin).
- **Suppression instead of stimulation:** Hormones that are released in pulses in the body often have the opposite effect when given continuously. With the kisspeptin analog TAK-448, testosterone in healthy men fell into the castration range during a 14-day continuous infusion (MacLean 2014, PMID 24762108). The same principle applies to gonadorelin when it is not given in the right rhythm (chapters Kisspeptin, Gonadorelin).
- **Breast enlargement in men** is a known consequence of increased growth hormone action (Liu 2007, PMID 17227934).
- **Sexual function:** Melanocortin agonists can trigger spontaneous erections; with Melanotan II, cases of priapism, a painful persistent erection, have been described (chapters Melanotan II, Setmelanotide).

### Blood sugar

Peptides can shift blood sugar in both directions – and both directions are relevant.

**Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar).** The greatest risk is with mecasermin, that is, IGF-1 as a drug: in a long-term study of 76 children, hypoglycemia was the most common side effect at 49% (Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294). For the laboratory product IGF-1 LR3, a similar or greater risk is biologically plausible but has never been measured (chapter IGF-1 LR3). Pramlintide enhances the effect of insulin injected at the same time; severe hypoglycemia was more frequent than with placebo in one study (0.57 versus 0.30 events per patient-year) (Edelman 2006, PMID 17003291). GLP-1 drugs alone hardly lower blood sugar into the dangerous range because their effect is coupled to blood sugar. In combination with sulfonylureas or insulin, however, the risk rises: with exenatide added to metformin and a sulfonylurea, mild to moderate hypoglycemia occurred in 28% and 19%, respectively, compared with 13% on placebo (Kendall 2005, PMID 15855571). More on this in the chapter "Interactions and Special Risk Groups."

Severe hypoglycemia after a supposed "weight-loss injection" in a person without diabetes is a warning sign of a counterfeit containing insulin. The cases are described in Part I in the chapter "The Gray Market."

**Higher blood sugar.** Growth hormone counteracts insulin. With ibutamoren, fasting blood sugar in healthy older people rose by an average of 0.3 mmol/L, and body weight by 2.7 kg compared with 0.8 kg on placebo (Nass 2008, PMID 18981485). In the review of growth hormone in older people, new-onset diabetes and impaired fasting glucose occurred somewhat more frequently (Liu 2007, PMID 17227934). With tesamorelin, fasting blood sugar rose temporarily in the first weeks (chapter Tesamorelin). For people with diabetes or its precursors, the growth hormone axis is therefore particularly delicate.

### What the comparison reveals

Across all organ systems, three patterns emerge.

First: the best-studied peptides – the approved GLP-1 drugs – have the best-known side effects. This is not because they are particularly dangerous, but because hundreds of thousands of people have been observed in studies and registries. Common complaints affect the gastrointestinal tract and are usually transient; more serious risks such as gallstones are established but rare in absolute terms; signals such as NAION are very rare and are being investigated further.

Second: the peptides of the growth hormone axis share a common profile of fluid retention, higher blood sugar, and a rise in stress hormones. For use over months or years in healthy people, there are virtually no data.

Third: for the majority of peptides in Groups B and D, organ risks cannot be described at all, because nobody has systematically looked for them. There, the greatest practical risk lies not in the active ingredient but in the product – more on this in the chapter "Gray-Market Products."
