## Macimorelin

Macimorelin is the most sober member of this group – and precisely for that reason a good counterexample to the gray-market peptides. It is not used for treatment but as a test: a person drinks a solution containing macimorelin, after which blood is drawn several times. If growth hormone barely rises, this suggests growth hormone deficiency. Macimorelin is approved for this purpose in the United States and the EU. It is an example of how a ghrelin mimetic can become a genuine medicine through clean comparative studies.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** small peptide-like molecule (peptidomimetic) that is active when swallowed (Piccoli 2007, PMID 17284637). Earlier designations: EP01572, ARD-07, AEZS-130.
- **Origin:** developed from research on orally active growth hormone releasers; approved on the application of Aeterna Zentaris.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a); triggers a single release of growth hormone whose magnitude reflects the functional capacity of the pituitary gland.
- **US approval:** yes (Macrilen, since 2017) for the diagnosis of growth hormone deficiency in adults. **EU approval:** yes (centrally authorized in 2019) for the same indication. For current availability in individual countries, see below.
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 (growth hormone secretagogues; macimorelin is explicitly named).
- **Evidence level: A** – two phase 3 trials on diagnostic accuracy, approval in the USA and EU. Limitation: it is a diagnostic agent, not a therapy.

### How it is supposed to work

Diagnosing growth hormone deficiency in adults is difficult. A single blood value is not enough, because growth hormone is released in bursts. A stimulation test is needed: the pituitary gland is deliberately stimulated, and one measures how much growth hormone it releases.

The reference test is the insulin hypoglycemia test (insulin tolerance test, ITT). In this test, blood glucose is deliberately lowered sharply with insulin – which is unpleasant, requires medical supervision and is not permitted in people with heart disease or seizures. Macimorelin offers an alternative: it activates the ghrelin receptor, which directly triggers growth hormone release, without lowering blood glucose. Because it is swallowed, no infusion is needed either.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **4** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **6** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov. A targeted follow-up search yielded further studies, including the first phase 3 trial, a pediatric study, several studies in healthy people and a real-world analysis.

#### Early pharmacology

As early as 2007, a study with 36 healthy men showed that the substance (then EP01572) enters the blood rapidly and dose-dependently after being swallowed and strongly releases growth hormone (Piccoli 2007, PMID 17284637). In a randomized phase 1 study with 28 healthy people, the half-life was 3.5 hours for the two lower doses; the GH peak occurred after 45 to 60 minutes. ACTH, cortisol and prolactin rose temporarily (Klaus 2020, PMID 32325373).

#### The approval trials

**First phase 3 trial:** In an open-label multicenter trial, macimorelin was compared with the combination test of arginine and GHRH that was customary at the time. The analysis included 50 adults with growth hormone deficiency and 48 healthy people. The GH peak averaged 2.36 ng/mL in those affected and 17.71 ng/mL in the healthy people. With a cutoff of 2.7 ng/mL, the test achieved a sensitivity of 82% and a specificity of 92%. The only serious drug-related adverse event reported was an asymptomatic prolongation of the QT interval on the ECG. During the trial, the GHRH preparation (sermorelin) was withdrawn from the US market, so some participants were tested with macimorelin only (Garcia 2013, PMID 23559086).

**Second phase 3 trial (basis for approval):** In a randomized crossover trial, adults with a high, intermediate and low likelihood of growth hormone deficiency as well as healthy controls received both the macimorelin test and the insulin test. 99% of the macimorelin tests but only 82% of the insulin tests were evaluable. With cutoffs of 2.8 ng/mL (macimorelin) and 5.1 ng/mL (ITT), sensitivity was 87% and specificity 96%; on repetition, the result was the same in 97%. No serious adverse events due to macimorelin occurred. A uniform cutoff of 5.1 ng/mL for both tests yielded 92% sensitivity with 96% specificity in a post hoc analysis (Garcia 2018, PMID 29860473; NCT02558829). Further analyses showed that age, body weight and sex did not substantially affect test accuracy (Garcia 2021, PMID 33320108).

#### Real-world experience

A retrospective analysis of 37 patients at one clinic found that with the cutoff of 2.8 ng/mL approved in the United States, 40.5% were classified as having growth hormone deficiency, whereas with 5.1 ng/mL it was 54.0%. The discordant classifications mainly concerned people with severe obesity and a low pretest probability (Yadav 2026, PMID 41793495). This shows that the cutoff is not trivial and that caution is needed in interpretation in people with severe obesity.

#### Children and other uses

- **Children:** An open-label dose-finding study with 24 children and adolescents with suspected growth hormone deficiency found no macimorelin-related adverse effects (Csákváry 2021, PMID 34438400). The subsequent phase 3 trial DETECT with 101 children (NCT04786873) was completed in June 2024; results have not been posted in the registry, and I have not found a publication in PubMed.
- **Cancer cachexia:** In a pilot study with 15 cancer patients with pathological weight loss, macimorelin was well tolerated over one week; weight and quality of life improved numerically, but without a significant difference from placebo (Herodes 2023, PMID 36860137).

### Animal and laboratory studies

In mice with treatment-resistant epilepsy, macimorelin reduced the number and duration of seizures during the two-week treatment but did not alter the course of the disease (Buckinx 2021, PMID 34157194). Structural studies show how macimorelin binds in the ghrelin receptor (Wang 2025, PMID 40542284). These studies are not relevant to the approved use.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** Macimorelin is taken only once per test; side effects are correspondingly limited. In the approval trial, no serious adverse events due to macimorelin occurred (Garcia 2018, PMID 29860473). ACTH, cortisol and prolactin rise temporarily (Klaus 2020, PMID 32325373).

**QT prolongation:** In a thorough ECG study with 60 healthy people, a deliberately excessive (supratherapeutic) single dose prolonged the QT interval – a measure of the heart's electrical recovery – by up to 9.61 milliseconds on average; the upper limit of the confidence interval exceeded the regulatory threshold of 10 milliseconds (Lissy 2021, PMID 32961034). Caution is therefore advised in people taking other QT-prolonging medications. Details on contraindications and interactions are governed by the prescribing information; the test is performed by a physician.

**Misdiagnosis:** Like any stimulation test, the macimorelin test can produce false-positive and false-negative results, especially in people with severe obesity (Yadav 2026, PMID 41793495).

A cancer risk is not to be expected from a single dose; the discussion about persistently elevated IGF-1 (see the introduction to this group) therefore does not apply to macimorelin.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU:** centrally authorized for the diagnosis of growth hormone deficiency in adults. Whether the product is actually marketed in Germany and Austria depends on the marketing decisions of the marketing authorization holder and should be checked for updates.
- **USA:** approved (Macrilen) for the same indication; the distribution rights have changed hands in recent years.
- **Use:** exclusively as a physician-performed function test according to the prescribing information; no therapeutic approval.
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2). For doping controls, metabolites of macimorelin and related ghrelin mimetics have been characterized (Lange 2021, PMID 33458843).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

*Established:* The pediatric trial DETECT (NCT04786873) has been completed; the results are pending. Another study examined how macimorelin affects the hormone copeptin (NCT03844217). *Author's assessment:* Extending the approval to children would be the logical next step but depends on the outcome of the DETECT trial. A therapeutic use, for example in cachexia, is not foreseeable based on the data so far.

### Conclusion

Macimorelin is a diagnostic agent approved in the United States and the EU: a single oral test detects growth hormone deficiency in adults about as reliably as the burdensome insulin test, with 87% sensitivity and 96% specificity in the approval trial. The most important known side effect is a slight prolongation of the QT interval at a supratherapeutic dose. Macimorelin is neither intended nor studied for treatment, muscle building or "anti-aging."
