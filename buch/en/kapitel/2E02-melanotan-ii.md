## Melanotan II

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** ring-shaped (cyclic) peptide of seven amino acids, a shortened and chemically stabilized variant of the body's own hormone alpha-MSH. The ring structure protects against rapid breakdown and makes the molecule considerably more potent than its natural model (Dorr 1996, PMID 8637402).
- **Origin:** developed in the late 1980s and early 1990s at the University of Arizona in Tucson, originally with the aim of producing a tan without sun and thus preventing skin cancer (Hadley 2006, PMID 16412534).
- **Mechanism of action:** non-selective agonist at melanocortin receptors: MC1R (pigment production in the skin), MC3R and MC4R (appetite, sexual function) and others.
- **Approval EU:** no.
- **Approval USA:** no.
- **WADA:** not named explicitly; as a substance not approved anywhere, it falls under group S0 (non-approved substances) and is therefore prohibited at all times (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **C** – only very small pilot studies from the 1990s with a total of around two dozen participants, supplemented by numerous case reports of side effects from uncontrolled self-administration.

### How it is supposed to work

The skin tans when pigment cells (melanocytes) produce more melanin. The signal for this comes, among other things, from the hormone alpha-MSH, which binds to the MC1R receptor on the pigment cells. After sunbathing, alpha-MSH production in the skin increases, and the pigment cells switch to the dark, protective eumelanin.

Melanotan II mimics this signal – without UV radiation first having to damage the skin. That was the original idea: a "tan from a syringe" that protects against sunburn. The problem: melanotan II binds not only to MC1R but also to the MC3R and MC4R receptors in the brain. These control appetite, energy expenditure and sexual arousal. This is where the typical accompanying effects come from: nausea, yawning and stretching, reduced appetite and spontaneous erections.

Research into this "side effect" gave rise to two other drugs which, unlike melanotan II, were developed further clinically:

- **Afamelanotide**, formerly called melanotan I, is a linear, longer molecule that acts preferentially on the skin and is approved as an implant for a rare light-related disease (chapter 2E03).
- **Bremelanotide** (PT-141) is a slightly modified form of melanotan II that is approved in the USA for the treatment of low sexual desire in women (chapter 2E01).

On the internet, these names are often mixed up. "Melanotan" without further specification can refer to one or the other depending on the seller – or to something else entirely (Langan 2011, PMID 21547334).

### Human studies

**Size of the evidence base (search October 2026):** The automated search for this book found **8** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and a single entry on ClinicalTrials.gov, which on inspection proved untrustworthy (see Outlook) – so there are **0** reliable registered studies. The PubMed figure is also misleading: four of the eight studies examined melanotan I, i.e. the later afamelanotide, not melanotan II. For melanotan II itself, there are only three small original studies and one summary by the same research group. The literature on side effects is far more extensive: case reports, case series, analyses of internet forums and laboratory analyses of gray-market products.

#### The Clinical Studies of the 1990s

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dorr 1996, PMID 8637402 | 3 healthy men, single-blind, alternating active drug and saline | 2 weeks | Tanning of face, upper body and buttocks in 2 of 3; spontaneous erections; nausea, fatigue |
| Wessells 1998, PMID 9679884 | 10 men with psychogenic erectile dysfunction, double-blind crossover study vs. placebo | Single doses | Erections in 8 of 10; duration of high rigidity 38.0 vs. 3.0 minutes |
| Wessells 2000, PMID 11018622 | 10 men with organic erectile dysfunction, double-blind crossover study | Single doses | Erections after 12 of 19 injections vs. 1 of 21 placebo doses; 4 of 19 injections with severe nausea |
| Wessells 2000 (summary), PMID 11035391 | both studies combined, 20 men | Single doses | Erections without sexual stimulation in 17 of 20; increased desire after 68% vs. 19% of doses |

The first study in humans (Dorr 1996, PMID 8637402) was a pilot study with only three men. Even here, all the effects later described by users on the internet appeared: two of the three men tanned visibly, mild nausea occurred at most dose levels, and one experienced more pronounced fatigue. A "stretching and yawning complex" was accompanied by spontaneous erections that recurred repeatedly for one to five hours depending on the dose.

The research group led by urologist Hunter Wessells then pursued the erectile effect. In two small crossover studies (each participant received active drug and placebo in random order), melanotan II triggered erections in the majority of men with erectile dysfunction, even without sexual stimulation (Wessells 1998, PMID 9679884; Wessells 2000, PMID 11018622). In the summary of both studies, the men reported increased desire after 68% of melanotan doses and after 19% of placebo doses. Severe nausea occurred in 12.9% (Wessells 2000, PMID 11035391).

After that, clinical research on melanotan II ended. Development switched to bremelanotide, which was tested as a nasal spray and later as an injection. There is not a single controlled study of melanotan II for skin tanning in healthy people.

#### The Studies on Melanotan I

For context: the remaining studies from the database search concern melanotan I. In them, volunteers produced more eumelanin after injections, almost twice as much as before in the skin of the forearm (Dorr 2000, PMID 11045725). In combination with sunlight, the tan was intensified and prolonged (Dorr 2004, PMID 15262693). These results belong to the history of afamelanotide and cannot be transferred to melanotan II, which acts more strongly in the brain.

#### Who Uses Melanotan II

Because melanotan II was never approved, almost all knowledge about its current use comes from observations outside of studies. As early as 2009, British experts described the spread of melanotan I and II in the general population in the British Medical Journal (Evans-Brown 2009, PMID 19224885). A systematic review counted melanotan among the commonly injected agents for body and performance enhancement, alongside anabolic steroids and growth hormone (Brennan 2017, PMID 26806443).

An analysis of British and Irish internet forums from 2016 and 2017 evaluated 623 posts by 205 people (Gilhooley 2021, PMID 34464955). The main motives users cited were a tanned complexion before vacations and before bodybuilding competitions. The forums showed misinformation, self-devised dosing schemes, combination with tanning beds and other risky practices. An online survey of users describes how easy the substance was to obtain (Callaghan 2018, PMID 30142729).

In recent years, advertising has increasingly shifted to social media such as TikTok, where melanotan is promoted as the "Barbie drug." Several dermatology journals have analyzed such content and warn against trivializing it (Orr 2025, PMID 38808957; Herlihy 2025, PMID 39148422; Sidhu 2026, PMID 41559011).

### Animal and laboratory studies

In basic research, melanotan II is a standard tool for activating melanocortin receptors. Accordingly, there are numerous animal experiments, but they mostly answer questions in neuroscience and do not concern safety in humans:

- **Weight and fat:** In overweight mice and rats on a high-fat diet, melanotan II reduced weight and fat mass, mainly through lower food intake (Strader 2007, PMID 17567964).
- **Body temperature:** In mice, it triggered a temporary severe drop in body temperature, mediated by mast cells and histamine (Jain 2018, PMID 29812984).
- **Social behavior:** In a mouse model of autism, one week of administration improved measures of social behavior (Minakova 2019, PMID 30629642). The suspected pathway involves activation of the oxytocin system (chapter 2E05).

None of these results has been confirmed in humans.

### Risks and side effects

The risks of melanotan II can be sorted into three groups: the expected effects via the melanocortin receptors, the skin changes with the open question of skin cancer, and severe individual cases.

#### Expected Effects

Nausea, facial flushing, yawning and stretching, loss of appetite, fatigue and spontaneous erections already occurred in the studies (Dorr 1996, PMID 8637402; Wessells 2000, PMID 11035391). Pigmentation affects not only the skin: case reports describe transverse dark bands in the fingernails (Paurobally 2013, PMID 22182016) and pigment changes in the oral mucosa (Bonchev 2026, PMID 41752902). Paradoxically, light, depigmented spots have also been described (Ume 2026, PMID 42212476).

#### Moles and Melanoma

This is the concern that occupies dermatologists the most. Melanotan II stimulates all pigment cells, including those in existing moles (nevi). Several case reports describe:

- **new moles appearing in large numbers** (eruptive nevi) after melanotan injections (Cousen 2009, PMID 19575725; Thestrup-Pedersen 2011, PMID 21453640), some with a conspicuous, atypical appearance (Reid 2013, PMID 23914578);
- **existing moles darkening and changing**;
- **melanomas**, i.e. black skin cancer, during or shortly after use (Paurobally 2011, PMID 21564053; Ong 2012, PMID 22724573). A 20-year-old woman with fair skin developed a melanoma on her buttocks three months after three to four weeks of self-treatment combined with tanning-bed sessions (Hjuler 2014, PMID 24355990). A case published in 2025 describes a melanoma of the oral mucosa in a 22-year-old who had used melanotan II nasal spray (Yassin Alsabbagh 2025, PMID 40210573).

A review of all published cases of suddenly appearing moles (179 people) found melanotan among the triggers, alongside immunosuppression and chemotherapy; 16% of cases had at least one dysplastic, i.e. conspicuously altered, mole (Burian 2019, PMID 31119650).

**What these data mean:** Case reports do not prove a causal relationship. Many of those affected were simultaneously using tanning beds or sunbathing intensively, which independently increases the risk of melanoma. A review concludes that a link has not been conclusively demonstrated, but that the accumulation of cases is reason for caution (Habbema 2017, PMID 28266027). Because melanotan II has never been studied systematically, no one can say how high a possible risk is. For comparison: for the related, approved afamelanotide, a study using dermoscopy found only temporary changes in moles with no indication of malignant transformation (chapter 2E03) – but these data do not apply to melanotan II.

#### Severe Individual Cases

- **Priapism:** a painful prolonged erection, a urological emergency. Several case reports describe it after melanotan injections (Devlin 2013, PMID 23537392; Dreyer 2019, PMID 30796078). In one case, surgical decompression was necessary (Mallory 2021, PMID 33460908); in another, erectile function had still not been restored four weeks later (Dreyer 2019, PMID 30796078).
- **Muscle breakdown and kidney damage:** A 39-year-old man, by his own account, injected six times the recommended starting amount. He developed a racing heart, agitation and severe muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) with a rise in the muscle enzyme CK to 17,773 IU/L and temporary kidney damage; the substance was confirmed in the laboratory as melanotan II (Nelson 2012, PMID 23121206).
- **Renal infarction:** a case report with literature review suspects melanotan II as the cause of an occlusion of renal vessels (Peters 2020, PMID 31953620).
- **Brain swelling:** A case report describes posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), a swelling in the back of the brain, in connection with melanotan (Kaski 2013, PMID 23648958).
- **Skin inflammation:** Pyoderma gangrenosum, a severe ulcerative skin disease, has also been described (Dickson 2025, PMID 41487785).

#### Gray-Market Risks

A laboratory bought vials of melanotan II from three online shops. All promised 10 mg per vial; in fact, they contained between 4.32 and 8.84 mg. Products from two shops contained 4.1 to 5.9% unknown impurities (Breindahl 2015, PMID 24771717). A forensic laboratory determined a purity of 30% in a seized sample (Deville 2024, PMID 39302005). In addition, there are the risks of non-sterile preparation and shared needles, which experts explicitly point out (Gilhooley 2021, PMID 34464955). More on purity and counterfeits on the gray market can be found in Part I and Part IV.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU:** Melanotan II is not approved in any member state. Because it is offered for use in humans with effects on bodily functions, it is legally considered a medicinal product; manufacture, import and sale without approval are prohibited. The label "for research purposes only" does not change this.
- **Germany and Austria:** not approved. Supplying it for use in humans violates the respective Medicinal Products Act. Authorities and customs seize shipments.
- **Official warnings:** Several national medicines and health authorities have warned against melanotan I and II (Habbema 2017, PMID 28266027). To the author's knowledge, the authorities in the United Kingdom and Ireland were among the first from 2008 onward, followed later by other European countries and Australia. In the United Kingdom, sale is explicitly illegal (Yassin Alsabbagh 2025, PMID 40210573).
- **USA:** no FDA approval; distribution as a drug for use in humans is not permitted.
- **WADA:** prohibited as a non-approved substance (S0).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established (registered studies):** none. ClinicalTrials.gov did contain an entry on melanotan II in vitiligo; however, the sponsor of this entry has also registered entries that describe themselves as fictitious. The entry is therefore not counted here as a genuine study. Incidentally, this shows that a registry entry is not proof that a study is actually taking place.

**Author's assessment:** There is no realistic path to approval as a tanning agent for healthy people: the benefit is cosmetic, the side effects via the brain are pronounced, and the question of moles and melanoma would have to be clarified in large, long studies. Medically meaningful applications of melanocortin agonists are being pursued with more selective molecules – afamelanotide for the skin, bremelanotide and setmelanotide for the brain. Popularity on social media is likely to further increase the number of case reports.

### Conclusion

Melanotan II is a research peptide from the 1990s that was tested in only around two dozen people in small studies and never received approval. It tans the skin but, via receptors in the brain, regularly triggers nausea and spontaneous erections. Case reports describe new and changed moles, melanomas, priapism and muscle breakdown; a causal link to skin cancer has not been proven, but has not been ruled out either. In laboratory analyses, gray-market products contained less active substance than stated and unknown impurities. The data are not sufficient to call melanotan II safe.
