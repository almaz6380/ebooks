# Methodology

This appendix describes how the studies for this book were searched for, selected, and checked. It is intended to enable you to follow the research and, if necessary, to repeat or update it yourself.

### Databases

The research is based on two freely accessible databases:

- **PubMed** (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), the literature database of the US National Library of Medicine. It lists publications from biomedical journals with title, authors, summary (abstract), and keywords. Each entry has a unique number, the PMID.
- **ClinicalTrials.gov** (clinicaltrials.gov), the trial registry of the US National Institutes of Health. Here, clinical studies from all over the world are registered before they begin, with design, planned number of participants, endpoints, and status. Each study has an NCT number.

Both databases were queried via their official, publicly accessible application programming interfaces: PubMed via the E-utilities of the National Center for Biotechnology Information, ClinicalTrials.gov via version 2 of its application programming interface. Other databases such as Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, the EU trial registry, or national registries were not systematically searched.

### Search period

The systematic queries were carried out in **October 2026**; the hit counts for all peptides date from October 28, 2026. Supplementary targeted searches for individual studies, case reports, and reviews were carried out during the same period. Studies published later are not taken into account. The book is current as of November 2026.

### Search strategy

For each of the 58 peptides and comparator substances, a separate PubMed search term was defined. It includes the international nonproprietary name as well as common abbreviations, code names, and trade names, and searches in title and abstract (tag `[tiab]`) or in the title only (`[ti]`). For ambiguous terms, the search term was restricted, for example for collagen peptides to papers on oral intake or for kisspeptin to papers on administration of the peptide.

Three filters were applied to this search term:

**Human studies.** To find clinical studies in humans, the search term was combined with the following filter:

`(clinical trial[pt] OR randomized controlled trial[pt]) AND humans[mh]`

The `[pt]` part searches for the publication type that PubMed assigns to each article; `humans[mh]` stands for the subject heading "humans" in the MeSH keyword system. This finds articles that are labeled as a clinical trial or as a randomized controlled trial and concern humans.

**Meta-analyses and reviews.** For summarizing papers, the search term was combined with this filter:

`(meta-analysis[pt] OR systematic review[pt])`

**Preclinical research.** For peptides with fewer than 25 human studies found, the most relevant remaining papers without the human studies filter were additionally evaluated in order to capture animal and laboratory studies, case reports, and analyses of gray-market products.

**Registered studies.** In ClinicalTrials.gov, a separate, usually simple search term with the name of the agent was used for each peptide. All entries were captured regardless of status and study phase.

Beyond these automated searches, targeted searches were carried out for each chapter for the most important approval and outcome trials, large meta-analyses, trial programs (for example STEP, SURMOUNT, or SELECT), safety data, case reports, and laboratory analyses of gray-market products. For Part I, papers on the history of peptide medicine, dosage forms, study methodology, and the gray market were also researched.

### Number of hits

In total, the 58 queries with the human studies filter yielded 3,216 hits, and with the filter for meta-analyses and reviews 1,739 hits. In ClinicalTrials.gov, 4,879 registry entries were found. These totals contain double counts, because some studies concern several peptides – for example, comparative studies between semaglutide and tirzepatide. The figures for each peptide are given at the beginning of the section "Human studies" in the respective chapter.

The distribution is very uneven: the approved GLP-1 drugs account for hundreds of human studies, whereas for many gray-market peptides there are none or only a handful.

### Selection and evaluation

For each peptide, all hits with the human studies filter were recorded as a short list (title, year, journal, first author); they form the basis of the study index in the appendix. The most important human studies and reviews according to PubMed relevance were read and evaluated with their abstracts, supplemented by the papers found through targeted searches.

The following applies to the presentation in the text:

- Every study mentioned was read on the basis of its PubMed entry or registry entry.
- Figures – such as numbers of participants, percentages, weight changes, or p-values – come exclusively from the abstract of the publication or from the registry entry. Anything not documented there is not given as a figure.
- Full texts were not systematically evaluated. Details that appear only in the full text, such as individual side effect rates or subgroups, are therefore partly missing.
- Animal and laboratory results are explicitly labeled as such.
- For ongoing studies, only what is in the registry is reported. They are labeled as *established* (registered, ongoing), and the author's own forecasts as *assessment*.

### Evidence levels

Each peptide was assigned an evidence level according to the following criteria:

| Level | Criterion |
|---|---|
| A | several large randomized trials in humans, approval as a drug |
| B | individual randomized trials in humans |
| C | only small or uncontrolled studies in humans |
| D | only animal and laboratory studies |

The rating refers to the overall data in humans and to the most important indication studied. It is the author's assessment based on the studies evaluated and not an official classification.

### Limitations of the methodology

Like any literature search, this one has limitations that you should keep in mind when reading:

**Hits are not the same as independent studies.** A large study is often published in several articles – main result, subgroups, follow-up, quality of life. Each of these articles is a separate hit. Conversely, studies can be combined in one article. The hit counts therefore reflect the order of magnitude of research, not the exact number of independent studies.

**Publication types are not always correct.** The human studies filter is based on PubMed's indexing. Some studies are not labeled as clinical trials and are therefore missing; some papers labeled as clinical trials turn out on closer inspection to be observational studies or laboratory work with human material. Very recent articles are often not yet fully indexed.

**Search terms may have gaps.** A search term finds only what is described with the chosen words in the title or abstract. Studies that refer to a substance only by a code name or a different spelling may be missing. Conversely, ambiguous abbreviations such as "KPV" or "SS-31" may also capture papers on completely different topics. For the chapters, the hits were therefore screened and not adopted unchecked.

**Languages.** PubMed predominantly lists English-language journals. Papers published only in other languages and in journals that are not indexed are largely missing. This particularly affects some peptides that are researched and used mainly in Russia or other Eastern European countries, such as Semax, Selank, Epitalon, or thymalin. For these, the actual body of studies may be more extensive, but it is difficult for international readers to verify.

**Unpublished studies.** Studies that were never published are not included in PubMed. The ClinicalTrials.gov registry helps to identify such gaps but does not capture all studies worldwide.

**Abstracts only.** The evaluation is based on the abstracts. They reflect the main results but may present limitations, side effects, or methodological weaknesses in abbreviated form.

**Snapshot.** The research reflects the status as of October 2026. In rapidly growing fields such as GLP-1 drugs, new studies appear every week.

### AI support and quality control

This book was researched and written with the support of artificial intelligence and revised by the author. The AI was used for the following tasks:

- carrying out the database queries via the interfaces mentioned, using purpose-written helper programs,
- reading and evaluating the abstracts and registry entries,
- producing draft texts according to fixed style and working rules.

To avoid the known risk that AI systems invent sources or figures, binding rules applied: every study in the text is cited with its PMID or NCT number; every figure must come from an abstract or registry entry that was actually retrieved; studies may be mentioned only if their entry was previously retrieved and read.

In addition, each chapter was checked with a verification program. For every PMID and NCT number mentioned in the text, it retrieves the entry in PubMed or ClinicalTrials.gov and reports an error if a number does not exist. It also compares the first author and year of publication given in the text before the PMID with the database entry and flags discrepancies. Finally, it searches for impermissible wording such as promises of cure, dosage recommendations, or references to sources of supply. A chapter was considered finished only when this check ran without errors.

This check ensures that every cited source exists and is correctly attributed. It cannot completely rule out that a result is presented in the text in abbreviated or misleading form. That is why all sources are given in such a way that you can look them up yourself – how to do this is explained in the section "About This Book" at the beginning.
