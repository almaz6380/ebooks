## Gray-Market Products: What Laboratory Analyses Show

In the chapter "The Gray Market," Part I described what "research peptides" are and presented individual sensational findings: semaglutide powder with a purity of 7.7 to 14.4%, Melanotan vials containing less than half the stated amount, counterfeit Ozempic containing insulin. This chapter goes one step further. It asks who analyzes such products in the first place, which types of errors recur across many investigations, what these analyses cannot show – and what data from poison control centers reveal about the consequences of dosing errors with GLP-1 drugs.

### Who analyzes gray-market products

Most of the data come from three sources.

**Official medicines control laboratories.** In Europe, they work together in a network coordinated by the European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines. They examine samples seized by customs, police, or health authorities. A Belgian laboratory, for example, developed a screening method with which 25 different peptides can be identified in unknown injectable preparations in a single run – including some that had not yet been seized but were already being discussed in internet forums (Vanhee 2015, PMID 26003685).

**Anti-doping and forensic laboratories.** They analyze seized goods from the sports environment in order to keep their detection methods up to date. The findings from Cologne, Norway, and Denmark in Part I come from this source.

**Test purchases by research groups.** These groups deliberately buy products from online sellers and check what arrives. Such studies are rarer, but come closest to the situation of buyers.

The methods used answer different questions: mass spectrometry shows *which* substance is present; liquid chromatography measures *how much* and *how pure*; special methods detect metals, solvent residues, bacteria, or endotoxins. No laboratory routinely tests for everything. If a report determines only identity, it says nothing about germs, and vice versa.

### Four types of errors that keep recurring

Across the individual analyses, the findings can be grouped into four categories.

| Type of error | Examples from analyses | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong active ingredient or none | 9 of 36 seized samples without any peptide or protein | Høj 2021, PMID 33686802 |
| Wrong amount | only 41% of 44 internet products with the correct amount of active ingredient | Van Wagoner 2017, PMID 29183075 |
| Low purity, degradation products | purity of 5 to 75% for certain peptides | Janvier 2018, PMID 30029448 |
| Dangerous accompanying substances | arsenic and lead, in some cases up to ten times the limit | Janvier 2018, PMID 30029448 |

#### Wrong active ingredient or none at all

In 2019, a Danish forensic laboratory received 36 seized samples suspected of containing peptides or proteins. Fifteen contained a prohibited doping agent, 12 a substance that was not on the Danish doping list, and 9 contained no peptide or protein at all (Høj 2021, PMID 33686802). In a Europe-wide customs operation, the laboratories reported growth hormone, sermorelin, Melanotan II, and preparations with no active ingredient at all among the seized injectable products; the shipments were on average too large for personal use and presumably partly intended for resale (Venhuis 2016, PMID 26456392). In a Belgian case, a preparation suspected to be IGF-1 instead contained a different growth factor, epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Vanhee 2017, PMID 27390261).

A horse doping laboratory analyzed products offered online under the names "TB500," "TB1000," and "SGF1000." The contents of the TB products did not consistently match earlier descriptions. "SGF1000" consisted predominantly of connective tissue and blood proteins from sheep; the supposedly growth-promoting components were extremely diluted (Delcourt 2023, PMID 36482504).

How often label and contents diverge is shown by a US study that concerned not peptides but so-called SARMs, which, however, use the same distribution channel: of 44 internet products, only 52% contained any active ingredient from the advertised class of substances at all. 39% contained other unapproved substances, including the growth hormone secretagogue ibutamoren, 9% contained no active ingredient at all, and 25% contained undeclared substances (Van Wagoner 2017, PMID 29183075).

#### Wrong amount

Even when the right substance is in the vial, the amount is often wrong. In the US analysis mentioned above, the measured amount of active ingredient matched the label in only 18 of 44 products (41%) (Van Wagoner 2017, PMID 29183075). A French toxicology laboratory examined 75 drugs from the bodybuilding scene seized on the black market: 33% were substandard, that is, incorrectly dosed, 32% were falsified, meaning they had different contents than stated, and only 19% fully matched the original (Fabresse 2021, PMID 33838562). A five-year European market surveillance with 324 samples from 13 countries concluded, based on the limited quantitative data, that most samples contained effective amounts and some were even overdosed (Barrios 2025, PMID 40551438).

Both are dangerous: too little active ingredient tempts people to "top up" the dose; too much leads directly to stronger side effects. With GLP-1 drugs, whose tolerability depends heavily on slow dose escalation, an unknown amount is particularly problematic.

#### Low purity, degradation products, and designer variants

The most comprehensive systematic study comes from Belgium. The control laboratory bought the ten most frequently counterfeited peptide drugs on the Belgian market from three presumably illegal internet pharmacies and tested them for active ingredient, peptide impurities, small organic foreign substances, metals, and solvent residues. The amount per vial varied greatly, and for peptides containing the amino acid cysteine, purity ranged between 5 and 75% (Janvier 2018, PMID 30029448).

Other laboratories found numerous oxidized, that is, chemically altered, peptide forms in black-market products of IGF-1 variants (Mongongu 2021, PMID 33587816) and a purity of 30% in a seized Melanotan II sample (Deville 2024, PMID 39302005). In preparations seized by Danish customs, variants of GHRP-2, GHRP-6, ipamorelin, and a GHRH fragment were found, each with an additional glycine amino acid placed in front (Gajda 2019, PMID 30136411). Such "designer variants" are presumably intended to evade doping tests. Their effect and safety in humans are completely unknown.

#### Dangerous accompanying substances

In the Belgian study, the toxic elements arsenic and lead were found. One sample was contaminated with lead, and several contained arsenic in concentrations up to ten times the international limit for injectable drugs. More detailed analysis showed that the arsenic was entirely in the more toxic inorganic form. The authors point out that arsenic is carcinogenic and that lead accumulates in the body – an additional, insidious risk with repeated use over months (Janvier 2018, PMID 30029448).

Added to this are germs and bacterial components. The same laboratory detected the bacterium *Bacillus cereus* in two illegal peptide preparations for injection (Janvier 2018, PMID 28941153). In the semaglutide test purchases from Part I, endotoxins were detectable in all samples (Ashraf 2024, PMID 39509151). Why this is relevant for the immune system is described in the chapter "Immune Reactions and Antibodies."

### Ever new substances

The gray market reacts quickly to trends. A European market surveillance with 1,104 reported cases from 16 countries between 2017 and 2019 found that 11% of samples contained biological molecules, above all "research peptides"; this was the second most common category overall (Deconinck 2021, PMID 33453144). Belgian laboratories identified the "anti-aging peptide" Epitalon (Vanhee 2015, PMID 25535022) as well as the nootropic peptides Selank and Semax in seized preparations; the laboratory then developed its own detection method for ten such peptides offered online (Vanhee 2020, PMID 31667971). Meanwhile, agents that are still in clinical trials are also being offered, such as retatrutide and amycretin (chapters Retatrutide, Amycretin).

For the control laboratories, this means a race: a substance for which no reference standard yet exists cannot be reliably identified. For buyers, it means that the least is known about the contents and quality of the newest products.

Increasingly, gray-market products are also appearing in forms that seem more harmless than an injection. A US study found 24 patches and one gel sold as dietary supplements and advertised as "natural GLP-1." None provided a certificate of analysis, many advertised misleadingly; under US law, selling such patches as dietary supplements is not permitted (White 2026, PMID 41480958). In the European market surveillance, 24% of muscle-building preparations were presented as dietary supplements – people who buy them often do not know that they are taking an unapproved drug (Barrios 2025, PMID 40551438).

### What the analyses do not show

As clear as the findings are, they have limits.

- **No representative sample.** Seized goods are a selection of what catches the attention of customs. Test purchases usually cover only a few sellers. The proportion of defective products in the market as a whole cannot be calculated from this.
- **Snapshots.** A seller whose goods are correct today may deliver a different batch tomorrow. Good results from one analysis do not apply to the next vial.
- **Not everything is tested.** Many reports determine only the identity of the active ingredient. Whether a product with the "right" contents was sterile and free of endotoxins then remains open.
- **No statement about effect and safety.** Even a pure product is only as safe as the active ingredient itself. For most gray-market peptides, human studies on this are lacking (see Part II).

### GLP-1 drugs: what poison control centers report

For the GLP-1 drugs, there is an additional data source that shows what actually goes wrong in use: calls to poison control centers. In the US, these are collected in a national database. These data do not always distinguish between the original product, compounded drugs, and gray-market goods, but they show the magnitude of the problems.

| Study | Period and scope | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| Gaw 2024, PMID 38421490 | US, 2017–2022, 5,713 cases | 79.9% medication errors; 6.2% serious outcomes, one death |
| Miller 2026, PMID 41634285 | US, 2012–2023, 10,033 cases | more cases after obesity approval, share with physician or hospital contact from 23.0 to 33.5% |
| Senthilkumar 2026, PMID 41784916 | US, 2017–2024, 13,924 cases | 91.7% with no or mild effects; 42 serious outcomes, two deaths |
| Ho 2025, PMID 40371098 | California, 2017–2023, 1,047 cases | 21.0% in the emergency department, 4.4% hospitalized; hypoglycemia in 3.8% |

Several patterns stand out:

**The number of cases rose sharply with increasing use.** From 2021 to 2022, the rate of reported cases per million inhabitants increased by 80.9% (Gaw 2024, PMID 38421490). Between 2017 and 2024, it rose by a total of 1,830.8% (Senthilkumar 2026, PMID 41784916).

**Most cases are medication errors.** In all analyses, unintentional errors in use predominated, for example a double or incorrectly measured dose (Gaw 2024, PMID 38421490; Ho 2025, PMID 40371098). In an analysis from a single center, 21% of cases were due to errors when using the prefilled pen for the first time (Marshall 2024, PMID 38861153). Errors therefore also happen with approved products. In the California analysis, 36 cases involved compounded drugs; in 91.7% of these, errors in measuring or injecting were the cause (Ho 2025, PMID 40371098). This fits with the case reports of confusion between milliliters, "units," and milligrams with vials described in Part I.

**The consequences are usually mild, but not always.** Nausea and vomiting predominate. A notable minority, however, needs medical treatment, and there were deaths (Gaw 2024, PMID 38421490; Senthilkumar 2026, PMID 41784916). Hypoglycemia occurred in 3.4% (Muschler 2025, PMID 39803696) to 3.8% (Ho 2025, PMID 40371098) of cases; in another analysis it occurred rarely, but also after use of a GLP-1 drug alone (Marshall 2024, PMID 38861153). How severe an overdose can be is shown by the case described in the chapter "Immune Reactions" of a woman who, after an accidental tenfold dose from a compounding pharmacy, had to be mechanically ventilated because of massive swelling in her throat and also developed hypoglycemia (Kraft 2026, PMID 42194666).

**Adolescents are a separate risk group.** Among children and adolescents between 6 and 17 years of age, the case rate rose most sharply between 2017 and 2024. They were hospitalized more often than adults (relative risk 2.66), and their cases were considerably more often due to intentional misuse (relative risk 8.12) (Senthilkumar 2026, PMID 41784916). More on this in the following chapter.

### What follows from this

The laboratory analyses and the data from poison control centers describe two sides of the same problem. The analyses show that gray-market products can deviate from the label in every conceivable respect: in the substance, in the amount, in purity, and in toxic accompanying substances. The poison control data show that measuring errors are common even with tested products – and that vials that first have to be reconstituted and converted are particularly error-prone.

Combining the two results in a risk that cannot be described by any study of an active ingredient: anyone who injects a gray-market peptide knows neither the contents nor the amount, and every deviation is further amplified by possible calculation errors of their own. The safety data from the approval trials described in Part II do not apply to these products.

### Key points in brief

- Official, forensic, and anti-doping laboratories regularly find wrong active ingredients, wrong amounts, low purity, and designer variants in gray-market peptides.
- A systematic Belgian analysis additionally found arsenic and lead, in some cases up to ten times the limit for injectables, as well as bacteria in injectable preparations.
- New "research peptides" appear faster than control laboratories can develop detection methods.
- Analyses are snapshots; a good result does not apply to the next batch.
- In the US, the number of calls to poison control centers concerning GLP-1 drugs has risen sharply. Most cases are medication errors with mild consequences; a minority are serious. With compounded preparations, almost all cases were due to measuring errors.
