## A Brief History: From Insulin to the GLP-1 Medications

The history of peptide medications is a history of breakthroughs, wrong turns, and astonishing coincidences. It begins with a desperate experiment in Toronto, leads via the venom of an American desert lizard, and ends – for now – with weight-loss injections that are among the best-selling medicines in the world. This chapter tells the most important milestones. It is meant to help you put today's excitement about peptides into perspective: much of what is sold as revolutionary stands on the shoulders of a hundred years of painstaking research.

### Before 1922: Messengers Are Discovered

Around 1900, a new idea took hold in physiology: organs communicate not only via nerves but also via chemical messengers in the blood. In 1902, the British physiologists William Bayliss and Ernest Starling described secretin, a substance from the intestinal wall that stimulates the pancreas to produce its juice. Shortly afterward, Starling coined the term "hormone" for such messengers. Secretin is – as we know today – a peptide. A peptide thus stood at the very beginning of all hormone research.

At the same time, it was known that diabetes mellitus was connected with the pancreas. Dogs whose pancreas had been removed developed fatal diabetes. Many research groups tried to extract the suspected blood-sugar-lowering substance from the gland. However, the extracts were either ineffective or so contaminated that they triggered severe reactions.

### 1922: Insulin – The First Peptide Medication

In the summer of 1921, a group at the University of Toronto achieved the decisive step. Frederick Banting and Charles Best, under the direction of John Macleod, obtained an extract that lowered blood sugar in dogs without a pancreas. The biochemist James Collip improved the purification to the point where the extract could be tested in humans. In January 1922, a 14-year-old boy with severe diabetes received the first injections in Toronto. In the same year, the group reported on its experience in patients (Banting 1922, PMID 20314060).

The effect was dramatic: type 1 diabetes, until then fatal within a short time, became a treatable chronic disease. A review marking the centenary describes precisely this transformation as the core of the discovery (Sims 2021, PMID 34267380). Banting and Macleod received the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1923.

The following decades, as one historical review summarizes, revolved around very practical questions: producing enough insulin, making it purer to avoid allergic reactions, establishing standards, and extending its duration of action so that fewer injections were needed (Owens 2022, PMID 34708913). Exactly these themes – production, purity, duration of action, administration – accompany peptide medicine to this day.

### The 1950s: Peptides Become Readable and Producible

For a long time, it was unclear how insulin was chemically structured. In the early 1950s, the British biochemist Frederick Sanger deciphered, step by step, the sequence of amino acids in the two insulin chains (Sanger 1952, PMID 13018185). Insulin thus became the first protein molecule whose complete sequence was known. Sanger received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this in 1958. The realization that proteins have a fixed, precisely defined order of building blocks was fundamental to all of molecular biology.

At almost the same time, the American chemist Vincent du Vigneaud achieved the first chemical synthesis of a peptide hormone: oxytocin, a hormone consisting of nine amino acids that triggers labor contractions and milk flow. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1955 for this. For the first time, an endogenous hormone had been "rebuilt" in the laboratory – synthetic oxytocin is still used in obstetrics today.

In the 1960s, Robert Bruce Merrifield developed solid-phase synthesis, in which a peptide chain is extended building block by building block on a solid support (Merrifield 1969, PMID 4307033). The process could be automated and made the production of any short peptide practical for the first time. Merrifield received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1984. Without this technique, neither most modern peptide drugs nor today's gray market would exist.

### The 1960s and 1970s: Measuring and Controlling

Another obstacle was measurability. Peptide hormones occur in the blood in tiny amounts. Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson developed a measurement method using antibodies and radioactively labeled molecules, the radioimmunoassay, and with it measured insulin in human blood for the first time (Yalow 1960, PMID 13846364). Yalow received the Nobel Prize in 1977. Suddenly it was possible to track when the body releases which peptide hormones.

In the same year, Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally were honored. They had deciphered the peptide messengers of the hypothalamus – the brain region that gives commands to the pituitary gland. Entire classes of medicines emerged from these discoveries: modified forms of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) are used, for example, in prostate cancer, endometriosis, and fertility treatment. The hormone somatostatin became the model for octreotide, which is used for hormone-producing tumors and acromegaly. The growth hormone-releasing peptides, which are covered in detail in Part II, also have their roots here.

### The 1980s: Genetic Engineering Changes Everything

Until the 1980s, insulin came from the pancreases of slaughtered animals. Human growth hormone was obtained from the pituitary glands of deceased people; this period lasted from 1958 to 1985 (Ranke 2018, PMID 29546874). This method of extraction had a tragic downside: some children who had received such hormone developed Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease years later, transmitted through contaminated preparations. The episode remains a stark reminder of how important purity and origin are for biological active substances.

Genetic engineering offered a way out. Human insulin, produced in bacteria, was used in clinical studies in humans from 1980 onward and was the first medicine made using recombinant DNA technology (The 1989, PMID 2690608). Recombinant growth hormone followed a few years later. For the first time, human peptides and proteins were available in practically unlimited quantities.

In the 1990s, manufacturers went one step further: they deliberately modified insulin molecules to make them act faster or more slowly. These "insulin analogs" were a forerunner of the principle that later made the GLP-1 medications so successful: a natural model, optimized in the laboratory for clinical use.

Other peptide drugs also became established. One example is teriparatide, a fragment of parathyroid hormone. In a large study of 1,637 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, new vertebral fractures occurred in 14% of the women on placebo, but in only 5% and 4%, respectively, in the two teriparatide groups (Neer 2001, PMID 11346808). Such studies with "hard" endpoints such as bone fractures set the standard against which new peptides must also be measured.

### The Incretins: From the Gut to the Medication

The story of the GLP-1 medications begins with an observation from the 1960s: sugar that is swallowed triggers a much stronger insulin release than the same amount of sugar injected directly into the blood. The gut must therefore send signals to the pancreas during a meal. These signaling substances were called incretins.

The first known incretin was GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide). In the 1980s, a second one was discovered: glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1 for short. In 1987, a British group showed that GLP-1 rises in human blood after a meal, that – when infused in amounts similar to those after eating – it increased insulin release and lowered blood sugar in seven volunteers, and that it was more effective than GIP in doing so (Kreymann 1987, PMID 2890903).

The problem: natural GLP-1 is broken down in the blood within minutes by the enzyme DPP-4 (Deacon 1995, PMID 7883856). As a medication, it was therefore hardly usable. Two approaches were pursued: inhibitors of the degrading enzyme (the so-called gliptins, which are approved as tablets and are not peptides) and stable mimetics of GLP-1 itself.

### The Lizard Venom: Exenatide

The first stable mimetic came from an unexpected direction. In the early 1990s, the American endocrinologist John Eng studied the venom of the Gila monster (Heloderma suspectum), a venomous reptile from the southwestern USA. From it he isolated a peptide of 39 amino acids, which he named exendin-4 (Eng 1992, PMID 1313797). Exendin-4 resembles human GLP-1 and activates the same receptor, but is barely attacked by DPP-4.

Synthetic exendin-4 was given the name exenatide and, in 2005, was approved in the USA as the first medication in this class. In one of the pivotal trials involving 336 people with type 2 diabetes who were already taking metformin, the long-term blood sugar value HbA1c fell after 30 weeks by 0.78 percentage points in the higher-dose group, while on placebo it rose slightly by 0.08 percentage points. At the same time, participants on exenatide lost weight, 2.8 kg at the higher dose (DeFronzo 2005, PMID 15855572). Initially, exenatide had to be injected twice daily.

At the time, the weight loss was regarded as a welcome side effect. Few people probably suspected that precisely this effect would make the entire class famous two decades later.

### Longer-Acting: Liraglutide and Semaglutide

The Danish manufacturer Novo Nordisk took a different path. Instead of using a foreign peptide, it modified human GLP-1 so that it binds to the transport protein albumin in the blood and is therefore broken down more slowly. The result was liraglutide, which is injected once daily and was approved in the EU in 2009. A review by the developers describes how suitable combinations of fatty acid and linker were sought to maximize albumin binding without losing activity at the receptor (Knudsen 2019, PMID 31031702).

Liraglutide was later also tested at a higher dose for weight management. In the SCALE trial with 3,731 participants without diabetes, people on liraglutide lost on average 8.4 kg over 56 weeks, compared with 2.8 kg on placebo (Pi-Sunyer 2015, PMID 26132939).

The next step was marked by semaglutide: a molecule bound even more strongly to albumin that needs to be injected only once a week (Lau 2015, PMID 26308095). In the pivotal trial STEP 1 with 1,961 adults with overweight or obesity, body weight fell on average by 14.9% over 68 weeks, compared with 2.4% on placebo (Wilding 2021, PMID 33567185). This magnitude was well above anything that earlier weight-loss medications had achieved.

### From Blood Sugar to the Heart

A turning point came when, after problems with another diabetes drug, the authorities began requiring from 2008 onward that new diabetes medications demonstrate their cardiovascular safety in large studies. These studies were actually only meant to show that the drugs did not endanger the heart. In the case of the GLP-1 medications, they showed more.

In the LEADER trial with 9,340 people with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk, the composite event of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or stroke occurred in 13.0% on liraglutide and 14.9% on placebo (Marso 2016, PMID 27295427). In the SUSTAIN-6 trial with 3,297 participants, the figures were 6.6% on semaglutide and 8.9% on placebo; however, retinal complications occurred more frequently on semaglutide (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186).

In 2023 came the SELECT trial, which for the first time studied people with overweight and cardiovascular disease but without diabetes. Among 17,604 participants, the composite cardiovascular event occurred over a mean of 39.8 months in 6.5% on semaglutide and 8.0% on placebo (Lincoff 2023, PMID 37952131). This showed that a peptide medication in obesity can influence not only weight but also hard events such as heart attacks. What these percentages mean – and why one must distinguish between relative and absolute differences – is explained in the chapter "Reading Studies Correctly."

### The Tablet and the Multi-Agonists

Two further developments have shaped recent years.

**The first peptide tablet in this class.** With a special excipient, it became possible to make semaglutide available as a tablet. In the PIONEER 1 trial, oral semaglutide lowered HbA1c in people with type 2 diabetes considerably more than placebo (Aroda 2019, PMID 31186300). How this works technically is explained in the next chapter.

**Combined active substances.** Tirzepatide simultaneously activates the GLP-1 and the GIP receptor. In the SURMOUNT-1 trial with 2,539 adults with obesity, mean weight loss after 72 weeks was 15.0% to 20.9% depending on the dose, compared with 3.1% on placebo (Jastreboff 2022, PMID 35658024). Triple agonists such as retatrutide and combinations with the hormone amylin are now being tested in large studies. Part II presents them individually, and Part V takes a look at the pipeline.

It is remarkable that the incretin GIP, which in the 1980s was considered weaker than GLP-1 (Kreymann 1987, PMID 2890903), is playing a role again in combination. Research rarely proceeds in a straight line.

### The Downside of Success

The enormous success of the GLP-1 medications had side effects beyond pharmacology. Supply shortages from 2022 onward meant that people with diabetes were at times unable to obtain their medications. In the USA, pharmacies used an exemption rule to produce copycat preparations. Counterfeits appeared worldwide, some of which contained insulin instead of semaglutide. And the growing interest in "peptides" in general opened up a market for substances that have never been properly studied in humans. The chapter on the gray market covers this in detail.

### What History Teaches

From a hundred years of peptide medicine, several lessons can be drawn that are useful for evaluating the substances in this book:

1. **Purity is not a detail.** From the contaminated insulin extracts of the early days to growth hormone from cadaver pituitary glands, history shows that manufacturing quality can be a matter of life and death.
2. **Mechanism of action does not equal benefit.** That a peptide acts on a receptor does not yet mean that it benefits people. For the GLP-1 medications, large studies with thousands of participants and several years of follow-up were needed to show this.
3. **Surprises happen in both directions.** The weight loss with exenatide was initially a side note, and the heart-protective effects were discovered in safety studies. Conversely, large studies also revealed unexpected risks, such as the retinal complications in SUSTAIN-6.
4. **Good ideas take time.** More than three decades passed between the description of GLP-1 in humans in 1987 and the approval of semaglutide for weight management. Peptides that are advertised on the internet today as a "breakthrough" without a single controlled human study are, at best, at the beginning of such a path.
