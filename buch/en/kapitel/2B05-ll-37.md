## LL-37

LL-37 is an endogenous defense peptide. Skin, mucous membranes and white blood cells produce it to kill bacteria, fungi and viruses. In the peptide scene, it is offered as a "natural antibiotic" and as an agent against chronic infections, Lyme disease or "immune deficiency." When it comes to the evidence, a careful distinction must be made: there are very many studies in which LL-37 was *measured* in the blood or tissue – but only a handful in which people *received* LL-37 as an active substance.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** peptide of 37 amino acids, the first two of which are leucines (hence "LL"). The only human cathelicidin – the name of a family of defense peptides.
- **Origin:** cleaved from the precursor protein hCAP18; the corresponding gene (CAMP) is switched on by vitamin D, among other things (Gombart 2005, PMID 15985530).
- **Mechanism of action:** destroys the envelope of microbes; at the same time attracts immune cells, promotes new blood vessel formation and wound healing, and influences inflammatory responses.
- **Approval EU/USA:** no.
- **WADA:** not explicitly listed; in the author's view, falls under S0 as a non-approved substance (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level: B** – individual small to medium-sized randomized trials with local application to wounds; the largest of these missed its primary goal.

### How it is supposed to work

LL-37 belongs to the innate immune system, the first, nonspecific line of defense. The peptide is positively charged and attaches to the negatively charged envelopes of bacteria, which thereby become permeable. In addition, it acts as a signaling substance: it attracts white blood cells and stimulates skin cells to migrate – which is why it became interesting for wound healing.

In humans, LL-37 production is closely linked to vitamin D. The gene for the precursor protein carries a switch to which the vitamin D receptor binds; this switch is found in primates, but not in mice, rats or dogs (Gombart 2005, PMID 15985530). This explains why many studies give vitamin D and measure LL-37 – and why animal experiments with LL-37 are only of limited transferability to humans.

The flip side: LL-37 can also fuel inflammation. In psoriasis, LL-37 binds the body's own genetic material from disintegrated cells and makes it "visible" to certain immune cells, which then release interferon (Lande 2007, PMID 17873860). In two thirds of the people with moderate to severe psoriasis studied, T cells directed against LL-37 itself were found (Lande 2014, PMID 25470744). In rosacea, affected individuals produce unusually large amounts of LL-37 in the facial skin, and in mice, injecting these peptides triggered skin inflammation (Yamasaki 2007, PMID 17676051).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** The automated search found **91** publications classified as human studies and **20** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov. By far the largest part of these does *not* administer LL-37 as an active substance: they are vitamin D or probiotic studies that record LL-37 as a measured value, or observational studies on LL-37 levels in diseases. Only five studies in which LL-37 itself was administered can be found in PubMed; in the registry, there are two.

#### Interventional studies: LL-37 as an active substance

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venous leg ulcers (Grönberg 2014, PMID 25041740) | 34 people, first-in-human, double-blind RCT with three concentrations and placebo | 4 weeks of treatment | wound area reduced by 68% with the lowest concentration and by 50% with the middle one; highest concentration no better than placebo |
| HEAL LL-37, venous leg ulcers (Mahlapuu 2021, PMID 34687253) | 148 people, Phase 2b, double-blind RCT | 3 arms, in addition to compression | no significant difference from placebo; benefit only in post hoc subgroup analysis of large wounds |
| Diabetic foot ulcer (Miranda 2023, PMID 37480520; NCT04098562) | People with mildly infected foot ulcer in Jakarta, double-blind RCT vs. placebo cream | 4 weeks | greater increase in granulation tissue; no reduction in inflammatory markers or bacterial count |
| COVID-19, oral LL-37 from lactic acid bacteria (Zhao 2023, PMID 37605995) | 238 hospital patients, open-label RCT, single center | hospital stay | virus PCR negative after 9.8 instead of 14.0 days on average; no serious side effects |
| Melanoma, injection into tumors (NCT02225366) | 4 people, Phase 1/2 without control group | up to 8 weeks | dose finding; small number of cases, see risks |

The wound studies show the familiar pattern: the first small study was encouraging – strikingly, the lower concentrations worked, but not the highest (Grönberg 2014, PMID 25041740). The larger Phase 2b trial HEAL LL-37 with 148 people found no advantage over placebo in the overall group. A benefit in large wounds appeared only in a post hoc subgroup analysis, which the authors themselves regard as a reason for a further trial, not as proof (Mahlapuu 2021, PMID 34687253).

The Indonesian study on diabetic foot ulcers measured as its main variable the proportion of granulation tissue, i.e., fresh healing tissue – an intermediate marker, not complete wound healing. Inflammatory markers and bacterial colonization did not decrease more on LL-37 than on placebo (Miranda 2023, PMID 37480520).

The COVID-19 study did not use LL-37 peptide but genetically modified lactic acid bacteria intended to produce LL-37 in the gut. It was not blinded and ran at a single center; what was measured was the time to a negative PCR test, not disease course or mortality (Zhao 2023, PMID 37605995).

#### Observational studies: LL-37 as a measured value

These studies say nothing about whether administered LL-37 helps. They only show how the body produces LL-37 in diseases. Some examples:

- In 50 people with pulmonary tuberculosis, LL-37 levels in the blood were higher than in pneumonia due to other bacteria and in healthy people (Majewski 2017, PMID 28956425).
- In 76 children with bacterial meningitis, LL-37 in the cerebrospinal fluid rose with the bacterial load; a high ratio of LL-37 to bacteria was associated with less hearing damage (Savonius 2018, PMID 28827496).
- In 30 intensive care patients with sepsis, LL-37 levels rose after high-dose vitamin D (Quraishi 2015, PMID 26086941).

#### Indirectly: stimulating LL-37 via vitamin D

In a double-blind study from Bangladesh with 288 people with pulmonary tuberculosis, more participants were culture-negative after four weeks if they additionally received vitamin D3 and phenylbutyrate – two substances that stimulate the body's own LL-37 production (71% vs. 42.2% on placebo) (Mily 2015, PMID 26394045). This is a statement about this combination, not about LL-37 as a peptide. Vitamin D itself is not a peptide and is not dealt with separately in this book.

### Animal and laboratory studies

In the laboratory, LL-37 kills a broad range of bacteria, fungi and some viruses and promotes the migration of skin cells. A review on its role in cancer summarizes that in cell and animal experiments, LL-37 *promotes* tumor growth in ovarian, lung, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancer as well as melanoma, but *inhibits* it in colon, gastric and some blood cancers (Chen 2018, PMID 29843147). The same substance can therefore have opposite effects depending on the tissue.

For transferability, it is important to note that mice have their own cathelicidin, which only resembles human LL-37, and regulate it differently (Gombart 2005, PMID 15985530). Mouse findings on LL-37 must therefore be assessed even more cautiously than for other peptides.

### Risks and side effects

#### Documented

In the wound studies, topically applied LL-37 was well tolerated; there were no safety concerns regarding local or systemic side effects (Grönberg 2014, PMID 25041740; Mahlapuu 2021, PMID 34687253).

A case report from the melanoma study shows, however, that LL-37 in injectable form is not harmless: in a 63-year-old woman who received eight weekly injections into skin metastases, numerous wart-like skin changes and blisters on the trunk and limbs appeared around 45 days after the start, initially thought to be skin cancer. Histologically, inflammatory changes with atypical skin cells were seen; all changes regressed within two months after discontinuation (Dolkar 2018, PMID 29665030).

#### Theoretical and derived from disease research

- **Autoimmunity and skin diseases:** LL-37 is a trigger of inflammation in psoriasis and a target of misdirected T cells (Lande 2007, PMID 17873860; Lande 2014, PMID 25470744), and is involved in skin inflammation in rosacea (Yamasaki 2007, PMID 17676051). People with these or other autoimmune diseases (such as lupus) should be especially cautious – there are no studies on this.
- **Cancer:** Because LL-37 promotes the growth of some tumor types in laboratory experiments (Chen 2018, PMID 29843147), its use in people with cancer or a history of cancer cannot be assessed without studies.
- **Cell damage at high concentrations:** The peptide attacks cell envelopes; at higher concentrations it can also damage human cells. This could explain why the highest concentration did not work in the first wound study.
- **Gray market:** As with all "research peptides," purity, content and sterility are unknown. This applies particularly to injections.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** LL-37 is not approved as a medicine. Development as a wound agent by a Swedish company (under the name ropocamptide) has, to the author's knowledge, not progressed beyond Phase 2. Sale for use in humans is not permitted.
- **USA:** no FDA approval.
- **WADA:** not listed by name; in the author's view, to be classified under S0 as a non-approved substance.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed:** As of October 2026, no ongoing trial in which LL-37 is administered as an active substance is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The two entries with LL-37 as an active substance (melanoma, NCT02225366; diabetic foot ulcer, NCT04098562) are completed or have an unknown status. The remaining registry entries measure LL-37 only as a biomarker, for example in the gums or with vitamin D administration.

**Author's assessment:** LL-37 itself is unlikely to have a future as a medicine – too unstable, damaging to cells at high concentrations and associated with autoimmune risks. Research is shifting to shortened or modified derivatives and to ways of increasing the body's own production. A wound trial in the subgroup of large leg ulcers, as called for by the HEAL authors, would be the next logical step; it has not been registered.

### Conclusion

LL-37 is an important endogenous defense peptide whose levels have been measured in many studies – but as an active substance it has been given to humans only in a few small studies. Applied to wounds it was well tolerated, but the largest randomized trial with 148 people found no advantage over placebo (Mahlapuu 2021, PMID 34687253). At the same time, LL-37 plays a role as a driver of inflammation in psoriasis and rosacea, and an injection treatment triggered pronounced skin changes in one documented case. For the applications advertised online – for example against chronic infections or as an injectable immune booster – there are no human studies.
