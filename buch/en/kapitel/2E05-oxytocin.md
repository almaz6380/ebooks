## Oxytocin (Nasal Spray)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** the body's own peptide hormone of nine amino acids. Two sulfur bridges close six of them into a ring. Oxytocin is closely related to the hormone vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), which differs in only two building blocks.
- **Origin:** produced in nerve cells of the hypothalamus and released into the blood by the posterior pituitary gland; also a messenger within the brain. In 1953, oxytocin was the first peptide hormone to be synthesized in the laboratory; Vincent du Vigneaud received the 1955 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at the oxytocin receptor. In the body, it triggers labor contractions and the milk let-down reflex; in the brain, it is involved in bonding, social behavior and stress regulation.
- **Approval EU and USA (injection/infusion):** yes, for decades in obstetrics, for inducing and augmenting labor and for preventing heavy bleeding after childbirth.
- **Approval as a nasal spray:** An oxytocin nasal spray was formerly approved in several European countries to promote milk flow during breastfeeding, but is no longer on the market in many places. Oxytocin is not approved anywhere for psychological, social or sexual uses (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **WADA:** not listed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** for use as a nasal spray – hundreds of randomized studies, but mostly small and with single doses; the few large treatment trials were negative. For obstetrics (infusion, injection), level A applies; it is not the focus of this chapter.

### How it is supposed to work

#### From Labor Hormone to "Cuddle Hormone"

Oxytocin takes its name from the Greek expression for "quick birth." Its classic tasks are physical: it makes the uterus contract and squeezes milk out of the glands of the breast. Since the 1990s, a second role has come into focus. In prairie voles, which form lifelong pairs, pair bonding depends on the oxytocin system in the brain (animal experiments). In sheep and rodents, it controls mothers' caregiving behavior toward their offspring.

From 2005 onward, these animal findings gave rise to a wave of human studies that administered oxytocin as a nasal spray and measured social behavior. The media coined labels such as "cuddle hormone," "trust hormone" or "love hormone." As this chapter shows, the evidence today is far more sobering than these catchwords.

#### The Problem with the Nose

As a peptide, oxytocin is too large and too water-soluble to cross the blood-brain barrier in significant amounts. The nasal spray is supposed to find a route directly into the brain via the olfactory and trigeminal nerves in the nasal mucosa. Whether this works, and to what extent, is one of the central open questions in the field:

- **Skeptical view:** Only a tiny fraction of the sprayed amount reaches the cerebrospinal fluid, while the level in the blood rises far above natural values. Many observed effects could therefore stem from effects in the body, for example on the heart or gut, which then indirectly influence the brain (Leng 2016, PMID 26049207).
- **Findings in monkeys:** In rhesus monkeys, labeled oxytocin reached the cerebrospinal fluid after both nasal and intravenous administration; the nose was not superior (Lee 2018, PMID 28289281). In a second study, labeled oxytocin was found after nasal – but not intravenous – administration in brain regions along the olfactory and trigeminal pathways (Lee 2020, PMID 32494001).
- **Findings in humans:** One study compared nasal spray, nebulizer and infusion. The decrease in blood flow in the amygdala could be fully explained by the rise in the blood; at the same time, there were indications of direct effects via the nose in other regions (Martins 2020, PMID 32127545).
- **Measurement problems:** Oxytocin levels in the blood do not reflect levels in the brain under resting conditions (Valstad 2017, PMID 28442403). Many older studies also used measurement methods now considered unreliable (Leng 2016, PMID 26049207).

Experts who view the field positively overall now consider a direct route from the nose to the brain to be established, but emphasize that dose, spray technique and the sex of the participants strongly influence the results (Quintana 2021, PMID 32807845). For readers, this means: whether an oxytocin nasal spray reaches the brain depends on many details, and even experts disagree about the magnitude.

#### Briefly in Context: Oxytocin in Obstetrics

As an infusion or injection, oxytocin is one of the most important medicines in the world; the World Health Organization includes it on its List of Essential Medicines. It is used to induce or strengthen labor contractions and to prevent heavy bleeding after childbirth. A Cochrane review of 24 studies concluded that prophylactic oxytocin roughly halves the risk of blood loss of 500 ml or more (relative risk 0.51), although the quality of the individual studies was partly low (Salati 2019, PMID 31032882). This use belongs exclusively in the hands of midwives and physicians; dosage and monitoring are governed by the prescribing information. It plays no role in assessing the nasal spray for psychological questions.

### Human studies

**Size of the evidence base (search October 2026):** The automated search for this book (term "intranasal oxytocin" in title or abstract) found **349** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **263** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov, of which around 20 are still recruiting. In addition, there are at least 15 meta-analyses and systematic reviews. This makes oxytocin the most frequently studied peptide in humans in this part of the book. However, the vast majority of studies gave only a single dose to mostly 20 to 60 young, healthy men and measured short-term changes in laboratory tests or in the brain scanner.

This chapter sorts the studies into four groups: basic studies in healthy people followed by the replication crisis, autism as the most important treatment field, other psychiatric disorders, and finally metabolism, pain and sexuality.

#### Social Behavior in Healthy People: Trust, Stress, Emotions

**Trust.** The study that founded the field appeared in Nature in 2005: men who had sniffed oxytocin entrusted more money to a stranger in an economic game (Kosfeld 2005, PMID 15931222). In this "trust game," an investor decides how much money to hand over to a trustee; the amount is multiplied, and the trustee decides how much to return. Oxytocin did not increase willingness to take risks in general, but specifically the willingness to take social risks.

**Stress.** In a study with 37 healthy men, oxytocin dampened anxiety during a standardized stress test (public speaking and mental arithmetic in front of an audience). The lowest levels of the stress hormone cortisol were found in men who received oxytocin and were additionally supported by their best friend (Heinrichs 2003, PMID 14675803).

**Recognizing emotions.** A meta-analysis of 33 studies found that a single dose slightly improved the recognition of basic emotions, especially fear, in healthy people. In people with mental illness, by contrast, there was no effect (Leppanen 2017, PMID 28467893).

Hundreds of other studies reported effects on gaze behavior, empathy, memory for faces, group affiliation, lying, generosity, jealousy and much more. Many of these findings occurred only in subgroups, for example only in men, only in anxious people or only with certain gene variants of the oxytocin receptor.

#### The Replication Crisis

From around 2015, doubts grew. The term replication crisis describes the problem that many published findings in psychology and medicine are not confirmed when other researchers repeat them. Oxytocin became a prime example.

| Study | Type | Key message |
|---|---|---|
| Kosfeld 2005, PMID 15931222 | original study, trust game | Oxytocin increases trust |
| Nave 2015, PMID 26581735 | critical review | The trust effect could not be reliably replicated |
| Lane 2015, PMID 26368396 | two replication studies (95 and 61 people) | Large effect in a different trust test not confirmed |
| Walum 2016, PMID 26210057 | statistical analysis of the field | Studies mostly too small; most published findings probably not real effects |
| Mierop 2020, PMID 32633663 | systematic review | Replication attempts for interactions mostly unsuccessful; statistical power critically low |
| Declerck 2020, PMID 32514040 | preregistered replication study | No effect on trust in the original condition |
| Vogt 2026, PMID 42561025 | preregistered study, 359 men with low baseline trust | Trust increased by around 15% |

A 2015 review concluded that the totality of the data does not show that human trust is reliably associated with or caused by oxytocin (Nave 2015, PMID 26581735). A statistical analysis of all nasal spray studies went further: the studies were generally too small to find realistic effects, and in all likelihood most published findings did not represent real effects (Walum 2016, PMID 26210057).

The most important test was a large, preregistered replication of the trust study with a statistical power of over 95%. In the condition corresponding to the original study, oxytocin showed no effect. A post hoc analysis suggested an effect only in people with low baseline trust (Declerck 2020, PMID 32514040). Precisely this hypothesis was tested in 2026 by another preregistered study with 359 men with low baseline trust, which found an increase in trust of about 15% (Vogt 2026, PMID 42561025).

**What this means:** The original, broad effect "oxytocin makes people more trusting" has not been confirmed. There may be a smaller effect in a particular subgroup and under particular conditions. That is scientifically interesting but far from any practical benefit. These experiences have changed the field: studies today are more often preregistered, are larger and also report negative results. An online platform presented in 2026 is intended to evaluate the evidence on an ongoing basis (Iversen 2026, PMID 41389446).

#### Autism

Autism spectrum disorders are characterized by difficulties in social communication and by restricted, repetitive behaviors. There are no medications that change these core features. Oxytocin was therefore long considered a great hope, and in some countries it was already being prescribed to children with autism outside of studies (Sikich 2021, PMID 34644471).

**Early studies.** A 2014 systematic review found seven randomized studies with a combined total of 101 people with autism, almost all male. The studies were small (median 15 participants) and had a moderate to high risk of bias. Almost all reported at least one positive result, mostly in gaze behavior or emotion recognition (Preti 2014, PMID 24679173).

**Treatment studies over weeks.** These were followed by studies in which children and adults received oxytocin over several weeks:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anagnostou 2012, PMID 23216716 | 19 adults, RCT | 6 weeks | Primary endpoints not met; improvement in secondary endpoints |
| Guastella 2015, PMID 25087908 | 50 male adolescents (12–18 years), RCT | 8 weeks | No benefit on primary or secondary endpoints |
| Yatawara 2016, PMID 26503762 | 31 young children, crossover study vs. placebo | 5 weeks each | Social responsiveness improved according to parents |
| Parker 2017, PMID 28696286 | 32 children (6–12 years), RCT | 4 weeks | Social abilities improved when baseline blood levels were taken into account; low baseline level predicted response |
| SOARS-B (Sikich 2021), PMID 34644471 | 290 children and adolescents (3–17 years), RCT | 24 weeks | No difference: −3.7 vs. −3.5 points in social withdrawal |
| Guastella 2023, PMID 36302965 | 87 children (3–12 years), RCT after placebo lead-in | 12 weeks | No effect overall; indication of an effect in 3- to 5-year-olds |

**The SOARS-B trial.** By far the largest and most meaningful study is SOARS-B (Study of Oxytocin in Autism to Improve Reciprocal Social Behaviors), funded by the US health authorities and published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2021 (Sikich 2021, PMID 34644471; NCT01944046). 290 children and adolescents between 3 and 17 years of age received oxytocin or a placebo nasal spray daily for 24 weeks. The primary endpoint was a scale for social withdrawal (ABC-mSW, 0 to 39 points).

The result was clearly negative: the score fell by 3.7 points under oxytocin and by 3.5 points under placebo. The difference of 0.2 points was neither statistically nor clinically significant. The secondary endpoints – two further measures of social function and an intelligence test – also did not differ. Side effects were equally frequent in both groups.

**The Australian study in young children.** The group led by Adam Guastella had previously found a positive result in young children (Yatawara 2016, PMID 26503762). A larger follow-up study with 87 children added a three-week placebo lead-in phase to filter out the strong placebo effect. Overall, oxytocin showed no effect on social responsiveness. Only in the subgroup of 3- to 5-year-olds was there a hint of improvement according to parent ratings; clinician ratings and all other measures showed no benefit (Guastella 2023, PMID 36302965). Notably, in the 2015 adolescent study, parents who believed their child was receiving oxytocin reported greater improvements – regardless of what the child actually received (Guastella 2015, PMID 25087908).

**Biological effects without clinical benefit.** A study with 79 children found higher oxytocin levels in saliva and changes in the epigenetic marking of the receptor gene after four weeks of oxytocin (Moerkerke 2024, PMID 38167302). In the brain scanner, by contrast, the same group showed no significant treatment effects on face processing (Moerkerke 2024, PMID 39709442). Such biological signals are scientifically interesting but do not demonstrate improvement in everyday life.

**Meta-analyses.** The summaries reach different conclusions depending on which studies they include:

- A meta-analysis of 17 randomized studies with 466 people with developmental disorders found no effect on emotion recognition and a small effect on understanding the thoughts of others (Keech 2018, PMID 29032324).
- A meta-analysis of 28 studies with 726 people with autism, including uncontrolled ones, reached a more positive verdict on social function (Huang 2021, PMID 33400920). The inclusion of uncontrolled studies makes this result susceptible to bias.
- The largest meta-analysis to date across all mental disorders (42 double-blind studies, 1,922 people) found no significant effect overall. After excluding two outliers, the effect size was close to zero (Bonnieux 2026, PMID 42134427).

**Interim conclusion on autism:** After the large SOARS-B trial and the Australian follow-up study, there is no robust evidence that oxytocin nasal spray improves the social behavior of children and adolescents with autism in everyday life. Researchers are discussing whether certain subgroups – very young children, children with low oxytocin levels – might benefit, and propose a personalized approach (Boulton 2026, PMID 42144048). Until this is confirmed in dedicated studies, it remains a hypothesis.

#### Other Mental Disorders

**Schizophrenia.** In a twelve-week study with 68 people, oxytocin did not improve social cognition (Jarskog 2017, PMID 28094169). A meta-analysis of nine studies found no consistent effect on so-called negative symptoms such as lack of drive and social withdrawal (Sabe 2021, PMID 33890987). The large 2026 meta-analysis saw a small, just significant effect in this group (effect size 0.12) and found that studies with more women tended to show larger effects (Bonnieux 2026, PMID 42134427).

**Alcohol.** In a Norwegian study with 40 people undergoing alcohol withdrawal, oxytocin did not significantly reduce the need for sedatives (Melby 2019, PMID 30784955). A meta-analysis of six randomized studies on alcohol dependence found no effect; a Bayesian analysis even moderately favored the absence of an effect (Santos 2026, PMID 42335670).

**Combination with psychotherapy.** A newer idea is to give oxytocin not on its own but as an enhancer of psychotherapy. A review of 13 studies with 518 participants found indications of fewer depressive symptoms and a better therapeutic relationship, but no effect on psychiatric symptoms overall or on social functioning; only four studies could be pooled (Pérez-Arqueros 2025, PMID 39366103).

**After childbirth.** In 45 mothers with (partly subthreshold) postpartum depression, a single dose increased positive mood and positive engagement with the child, but not sensitivity in interacting with the child (Riem 2025, PMID 40614394).

#### Metabolism, Weight and Pain

**Obesity.** Because oxytocin reduces food intake and increases energy expenditure in animals, it was tested as a weight-loss drug. A meta-analysis found that a single dose reduced the amount eaten at a test meal in people without mental illness (Chen 2021, PMID 34600216). The decisive test was a study with 61 adults with obesity who sprayed oxytocin or placebo four times a day for eight weeks. Weight did not change: +0.20 kg under oxytocin, +0.26 kg under placebo. Body fat, liver fat and energy expenditure also remained unchanged; only at a test meal did participants eat less (Plessow 2024, PMID 38815173). A small pilot study in 21 older people with overweight and muscle loss found a gain in muscle mass of 2.25 kg (Espinoza 2021, PMID 34029521); this needs to be confirmed in larger studies.

**Prader-Willi syndrome.** In this rare genetic disorder with insatiable hunger and reduced oxytocin in the hypothalamus, expectations were particularly high. A meta-analysis found no effect on food cravings or weight (Shalma 2023, PMID 36774885). In a study with 23 children, food cravings and repetitive behavior even tended to improve more under placebo (Hollander 2021, PMID 33190843).

**Pain.** A meta-analysis of 15 studies found no significant effect on pain intensity or pain unpleasantness (Lopes 2023, PMID 36463692).

#### Sexuality

Oxytocin is released during orgasm and is promoted on the internet as a means to a more intense love life. In a laboratory study with ten men, a nasal spray markedly increased the oxytocin level in the blood but did not change desire, arousal, orgasm or the refractory period according to the questionnaires (Burri 2008, PMID 18375074). A meta-analysis of medications for female sexual dysfunction, which also included oxytocin studies, found that around two-thirds of the improvement also occurred under placebo (Weinberger 2018, PMID 29995725). There is no robust evidence of an effect on sexuality.

#### Oxytocin Deficiency as a Disease

A new and medically plausible field is genuine oxytocin deficiency. People whose hypothalamus or pituitary gland has been damaged, for example by a tumor or surgery, often have vasopressin deficiency (formerly central diabetes insipidus). A Swiss study used the drug MDMA, which strongly releases oxytocin in healthy people, as a provocation test. In 15 healthy people, oxytocin rose sharply; in 15 patients, it barely rose; the area under the curve was 82% larger in healthy people, and the patients hardly felt the typical social and anxiolytic effects (Atila 2023, PMID 37192642). This suggests that many of these people also have an oxytocin deficiency. Whether replacement via nasal spray relieves their psychological symptoms is being tested in an ongoing study (NCT06036004). Kisspeptin is also being investigated as a possible test for oxytocin deficiency (Galbiati 2025, PMID 39965102; chapter 2E04).

### Animal and laboratory studies

The enthusiasm for oxytocin rests on convincing animal findings: in prairie voles, the oxytocin system is necessary for pair bonding, and in rodents and sheep for maternal behavior. In mice in an autism model, an agent that stimulates the oxytocin system improved measures of social behavior (Minakova 2019, PMID 30629642). In rhesus monkeys, labeled oxytocin reached certain brain regions via the nose (Lee 2020, PMID 32494001).

However, these findings can only be transferred to a limited extent: the distribution of oxytocin receptors in the brain differs greatly between species, even between closely related vole species. Many animal experiments administered oxytocin directly into the brain, which is not possible in humans.

### Risks and side effects

#### Established: Nasal Spray in Studies

In controlled studies, oxytocin nasal spray is considered well tolerated. A systematic review of 38 randomized studies with 1,529 participants found no side effects with short-term use that occurred more frequently than under placebo. Participants were also unable to tell whether they had received oxytocin or placebo (MacDonald 2011, PMID 21429671). In SOARS-B, side effects over 24 weeks were equally frequent in both groups (Sikich 2021, PMID 34644471). In 95 older men, no changes in blood pressure, urine or blood values occurred over four weeks (Rung 2021, PMID 33982141). In pediatric studies, thirst, increased urination and constipation were reported (Yatawara 2016, PMID 26503762).

#### Possible and Worth Noting

- **Fluid balance:** Oxytocin resembles vasopressin and in high doses can cause the body to retain water. In obstetrics, water intoxication with sodium deficiency is a known, rare complication of high-dose infusions. With the nasal spray, one case of increased intracranial pressure with a simultaneously very high fluid intake has been described (Mayer-Hubner 1996, PMID 8596357).
- **Circulation:** In twelve healthy men, a nasal spray increased the activity of the nerves that constrict blood vessels and slightly raised diastolic blood pressure (Meusel 2021, PMID 33296278).
- **Pregnancy:** Because oxytocin triggers labor contractions, use during pregnancy outside medical supervision is dangerous.
- **Long-term use:** Data covering more than six months are lacking, especially in children.
- **Social effects:** Individual laboratory studies reported that under certain conditions oxytocin can also increase envy, distrust of strangers or favoritism toward one's own group. These findings are just as uncertain as the positive ones.

#### Gray Market

Oxytocin nasal sprays are sold on the internet as "love" or "trust" sprays. The content and strength of such products have not been independently tested. There is no evidence of efficacy for any of these products.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** Oxytocin for infusion and injection is approved as a prescription medicine for obstetrics. There is no approved nasal spray for psychological or social uses; formerly approved sprays for promoting milk flow are no longer available in many places. A prescription for the treatment of autism or other conditions would be an individual off-label treatment attempt with corresponding obligations regarding informed consent and liability.
- **USA:** Oxytocin injection (Pitocin and generics) approved for obstetrics; no approved nasal spray. Compounding pharmacies prepare nasal sprays on medical prescription; their quality is not tested in the same way as that of finished medicines.
- **WADA:** not listed.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established (registered studies):** Among the ongoing studies are some larger ones that could answer individual open questions:

- **Chronic pain:** A Canadian phase 2/3 trial with 336 participants (NCT04903002) has been completed; results are pending.
- **Psychotherapy plus oxytocin:** A study by the US Department of Veterans Affairs with 240 people is testing oxytocin as an adjunct to couples therapy in post-traumatic stress disorder (NCT06194851, until 2028). In Germany, studies are testing oxytocin as an adjunct to psychosocial treatment in schizophrenia (NCT06881810 with 98 people; NCT07265180 at the Charité with 120 people and a dose comparison).
- **Oxytocin deficiency:** A Swiss study with 112 people is testing oxytocin replacement in vasopressin deficiency (NCT06036004).
- **Autism and bone:** A study with 96 children is investigating effects on bone health (NCT05754073).
- **Eating disorders:** a phase 2 trial in binge eating (NCT05664516).
- **Industry development:** One company has tested a high-dose oxytocin nasal spray (TNX-1900) in chronic migraine (NCT05679908, 88 people, completed) and is planning a study on pain treatment after pituitary surgery (NCT07730294).

**Author's assessment:** The story of oxytocin nasal spray is a lesson in the limits of small studies. After two decades of research, there is not a single application in which a benefit has been confirmed in large, well-conducted studies. The most promising directions today are more narrowly defined: oxytocin as an adjunct to psychotherapy rather than as a medication on its own, as replacement in proven deficiency, and targeted at subgroups defined in advance. Approval for autism or social anxiety appears unlikely in the coming years.

### Conclusion

As an infusion, oxytocin is an indispensable medicine in obstetrics; as a nasal spray, by contrast, it is a research tool without proven therapeutic benefit. The famous finding that oxytocin increases trust did not withstand replication studies; at most, a small effect is suggested in people with low baseline trust. The largest autism study, SOARS-B with 290 children and adolescents, found no difference from placebo after 24 weeks, and results were also negative in obesity, alcohol dependence, Prader-Willi syndrome and pain. In studies, the spray was well tolerated. Anyone buying a "love" or "trust" spray today is purchasing a promise that research does not keep.
