## Mecasermin (IGF-1)

Mecasermin is genetically engineered human IGF-1 – that is, the messenger through which growth hormone exerts much of its effect. As a medicine (Increlex), it is approved for a very rare group of children: children whose bodies produce enough growth hormone but do not respond to it and therefore produce hardly any IGF-1. Mecasermin is thus a rare example of an approved peptide in this group – and at the same time the drug in which the question of hypoglycemia and cancer risk from IGF-1 arises most clearly.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** recombinant (genetically engineered in bacteria) human IGF-1 of 70 amino acids, identical to the endogenous hormone.
- **Origin:** IGF-1 was described as "somatomedin" in the 1970s; mecasermin was developed by Tercica and marketed by Ipsen, now by another marketing authorization holder (as of 2026, please check).
- **Mechanism of action:** binds to the IGF-1 receptor and directly replaces the missing IGF-1; promotes growth, is anabolic and – at higher doses – lowers blood glucose in a manner similar to insulin.
- **EU approval:** yes (Increlex) for the long-term treatment of growth failure in children and adolescents with severe primary IGF-1 deficiency. **US approval:** yes (Increlex), same indication.
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 (IGF-1 and its analogs as growth factors).
- **Evidence level: A** – approved in the EU and USA. Limitation: because of the rarity of the disease, approval is based on open-label long-term studies and registries, not on large randomized trials. Randomized trials exist for other uses, such as ALS (negative).

### How it is supposed to work

Growth hormone from the pituitary gland acts mainly on the liver, which then produces IGF-1. IGF-1 in turn makes bones grow in length and promotes the building of muscle and other tissue. In some children this chain is interrupted: their growth hormone receptor is nonfunctional due to a genetic variant, or signal transmission is disrupted. The classic example is Laron syndrome. These children have plenty of growth hormone but hardly any IGF-1, and growth hormone injections do not help them. Without treatment, they often reach only 120 to 130 cm as adults (Saavedra 2026, PMID 42341624).

Mecasermin bypasses the defective step and supplies IGF-1 directly. Because IGF-1 resembles insulin, it can lower blood glucose – the most important side effect.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **21** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and **37** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov; some of these registry entries concern other topics and appear only because of the search term. In addition, there are three systematic reviews. A targeted follow-up search yielded the important long-term studies and registry analyses for the approved indication.

#### Severe primary IGF-1 deficiency in children

The basis for approval is a mostly open-label study running for up to twelve years with 76 children with IGF-1 deficiency due to growth hormone resistance. Growth velocity rose from an average of 2.8 cm per year before treatment to 8.0 cm in the first year of treatment; in subsequent years it declined but remained above baseline for up to eight years. The most common side effect was hypoglycemia (in 49% of the children, although it was also observed before the start of therapy), followed by changes in fatty tissue at the injection site (32%) and enlargement of the tonsils and adenoids (22%) (Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294).

Since 2008, a European, now global, registry has been collecting real-world data (NCT00903110). In 138 previously untreated prepubertal children, height improved in the first year; children with Laron syndrome responded somewhat better, and among the others 56% were considered responders. 65.3% of the children had at least one adverse effect, most commonly hypoglycemia (Bang 2021, PMID 33434161). 102 children and adolescents who had already reached near-final height gained an average of 0.9 standard deviations in height, and 1.4 when treatment started before puberty. Almost half of these early-treated children reached a height in the normal range, but only 10.5% of those with Laron syndrome (Ramon-Krauel 2026, PMID 40626687). Severe hypoglycemia was rare at 0.01 events per patient per treatment year; children with Laron syndrome or previous hypoglycemia were particularly at risk (Bang 2023, PMID 37579214).

#### Other uses

- **ALS:** In a phase 3 trial with 330 people with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, IGF-1 made no difference compared with placebo over two years – neither in muscle strength nor in survival without tracheostomy (Sorenson 2008, PMID 19029516).
- **Duchenne muscular dystrophy:** In 17 treated boys compared with 21 controls, the boys grew faster over six months (6.5 vs. 3.3 cm per year), but walking distance in the 6-minute test did not change (Rutter 2020, PMID 32108355).
- **Rett syndrome:** A systematic review describes indications of preserved social and cognitive abilities, but inconsistent results, and calls for larger studies (Ribeiro 2025, PMID 41174329).
- **Preterm infants:** IGF-1 together with its binding protein IGFBP-3 did not prevent retinopathy of prematurity in 121 extremely preterm infants, but reduced severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia (21.3% vs. 44.9%). Fatal serious events occurred in 19.7% of treated and 11.7% of control infants (Ley 2019, PMID 30471715). A follow-up trial on lung development is ongoing (NCT03253263).
- **Anorexia:** In 90 women with anorexia nervosa, six months of IGF-1 treatment followed by a bone medication increased spinal bone density more than placebo (Haines 2021, PMID 34355814).
- **Adults with Laron syndrome:** In ten adults, eight weeks of IGF-1 reduced the fat fraction and increased lean mass (Mauras 2000, PMID 10999782).
- **Athletes:** In a randomized, double-blind study with 56 recreational athletes over 28 days, blood lipids and insulin levels changed on IGF-1/IGFBP-3 (Guha 2021, PMID 33249593). This study provides no performance data.

### Animal and laboratory studies

The effects of IGF-1 on growth, muscle and metabolism have been studied very extensively in animal and cell studies and form the basis of the therapy. Important for the safety question are cell experiments with blood serum from people with Laron syndrome: cells treated with this IGF-1-poor serum and then exposed to oxidative stress had fewer DNA breaks, and damaged cells died more often (Guevara-Aguirre 2011, PMID 21325617). When IGF-1 was added to the serum, these differences disappeared (Guevara-Aguirre 2015, PMID 25316432). IGF-1 thus tends to keep even damaged cells alive – a possible mechanism for a cancer risk.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:**

- **Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar)** – the most common and most important side effect, especially in young children, in Laron syndrome and after previous hypoglycemia (Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294; Bang 2023, PMID 37579214). The prescribing information therefore requires a close temporal link with meals; it governs the details itself.
- **Enlargement of the tonsils and adenoids** (22%) and changes in fatty tissue at the injection site (32%) (Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294).
- In the preterm infant trial, numerically more fatal serious events occurred on IGF-1/IGFBP-3 than on standard care (Ley 2019, PMID 30471715); the trial was not designed to evaluate this difference.

**Cancer risk – what is established and what is not:**

IGF-1 is a growth and survival signal for cells. The concern that more IGF-1 increases cancer risk is therefore biologically plausible and is supported by several types of findings:

- *Observational studies:* People with higher IGF-1 levels in the blood are somewhat more likely to develop prostate cancer and premenopausal breast cancer (Renehan 2004, PMID 15110491). In the UK Biobank, each increase of 5 nmol/L was associated with an 11% higher breast cancer risk (Murphy 2020, PMID 32169310); similar associations are seen for colorectal cancer (Murphy 2020, PMID 31884074) and prostate cancer (Watts 2023, PMID 35726641).
- *Genetic analyses (Mendelian randomization):* People who have genetically determined higher IGF-1 levels are also somewhat more likely to develop breast and colorectal cancer – an indication that the association could be causal (Murphy 2020, PMID 32169310; Murphy 2020, PMID 31884074).
- *The counterpart:* In an Ecuadorian group with Laron syndrome, that is, lifelong IGF-1 deficiency, only a single, nonfatal cancer occurred over 22 years of observation, compared with 17% in unaffected relatives (Guevara-Aguirre 2011, PMID 21325617).

The magnitude is important: the measured risk increases with naturally higher IGF-1 are moderate (depending on the comparison and type of cancer, mostly between about 5 and 65% in relative terms). For the children for whom mecasermin is approved, a missing hormone is being replaced, not a normal level being raised. Nevertheless, after market launch, cases of benign and malignant neoplasms were reported in treated children; the prescribing information in the EU and USA was subsequently supplemented with corresponding warnings, and mecasermin must not be used in active or suspected cancer (as of 2026, see the current prescribing information for details). Whether there is a causal relationship cannot be determined from individual reports; the long-term registry (NCT00903110) is intended to provide data on this.

For adults who use IGF-1 without a deficiency to enhance performance or for "rejuvenation," these considerations should be taken much more seriously: here a normal level would be permanently raised – exactly the situation that is associated with a higher risk in the observational studies. The topic is discussed in detail in Part IV.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** approved (Increlex) exclusively for children and adolescents with severe primary IGF-1 deficiency; prescription only, treatment by physicians experienced in pediatric endocrinology according to the prescribing information. The EU approval is tied to data collection in the long-term registry (Saavedra 2026, PMID 42341624).
- **USA:** approved (Increlex) for the same indication.
- **Other uses** (adults, sport, anti-aging) are not approved.
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2). For detecting IGF-1 misuse, marker methods similar to those of the growth hormone test are being developed (Guha 2013, PMID 23934394).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

*Established:* The global Increlex registry (NCT00903110) is scheduled to run until the end of 2027 according to its entry. A phase 2 trial of IGF-1/IGFBP-3 for the prevention of bronchopulmonary dysplasia in extremely preterm infants (NCT03253263, 295 infants) is active but no longer recruiting. *Author's assessment:* New approvals for adults are not in sight; the significance of mecasermin will remain limited to rare growth disorders and possibly neonatal medicine for preterm infants.

### Conclusion

Mecasermin replaces the missing IGF-1 in children with severe primary IGF-1 deficiency and markedly improves their growth, especially when treatment starts early. The most common side effect is hypoglycemia; after reports of neoplasms, the warnings were strengthened. In other uses, such as ALS, IGF-1 brought no benefit. For healthy people, its use is neither supported by evidence nor justifiable, because persistently elevated IGF-1 is associated with a somewhat higher cancer risk in large observational and genetic studies.
