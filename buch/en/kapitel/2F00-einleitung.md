# Part II · F – Collagen, Nutrition and Cosmetics

Most people encounter the peptides in this group not in a doctor's office but in the supermarket, the drugstore and in advertising: as a collagen drink, a blood-pressure yogurt, a sports capsule or an "anti-aging peptide" in a face cream. They are freely available, considered harmless and advertised with studies. That is precisely why it is worth taking a close look at what these studies show.

### What Unites the Group

All six substances are either fragments of proteins that occur in the body or in food (collagen peptides, lactotripeptides from milk, carnosine, GHK-Cu) or are deliberately modeled on them (Matrixyl, Argireline). None of them is approved as a medicine. They are therefore not tested according to the strict standards that apply to medicines, but according to the rules for foods or cosmetics.

This gives rise to common patterns that recur in all chapters:

- **Many small studies, few large ones.** Typical are 20 to 150 participants over 4 to 24 weeks. Hard endpoints such as bone fractures, heart attacks or joint replacement have never been studied.
- **Manufacturer funding.** A large proportion of the studies were paid for by raw material or product manufacturers. For collagen, a meta-analysis shows that the benefit for the skin disappears when only independently funded studies are considered (Myung 2025, PMID 40324552).
- **The problem of the site of action.** A peptide has to get to where it is supposed to act. Swallowed peptides are largely digested; peptides in creams penetrate the skin barrier only to a small extent. In every chapter, the central question is therefore whether the mechanism shown in the laboratory comes into play in the body at all.
- **Combination products.** Many products contain several active ingredients. A positive study result can then not be attributed to the individual peptide.

### Food, Cosmetics, Medicines – Three Legal Worlds

Whether a substance counts as a food, a cosmetic or a medicine is determined not by its chemistry but by its intended purpose and presentation. Part I explains the legal framework in detail; for this group, three points are important:

- **Foods and food supplements** (collagen peptides, lactotripeptides, carnosine) require no approval and no proof of efficacy in the EU. In return, what they may be advertised with is strictly regulated. Under the Health Claims Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006), health claims are only permitted if they have been assessed by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and authorized by the European Commission. **For collagen, collagen peptides, lactotripeptides and carnosine, there is not a single authorized health claim.** EFSA has issued negative opinions on the claims applied for – for example on joints for collagen or on blood pressure for lactotripeptides. If a collagen package nevertheless advertises skin or bone benefits, the legally permissible claim usually refers to added vitamin C, for which the claim "contributes to normal collagen formation" is authorized.
- **Cosmetics** (GHK-Cu, Matrixyl, Argireline) are subject to the EU Cosmetics Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009). They may only be applied externally, must be safe and may not claim any medicinal effect. Advertising claims must be substantiated, but there is no official efficacy assessment before sale.
- **Medicines** must demonstrate their efficacy and safety in studies and be approved. If a cosmetic peptide is injected – as happens with GHK-Cu and Argireline on the gray market – it leaves the world of cosmetics: it becomes an unapproved medicine, without any studies or quality controls existing for this use.

Similar principles apply in the USA; there, however, food supplements may be advertised with general "structure/function claims," provided they carry a notice that the FDA has not evaluated them (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Overview

| Peptide | Evidence | Approval | In brief |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collagen peptides | B | Food, no health claim | Small effects on joint pain; skin benefit not shown in independent studies |
| Lactotripeptides (VPP/IPP) | B | Food, blood pressure claim rejected | Blood pressure reduction of about 1–4 mmHg, barely detectable in Europe |
| Carnosine | B | Food supplement, no health claim | Indications for blood sugar; in sport, the precursor beta-alanine is better supported |
| GHK-Cu | C | Cosmetic; injection not approved | One old wound study; hardly any skin data; injection untested |
| Palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl) | B | Cosmetic | One split-face study from 2005; skin penetration unclear |
| Acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline) | B | Cosmetic | "Botox in a tube" not demonstrated; barely penetrates the skin |

In this group, level B explicitly does not mean that an effect is established, only that randomized studies exist.

### Reading Guide

The chapter on **collagen peptides** is the most detailed, because there are the most studies on them and because they illustrate well how meta-analyses, manufacturer funding and the placebo effect interact. If you read only one chapter in this group, choose this one. The chapter on **lactotripeptides** is a lesson in how a convincing laboratory mechanism can shrink in larger studies. The three **cosmetic peptides** are shorter; they share the same core question – whether an active ingredient from a cream even reaches the place where it is supposed to act. The risks of gray-market injections are covered in detail in Part IV.
