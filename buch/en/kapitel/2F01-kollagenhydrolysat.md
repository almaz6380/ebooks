## Collagen Peptides (Collagen Hydrolysate)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** mixture of peptides of varying length, obtained by enzymatic cleavage (hydrolysis) of collagen or gelatin. Typical products contain fragments with an average molecular mass of about 2,000 to 5,000 daltons. The amino acids glycine, proline and hydroxyproline are characteristic; the essential amino acid tryptophan is absent.
- **Origin:** skin, bones or scales of cattle, pigs, chickens and fish – by-products of meat and fish processing. Many studies use proprietary branded raw materials (e.g. Verisol, Fortigel, Bodybalance, Tendoforte) that come from a small number of manufacturers.
- **Mechanism of action:** supply of building blocks for the body's own connective tissue; it is also discussed that certain small peptides (above all prolyl-hydroxyproline) act as signaling substances on connective tissue cells.
- **Distinction:** *Undenatured* type II collagen (e.g. UC-II) is a different product with a different mechanism of action and is discussed separately below.
- **Approval EU:** no approval as a medicine; marketable as a food or food supplement. For collagen itself, there is **no** authorized health claim.
- **Approval USA:** no approval as a drug; sold as a dietary supplement.
- **WADA:** not listed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – numerous randomized, placebo-controlled studies, but mostly small, short and funded by manufacturers.

### How It Is Supposed to Work

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body. It forms the framework of skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone. Its basic unit is a triple helix of three protein chains in which a pattern repeats constantly: glycine, then any amino acid, then frequently proline or hydroxyproline. With age, collagen production in the skin declines and the collagen network becomes more fragile – visible as wrinkles and decreasing firmness.

Advertising often suggests a simple logic: if you eat collagen, you build collagen. That is not how digestion works. Proteins are largely broken down in the stomach and small intestine into individual amino acids and very small peptides; the body reassembles its proteins from these according to its own needs. Dietary collagen is therefore not "incorporated" into the skin as collagen.

There are, however, two serious hypotheses as to why collagen peptides might still have an effect:

1. **Signaling peptides.** Di- and tripeptides containing hydroxyproline are unusually stable against digestive enzymes. After drinking gelatin hydrolysate, the concentration of peptide-bound hydroxyproline in the blood of volunteers rose markedly, peaking after one to two hours; the main component was the dipeptide prolyl-hydroxyproline (Iwai 2005, PMID 16076145). Similar results were seen for a preparation with a high proportion of tripeptides (Yamamoto 2016, PMID 26934933). In cell culture and animal experiments, such peptides stimulate connective tissue cells (fibroblasts) to grow and to produce matrix building blocks. Whether the amounts reached in the blood in humans are sufficient to trigger a measurable effect in skin or cartilage has not been reliably demonstrated.
2. **Supply of building material.** Collagen peptides supply large amounts of glycine, proline and hydroxyproline. In a small study with eight men, the blood marker for new collagen formation doubled when they had taken 15 g of vitamin C-enriched gelatin one hour before a short bout of jumping exercise (Shaw 2017, PMID 27852613). This supports the idea of timing collagen peptides with exercise, but it is a measurement in the blood, not proof of stronger tendons.

Important for context: collagen is an incomplete protein source. Because it lacks tryptophan, a gelatin-based peptide mixture is even used deliberately in research to lower the tryptophan level in the blood in the short term (Stenbæk 2016, PMID 26655163). For muscle building, collagen is therefore inferior to other proteins – more on this below.

### Human Studies

The body of evidence is extensive but inconsistent. The PubMed search evaluated for this book (collagen hydrolysate or collagen peptides in combination with intake or supplementation, search date October 2026) yielded 101 human studies. On ClinicalTrials.gov, the search "collagen peptides" found 237 registry entries; a considerable proportion of these, however, concern other topics (such as collagen implants in dentistry or the cancer drug endostatin, a collagen fragment). In addition, there are at least a dozen meta-analyses.

Three features characterize almost all studies and should be kept in mind when reading:

- **Small sample sizes and short duration:** mostly 40 to 150 participants over 8 to 24 weeks.
- **Manufacturer funding:** A large proportion of the studies were paid for by the raw material or product manufacturers or co-authored by their employees. Proprietary peptide mixtures are frequently tested.
- **Combination products:** Many "collagen drinks" additionally contain vitamin C, zinc, biotin, hyaluronic acid or plant extracts. A positive result can then not be attributed to collagen alone.

#### Skin: Moisture, Elasticity, Wrinkles

The skin studies are the most numerous. Measurements are usually made with devices: corneometer (moisture of the stratum corneum), cutometer (elasticity), skin replicas or 3D cameras (wrinkle depth), ultrasound (density of the dermis).

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proksch 2014a, PMID 23949208 | 69 women (35–55 yrs), 2.5 g or 5 g vs. placebo | 8 weeks | Elasticity significantly better than placebo in both dose groups; moisture not significant |
| Proksch 2014b, PMID 24401291 | 114 women (45–65 yrs), 2.5 g Verisol vs. placebo | 8 weeks | Eye wrinkle volume −20% vs. placebo; procollagen I in skin samples +65% |
| Kim 2018, PMID 29949889 | 64 people, 1 g low-molecular-weight peptide vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Moisture and three wrinkle parameters better than placebo; elasticity partly |
| Bolke 2019, PMID 31627309 | 72 women aged 35+, drinking ampoule with 2.5 g peptides plus vitamins, zinc, acerola vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Moisture, elasticity, roughness and density better than placebo (combination product) |
| Kim 2022, PMID 36516059 | 100 people, low-molecular-weight peptides vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Crow's feet, roughness, elasticity, moisture better than placebo (analysis of 84 people) |
| Vleminckx 2024, PMID 39143887 | 85 East Asian women (43–65 yrs), 5 g vs. maltodextrin | 12 weeks | Skin density and moisture better than placebo after 84 days |

Individual studies also examined cellulite (105 women, six months; improvement mainly in normal-weight women, Schunck 2015, PMID 26561784) and brittle nails. The nail study, however, was open-label and without a control group: 25 participants, nail growth +12%, broken nails −42% (Hexsel 2017, PMID 28786550). Without a comparison group, a placebo effect cannot be ruled out here.

In patients with pressure ulcers (decubitus), a collagen hydrolysate with a high proportion of prolyl-hydroxyproline was tested in addition to standard treatment. In 120 participants, wound healing after 16 weeks was better in this group than under placebo (Sugihara 2018, PMID 30061579). A small study from 2026 in moderate atopic dermatitis reported lower use of cortisone cream with 5 g of collagen peptides (0.94 g versus 5.60 g; Proksch 2026, PMID 41758763); the authors themselves call for larger confirmatory studies.

#### The Meta-Analyses on Skin – and the Question of Funding

If the skin studies are pooled, the picture at first glance is clear:

- 19 studies with 1,125 participants: favorable effect on moisture, elasticity and wrinkles (de Miranda 2021, PMID 33742704).
- 26 studies with 1,721 participants: significant improvement in moisture and elasticity; however, the authors identify several sources of bias and call for large studies (Pu 2023, PMID 37432180).
- 10 studies with 646 participants: moisture and elasticity improved, with moderate heterogeneity and a predominantly unclear risk of bias (Danessa 2025, PMID 40826844).

A 2025 meta-analysis went one step further and separated the studies by funder and quality. Across all 23 studies with 1,474 participants, an advantage was again seen. However, when only the studies *without* industry funding were considered, the effect on moisture, elasticity and wrinkles disappeared. The methodologically high-quality studies also showed no significant effect in any category; only in the low-quality studies was there an improvement in elasticity. The authors conclude that there is currently no clinical evidence to support the use of collagen supplements for the prevention or treatment of skin aging (Myung 2025, PMID 40324552).

This result is not a final verdict – the number of independent studies is small, and a lack of statistical significance does not automatically mean that there is no effect. But it shows how heavily the positive overall balance depends on studies that the suppliers financed themselves. For readers, this means: the claim "clinically proven" on a collagen package is usually based on studies by the product's own raw material supplier.

#### Joints and Osteoarthritis

The second large group of studies concerns joint pain – both in osteoarthritis and in physically active people without a diagnosis.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clark 2008, PMID 18416885 | 147 athletes with activity-related joint pain, 10 g vs. placebo | 24 weeks | Several pain scores better than placebo; only 97 of 147 evaluable |
| Bruyère 2012, PMID 22500661 | 200 people aged 50+ with joint pain, 1.2 g vs. placebo | 6 months | Response rate 51.6% vs. 36.5% after 6 months; no difference after 3 months |
| Kumar 2015, PMID 24852756 | Knee osteoarthritis, peptides from pig skin or bovine bone vs. placebo | 13 weeks | WOMAC, pain and quality-of-life scores better than placebo |
| Zdzieblik 2017, PMID 28177710 | 139 athletes with knee complaints, 5 g vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Pain on exertion better than placebo (p = 0.046); pain at rest not |
| Bongers 2020, PMID 31990581 | 167 active people (median 63 yrs) with knee pain, 10 g vs. placebo | 12 weeks | No difference: pain fell equally in both groups |

The Bongers study is remarkable because it is one of the larger ones and reports a clearly negative result: knee pain and knee function improved – but under placebo just as much as under collagen. This illustrates how large the placebo effect is in joint pain.

The meta-analyses nevertheless mostly reach a favorable result:

- A meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled studies found a significant reduction in the total WOMAC score (an osteoarthritis questionnaire) and in the pain score on the visual analog scale; however, the WOMAC subscales for pain and functional limitation did not differ significantly (García-Coronado 2019, PMID 30368550).
- The most comprehensive analysis to date included 35 studies with 3,165 patients with osteoarthritis. Collagen derivatives – hydrolyzed and undenatured collagen are pooled here – had small to moderate effects on pain (standardized mean difference −0.35) and function (−0.31). Side effects and study discontinuations were not more frequent than under control (Liang 2024, PMID 38218227).
- An analysis of only four studies with 507 patients with knee osteoarthritis also found pain relief, but rated all four studies as having a high risk of bias (Lin 2023, PMID 37717022).
- An updated meta-analysis with 11 studies and 870 participants reported improvements in function and pain, but with very high heterogeneity (I² 75% and 88%) (Simental-Mendía 2025, PMID 39212129).

For context: a standardized mean difference of about 0.3 is considered a small effect. It is of a similar magnitude to that of some other food supplements for osteoarthritis and considerably smaller than the effect one would hope for from a pain medication. A large review of food supplements in osteoarthritis found large short-term effects on pain for collagen hydrolysate, but concluded overall that the quality of the evidence is very low and that no supplement showed clinically meaningful long-term effects (Liu 2018, PMID 29018060).

#### Distinction: Undenatured Type II Collagen (UC-II)

Undenatured type II collagen is gently extracted from chicken breast cartilage so that the original structure of the collagen is partly preserved. It is taken in very small amounts – 40 milligrams per day in the studies, i.e. about one hundredth to one two-hundred-fiftieth of the gram amounts of collagen hydrolysate. It is not intended to act as a building material but via so-called oral tolerance: the immune system in the gut is supposed to learn not to treat cartilage collagen as a target for attack. This idea comes from animal models of joint inflammation.

Historically, the principle was first tested in rheumatoid arthritis. A small study with 60 patients reported a decrease in swollen and tender joints in 1993 (Trentham 1993, PMID 8378772). A larger study with 274 participants and four doses subsequently found a significant advantage only at the lowest dose and only according to one of three assessment criteria (Barnett 1998, PMID 9485087). The approach has not become established as a rheumatoid arthritis therapy.

For osteoarthritis and joint complaints, the available studies are mainly close to the manufacturer:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lugo 2016, PMID 26822714 | 191 people with knee osteoarthritis, UC-II vs. glucosamine/chondroitin vs. placebo | 180 days | Total WOMAC score better than placebo (p = 0.002) and than glucosamine/chondroitin (p = 0.04) |
| Lugo 2013, PMID 24153020 | 55 healthy people with activity-related knee pain, UC-II vs. placebo | 120 days | Knee extension 81.0° vs. 74.0°; longer pain-free exertion |
| Yuenyongviwat 2025, PMID 40897777 | 68 people with knee osteoarthritis, UC-II plus collagen hydrolysate vs. placebo | 12 weeks | No difference in pain, function, rescue medication |

The most recent study comes from an independent research group that explicitly points out that most previous collagen studies were funded by industry. Like Bongers 2020, it found improvements in both groups, but no advantage over placebo (Yuenyongviwat 2025, PMID 40897777, registered as NCT06229951).

Important for consumers: UC-II and collagen hydrolysate are not interchangeable. If you read studies on one product, you cannot infer anything about the other.

#### Bone

For bone, the data are thin but interesting:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| König 2018, PMID 29337906 | 131 postmenopausal women with reduced bone density, 5 g vs. placebo | 12 months | T-score spine +0.1 vs. −0.03; femoral neck +0.09 vs. −0.01 |
| Elam 2015, PMID 25314004 | 39 women with osteopenia, calcium-collagen chelate plus vitamin D vs. calcium plus vitamin D | 12 months | Loss of whole-body bone density −1.33% vs. −3.75% (completers) |
| Lampropoulou-Adamidou 2022, PMID 34980546 | 51 women with osteopenia, 5 g peptides plus calcium/vitamin D vs. calcium/vitamin D | 12 months | Higher bone density at the tibia and lumbar spine with collagen |

The differences are small, the studies short, and no study has examined bone fractures – the endpoint that actually matters. Collagen peptides are no substitute for osteoporosis therapy with proven fracture reduction.

#### Muscles and Tendons

In sport, collagen peptides are promoted for two different goals: muscle building and strengthening tendons and ligaments.

**Muscle building.** The most frequently cited study examined 53 older men with sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) who did twelve weeks of strength training and received 15 g of collagen peptides or placebo (silicon dioxide). Fat-free mass increased by 4.2 kg under collagen and by 2.9 kg under placebo (Zdzieblik 2015, PMID 26353786). The problem: the placebo group received no protein at all. The advantage could therefore simply be due to the additional protein intake.

Comparisons with high-quality protein answer precisely this question. In 22 older women, whey protein increased muscle protein synthesis both in the short term and over six days, whereas collagen peptides did not (Oikawa 2020, PMID 31919527). In young adults, muscle thickness increased more after ten weeks of strength training with whey protein than with collagen, even when the collagen was enriched with leucine to the same content (Jacinto 2022, PMID 35042187). A study with 25 young men found no higher synthesis of muscle or connective tissue protein with 2 × 15 g of collagen per day than with placebo (Kirmse 2024, PMID 39086044).

**Tendons.** Here the results are contradictory:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jerger 2023, PMID 37424319 | 50 men, strength training plus 5 g vs. placebo | 14 weeks | Cross-sectional area of the patellar tendon increased more; stiffness and strength without group difference |
| Balshaw 2023, PMID 37436929 | 39 young men, strength training plus 15 g vs. placebo | 15 weeks | No differences in tendon size or mechanics |
| Miyamoto 2025, PMID 40623147 | 50 untrained men, 10 g vs. placebo | 16 weeks | Stiffness of calf muscle and Achilles tendon increased; cross-sectional area unchanged |
| Praet 2019, PMID 30609761 | 20 people with Achilles tendon complaints, crossover with training | 6 months | Faster improvement on the symptom questionnaire in the collagen phase (pilot study) |
| Dressler 2018, PMID 29769831 | 50 athletes with chronic ankle instability, 5 g vs. placebo | 6 months | Subjective stability better; mechanical stability unchanged |

A meta-analysis of 19 studies with 768 healthy adults found small advantages in fat-free mass, tendon morphology, muscle architecture and maximal strength with long-term collagen intake combined with training. The certainty of the evidence was rated as moderate for body composition, very low for tendon morphology and low for the other outcomes (Bischof 2024, PMID 39060741). An earlier systematic review concluded that collagen is not equivalent to higher-quality protein sources for muscle protein synthesis (Khatri 2021, PMID 34491424).

**Interim conclusion on sport:** For muscle building, there are better and cheaper protein sources. For tendons and ligaments, a benefit is biologically plausible and suggested in some studies, but has not been robustly demonstrated.

#### Other Areas of Use

In smaller studies, collagen peptides have been studied for blood pressure and vascular function, sleep, appetite, gum inflammation and digestive complaints, among other things. A meta-analysis of 12 placebo-controlled studies reported a reduction in systolic blood pressure of 5.04 mmHg and in LDL cholesterol of 4.09 mg/dl, but with extremely high heterogeneity among the individual studies (I² 98.9% and 93.4%) (Jalili 2023, PMID 35658958). These areas have been studied too little to allow robust statements.

### Animal and Laboratory Studies

In cell cultures, collagen peptides and in particular prolyl-hydroxyproline stimulate skin fibroblasts to grow; in mice and rats, they improved the skin barrier, bone density and cartilage metabolism in experiments. In human skin samples in the laboratory (ex vivo), collagen peptides promoted the formation of collagen and glycosaminoglycans (Asserin 2015, PMID 26362110). A systematic review of eleven mostly experimental studies concluded that collagen hydrolysates at concentrations of 50 to 500 micrograms per milliliter stimulate fibroblasts without being toxic (Inacio 2024, PMID 38892477). In rodent experiments with a high-calorie diet, a meta-analysis described effects on weight and fat mass (Bischof 2026, PMID 41168365) – a finding that has not been demonstrated in humans.

These results make the hypotheses plausible. But they do not prove that the peptide concentrations reached in humans after drinking are sufficient at the target tissue.

### Risks and Side Effects

**Established:** In the randomized studies, side effects did not occur more frequently than under placebo. The large osteoarthritis meta-analysis found no increased risk of adverse events or study discontinuations (Liang 2024, PMID 38218227). Occasionally, a feeling of fullness, bloating or an unpleasant taste is reported.

**Worth noting:**

- **Allergies and intolerances:** Collagen from fish can be problematic in people with fish allergy; products from cattle or pigs are relevant for people who avoid these animals for religious or ethical reasons. The origin is stated in the ingredients list.
- **Oxalate and kidney stones:** Hydroxyproline is partly broken down to oxalate in the body. In one study, urinary oxalate excretion rose by 43% after 30 g of gelatin per day compared with whey protein; even 5 to 10 g of gelatin led to measurable changes (Knight 2006, PMID 17021603). The significance for healthy people is unclear. People with calcium oxalate kidney stones or congenital hyperoxaluria should discuss intake with a doctor (a theoretical but biologically grounded risk).
- **Amount of protein:** In kidney diseases with protein restriction, collagen also counts toward daily protein intake.
- **Product quality:** Food supplements are not tested for efficacy before sale. The manufacturer is responsible for purity and freedom from contaminants; food control authorities only carry out spot checks.
- **False security:** The greatest practical risk is that collagen is used instead of effective measures – for example instead of UV protection against skin aging or instead of osteoporosis treatment.

### Legal Status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** Collagen peptides are foods. In Germany, food supplements must be notified to the Federal Office of Consumer Protection and Food Safety (BVL) before they are first sold; in Austria, separate rules apply. Efficacy is not assessed in either country. Under the EU Health Claims Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006), health claims are only permitted if they are authorized in the EU list. **There is no authorized health claim for collagen or collagen peptides.** The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has issued negative opinions on claims applied for, for example regarding joint health, because a cause-and-effect relationship had not been demonstrated. That is why many products contain vitamin C: for vitamin C, the claim that it contributes to normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin, cartilage, bones, gums, teeth and blood vessels is authorized. The advertising statement then legally refers to the vitamin, not to the collagen. Beauty claims ("for firmer skin") fall into a gray area and are judged differently by the courts.
- **USA:** Collagen peptides are sold as dietary supplements. Manufacturers may make so-called structure/function claims ("supports healthy skin"), but must carry a notice that the claim has not been evaluated by the FDA. Disease-related promises are not permitted.
- **Sport:** Collagen peptides are not on the WADA Prohibited List. As with all food supplements, there is a residual risk of contamination; elite athletes should look for tested lists (e.g. the Cologne List) (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing Studies and Outlook

**Established (registered studies, as of October 2026):**

- **Knee osteoarthritis:** A study by the Istituto Ortopedico Rizzoli compares a collagen hydrolysate formulation with placebo in 204 participants (NCT04998188). A smaller study compares different forms of collagen (NCT07800780).
- **Knee replacement:** Collagen peptides after total knee arthroplasty, endpoints connective tissue remodeling and wound healing (NCT05823727).
- **Skin:** A comparison of oral and topical collagen application alone and combined, with 165 women planned (NCT07473037).
- **Aging:** A study by the University of Vienna is investigating collagen peptides and markers of cellular aging in a planned 125 participants (NCT07456449).
- **Metabolism:** A study at King's College London is investigating quality of life, appetite and blood sugar control (NCT07229014).

**Assessment:** The decisive question for the coming years is not whether there will be further small positive studies – that is to be expected – but whether large, independently funded studies with preregistered endpoints confirm the effects. The meta-analysis by funding source (Myung 2025, PMID 40324552) has made the bar for this visible. For joint pain, a small effect is the most likely to be established; for skin and tendons, the situation remains open. Approval as a medicine is not to be expected, since the products are already freely available as foods and no manufacturer would have to bear the effort of an approval program.

### Conclusion

Collagen peptides are a well-tolerated food product with a large but methodologically weak study base. For joint pain in osteoarthritis, meta-analyses show small effects, but individual larger studies found no difference from placebo. For skin, the benefit measured in manufacturer studies disappears when only independently funded or methodologically high-quality studies are considered. For muscle building, collagen is inferior to other protein sources; undenatured type II collagen is a separate product with its own, likewise limited, data. In the EU, collagen may not be advertised with health claims – advertising messages usually refer to added vitamin C.
