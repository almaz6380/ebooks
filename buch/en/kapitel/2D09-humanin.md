## Humanin

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** endogenous peptide of 24 amino acids. On the gray market and in research, the modified form **HNG** (humanin-G, also S14G-humanin) is mostly used, in which serine at position 14 is replaced by glycine; it is considerably more potent in laboratory experiments.
- **Origin:** discovered in 2001 by Japanese researchers while searching brain tissue from a person with Alzheimer's disease for factors that protect nerve cells from dying – hence the name. It is encoded in the genome of the mitochondria; similar segments also exist in the nuclear genome.
- **Mechanism of action:** a "mitokine" (signaling molecule of the mitochondria) said to protect cells from programmed cell death. Among other things, it binds to a receptor complex of the CNTF receptor, WSX-1 and gp130 on the cell surface.
- **EU approval:** no.
- **US approval:** no.
- **WADA:** not listed by name; as a substance not approved anywhere, humanin or HNG falls in our assessment under the catch-all category S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **D** for administration as a drug – not a single study in which humans received humanin or HNG; in humans, there are only measurements of endogenous levels.

### How it is supposed to work

In 2001, the discoverers searched a gene library from brain tissue for segments that protect nerve cells from the toxic effects of Alzheimer's genes. They found a short peptide that, in cell cultures, protected nerve cells from numerous types of damage typical of Alzheimer's – against amyloid-beta and against the effects of several hereditary Alzheimer's mutations, but not against other toxins such as glutamate or prion fragments (Hashimoto 2001, PMID 11717357). The variant HNG was more effective than other protective factors tested.

The same group later showed that humanin binds to a receptor complex belonging to the cytokine receptor family (CNTF receptor, WSX-1 and gp130) and acts via the signaling molecule STAT3 (Hashimoto 2009, PMID 19386761). In addition, laboratory studies describe binding to proteins that control cell death and to the growth factor binding partner IGFBP-3.

A special feature: like MOTS-c (see the previous chapter), humanin is encoded in the genome of the mitochondria. These "mitochondrial peptides" are regarded as a new signaling level through which mitochondria communicate with the rest of the body (Miller 2022, PMID 35499074).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 8 publications classified as human studies; in none of them was humanin administered. They examine how endogenous levels change with training, oxygen therapy or dietary supplements, or concern patients' cells in the laboratory. In total, PubMed lists around 550 papers on humanin. On ClinicalTrials.gov, 3 studies are registered, all observational studies on humanin levels (in heart surgery, acute kidney failure and kidney transplantation).

#### Levels and age

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lee 2014, PMID 25040290 | mice and humans, observation and hormone administration | – | humanin levels decline with age; growth hormone and IGF-1 lowered them in mice and humans |
| Yen 2020, PMID 32575074 | children of centenarians and comparison subjects; animal models | cross-sectional | markedly higher levels in children of centenarians; lower in Alzheimer's and MELAS |
| Miller 2024, PMID 38520065 | centenarians with the Alzheimer's risk gene APOE4; mouse model | genetic analysis | rare humanin variant P3S more frequent in long-lived APOE4 carriers |
| Gidlund 2016, PMID 27923980 | 55 men with impaired glucose metabolism, strength training, Nordic walking or control, randomized | 12 weeks | humanin in muscle 35% higher after strength training, unchanged in blood |
| von Walden 2021, PMID 34351816 | 30 people, one endurance or strength session or control, randomized | single session | humanin in blood elevated after endurance exercise |
| Xu 2019, PMID 31134827 | 158 people with vascular dementia, hyperbaric oxygen therapy or standard care | 12 weeks | higher humanin levels and MMSE scores with hyperbaric therapy |

The observations form a coherent picture: humanin levels decline with age, are higher in children of very old people, lower in some diseases, and rise with exercise. The connection with the growth hormone system is interesting: growth hormone and IGF-1 lowered humanin levels in mice and humans, and long-lived mice with growth hormone deficiency had elevated levels (Lee 2014, PMID 25040290). This is noteworthy because growth hormone releasers are frequently offered on the same gray market (see Part II · C).

But here too: a level associated with health is not proof that supplementation makes people healthier. Humanin could just as well be an accompanying sign of healthy mitochondria without itself being the cause.

#### Administration as a drug

There is no published study and no registry entry in which humans received humanin or HNG (search October 2026). No case reports of side effects after self-administration were found either.

### Animal and laboratory studies

The preclinical literature is extensive:

- **Lifespan:** In roundworms, increased humanin production extended lifespan; in middle-aged mice, HNG improved metabolic values and lowered inflammatory markers (Yen 2020, PMID 32575074).
- **Heart:** In mice, HNG reduced infarct size after an experimentally induced heart attack (Muzumdar 2010, PMID 20651283); a 14-month treatment of old mice reduced age-related scarring of the heart muscle (Qin 2018, PMID 30004252).
- **Alzheimer's models:** In mice with APOE4, the rare variant P3S reduced amyloid deposits more than normal humanin (Miller 2024, PMID 38520065).
- **Retina:** In cell models with mitochondria from people with age-related macular degeneration, HNG protected against cell damage (Nashine 2017, PMID 28726777).

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** There are no safety data on administration to humans.

**Theoretical – with concrete warning signs from the laboratory:**

- **Cancer:** Humanin protects cells from programmed cell death – and this also applies to cancer cells. In mice with triple-negative breast cancer, supplemental humanin reduced cell death in the tumor, weakened the effect of chemotherapy and accelerated tumor growth and lung metastases. In biopsies of this type of breast cancer, humanin was found at increased levels. The authors explicitly advise caution in using humanin against degenerative diseases (Moreno Ayala 2020, PMID 32444831).
- **Inflammation in aged cells:** In cell experiments, humanin and MOTS-c increased the release of inflammatory messengers by aged ("senescent") cells (Mendelsohn 2018, PMID 30058454).
- **Immune reactions:** Repeated injection of a peptide can lead to the formation of antibodies; not studied.

**Gray market:** Humanin and HNG are offered as "longevity peptides" for injection. Content, purity and sterility are unverified.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved; as a substance with a pharmacological effect, humanin is considered a medicine, and selling it for use in humans is not permitted (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **USA:** not approved.
- **Sport:** in our assessment WADA category S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established:** Only observational studies on humanin levels are registered, for example after heart surgery (NCT03431844, 106 participants, completed) and in kidney transplantation (NCT07678073). There is no study of humanin administration.

**Assessment:** Humanin is a scientifically exciting signaling peptide and a possible biomarker of aging. Whether it will ever be tested as a medicine is open; the cancer findings from the animal model would have to be carefully clarified before any use in humans.

### Conclusion

Humanin is an endogenous mitochondrial peptide discovered in 2001 as a protective factor for nerve cells. Its levels decline with age, are higher in children of centenarians and rise with exercise; in animal experiments, the variant HNG protected the heart, brain and metabolism. Humanin has never been given to humans as a drug, and in mice it promoted the growth of breast cancer. The data are not sufficient to assess the benefit or safety of supplementation.
