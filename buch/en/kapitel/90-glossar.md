# Glossary

The following explanations are deliberately kept short. More detailed accounts can be found in the chapters of Part I, especially in "What Peptides Are – and What They Are Not" and "Reading Studies Correctly."

### A

**Absolute risk reduction**: The difference in the frequency of an event between two groups, expressed in percentage points. If a risk falls from 8% to 6.5%, the absolute risk reduction is 1.5 percentage points.

**Acromegaly**: A disease caused by persistent overproduction of growth hormone in adulthood, usually due to a benign tumor of the pituitary gland. Enlarged hands, feet, and facial features are typical.

**Adverse event**: Any health problem that occurs during a study, regardless of whether it was caused by the treatment. Only the comparison with the control group shows whether a connection is likely.

**Agonist**: A substance that binds to a receptor and activates it, that is, triggers a signal. Semaglutide, for example, is an agonist at the GLP-1 receptor.

**Albumin**: The most abundant protein in blood plasma. Some peptide drugs are designed to bind to albumin and thereby remain in the body longer.

**Amino acid**: The basic building block of peptides and proteins. Humans essentially use 20 standard amino acids, which are linked into chains via peptide bonds.

**Amylin**: A peptide hormone that is released from the pancreas together with insulin and influences, among other things, satiety and gastric emptying. Modified versions such as pramlintide and cagrilintide are used or being researched as drugs.

**Analog**: A molecule modeled on a natural template but deliberately modified – for example, to make it more stable or longer-acting.

**Angiogenesis**: The formation of new blood vessels. It is important for wound healing but can also promote the growth of tumors.

**Antagonist**: A substance that binds to a receptor without activating it, thereby blocking the action of the body's own messenger substances.

**Antibodies**: Protein molecules of the immune system that bind specifically to foreign structures. Antibodies formed against peptide drugs can weaken their effect or trigger reactions.

**Antimicrobial peptides**: Endogenous peptides that are part of the innate immune defense and can damage bacteria, fungi, or viruses. One example is LL-37.

**Approval (marketing authorization)**: The official permission to place a drug on the market, granted after review of its quality, efficacy, and safety.

### B

**Bioavailability**: The proportion of an administered active ingredient that reaches the bloodstream unchanged. With an intravenous infusion it is 100%; with peptide tablets it is often only a few percent or less.

**Biologic**: A drug whose active ingredient is produced with the help of living cells, for example by genetic engineering in bacteria or mammalian cells. Many larger peptides and all antibody drugs belong to this category.

**Biosimilar**: A follow-on version of a biological drug after patent protection has expired. It must be demonstrably very similar to the original in quality, efficacy, and safety.

**Blinding**: Concealing group assignment from participants and/or study staff in order to avoid expectation effects.

**BMI (body mass index)**: Body weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. A rough measure of underweight, normal weight, and overweight.

### C

**Case report**: The description of a single case of illness in a scientific journal. Case reports are important for detecting rare side effects but say little about efficacy.

**Cohort study**: An observational study that follows a group of people over a long period and compares how often certain events occur with different characteristics or treatments.

**Compounding**: In the US, the preparation of drugs by pharmacies themselves, for example during supply shortages. Such preparations are not approved finished drugs.

**Confidence interval**: A range around a study result within which the true value lies with a certain probability (usually 95%). Wide intervals indicate an uncertain estimate.

**Conflict of interest**: A situation in which financial or other interests could influence the judgment of researchers or authors, for example through funding of a study by the manufacturer.

**Confounding (confounder)**: A factor that is associated with both a treatment and an outcome and can thus create an apparent association. A typical problem of observational studies.

**Control group**: The comparison group in a study, which receives no active ingredient, a placebo, or a standard treatment.

**Cosmetic product**: A product for external use that cleans, cares for, protects, or changes the appearance. It may not claim any medicinal effect and does not require approval in the EU.

**C-terminus**: The end of a peptide chain with the free acid group. Peptide sequences are written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

**Cyclic peptide**: A peptide whose chain is closed into a ring. Ring structures often make peptides more stable against enzymes.

### D

**Dalton**: A unit of measurement for the mass of molecules. An amino acid in a peptide chain weighs on average slightly more than 100 daltons.

**Dietary supplement**: A food intended to supplement the normal diet with nutrients or other substances. It does not require approval but may not have a pharmacological effect.

**Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4)**: An enzyme that, among other things, rapidly breaks down the gut hormone GLP-1. Inhibitors of this enzyme (gliptins) are used as diabetes tablets.

**Double-blind**: A study in which neither the participants nor the study staff know who receives the active ingredient and who receives placebo. This reduces the influence of expectations on the results.

**Drug (medicinal product)**: A substance or preparation intended to cure, alleviate, or prevent diseases, or that influences bodily functions pharmacologically, immunologically, or metabolically. In the EU, a drug requires approval before it can be sold.

### E

**Endotoxins**: Heat-stable components of the cell wall of certain bacteria. If they enter the blood, they can trigger fever, inflammation, and circulatory reactions; they remain active even after the bacteria have been killed.

**Endpoint**: The characteristic by which the success of a treatment is measured in a study, for example weight loss, blood sugar, or heart attacks. The most important one is defined before the start of the study as the primary endpoint.

**Estimand**: A precise description of which treatment effect is estimated in a study – for example, regardless of whether participants discontinue treatment, or only for the period of actual use.

**EudraVigilance**: The European Medicines Agency's database for reports of suspected side effects.

**Evidence level**: In this book, a rating from A to D of how well the effect of a peptide in humans is supported. A stands for several large randomized trials and approval, D for exclusively animal and laboratory studies.

**Evidence pyramid**: A ranking of study types according to their strength of evidence, from laboratory and animal studies at the bottom to meta-analyses of randomized trials at the top.

### F

**FAERS**: The US Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) reporting system for suspected side effects.

### G

**Ghrelin**: A peptide hormone produced mainly in the stomach that triggers hunger and stimulates the release of growth hormone.

**GHRH (growth hormone–releasing hormone)**: A hormone from the hypothalamus that stimulates the pituitary gland to release growth hormone. Sermorelin, tesamorelin, and CJC-1295 are derived from it.

**GHRP (growth hormone–releasing peptides)**: A group of synthetic peptides that increase the release of growth hormone via the ghrelin receptor, for example GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin, and ipamorelin.

**GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide)**: A gut hormone from the incretin group. Tirzepatide activates the GIP receptor in addition to the GLP-1 receptor.

**GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1)**: A gut hormone released after eating that enhances insulin release depending on blood sugar, slows gastric emptying, and influences the feeling of satiety.

**GLP-1 receptor agonist**: An agent that activates the GLP-1 receptor, for example exenatide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, or semaglutide.

**Glucagon**: A peptide hormone of the pancreas that raises blood sugar. Some new agents additionally activate the glucagon receptor.

**GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone)**: A hormone from the hypothalamus that regulates the release of LH and FSH, the hormones that control the sex hormones. Gonadorelin is a synthetic form.

**Growth hormone (somatotropin)**: A protein from the pituitary gland that influences growth, metabolism, and body composition. Many peptides in this book are intended to increase its release.

### H

**Half-life**: The time after which half of an active ingredient has been broken down or excreted by the body. It largely determines how often a drug has to be given.

**Hard endpoint**: A study outcome that matters directly to patients, for example death, heart attack, stroke, or bone fracture.

**Hazard ratio**: A measure of how the risk of an event over time differs between two groups. A value of 0.80 means a relative risk that is about 20% lower.

**HbA1c**: The proportion of hemoglobin (the red blood pigment) to which sugar is bound. It reflects the average blood sugar over the past two to three months and is an important surrogate endpoint in diabetes studies.

**HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography)**: A laboratory method with which mixtures of substances can be separated and the purity of peptides determined.

**Hydrolysate**: A mixture of fragments produced when proteins are split with enzymes or acid. Collagen peptides are obtained in this way.

**Hypoglycemia**: Low blood sugar, that is, a blood sugar level that is too low. It can lead to trembling, sweating, confusion, and loss of consciousness and is an emergency.

**Hypothalamus**: A region of the brain that controls the pituitary gland via releasing hormones and is involved in hunger, thirst, sleep, and body temperature.

### I

**IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1)**: A growth factor produced mainly in the liver under the influence of growth hormone. It mediates many effects of growth hormone and serves as a laboratory value for assessing it.

**Immunogenicity**: The property of a substance to trigger a reaction of the immune system, for example the formation of antibodies.

**Impurity**: Unwanted components of a product, for example defective peptide chains from synthesis, solvent residues, metals, or bacterial components.

**Incretins**: Gut hormones that enhance insulin release after a meal. The most important are GLP-1 and GIP.

**Insulin**: A peptide hormone of the pancreas consisting of 51 amino acids that lowers blood sugar. In 1922 it was the first peptide used as a drug.

**Intention-to-treat analysis**: An analysis of a study in which all participants are counted in the group to which they were assigned – even if they discontinued treatment. It is considered a more realistic estimate of the effect in everyday life.

**In vitro**: "In glass" – experiments in the laboratory on cells, tissues, or isolated molecules outside a living organism.

### L

**Lyophilisate**: A freeze-dried powder. Many peptides are stored in this form and have to be dissolved in a liquid before use.

### M

**Mass spectrometry**: A measurement method that determines molecules by their mass. It can be used to identify peptides unambiguously in products and body fluids, for example in doping controls.

**Medicinal product by function**: A legal term for a product that counts as a drug because of its pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic action – regardless of how it is advertised.

**Medicinal product by presentation**: A legal term for a product that counts as a drug because it is presented or advertised as a remedy for diseases.

**Melanocortin receptors**: A family of receptors that influence, among other things, skin pigmentation, appetite, and sexual function. Afamelanotide, bremelanotide, Melanotan II, and setmelanotide act on these receptors.

**Meta-analysis**: A statistical summary of the results of several studies on the same question. It is only as good as the studies included in it.

**Mimetic**: A substance that imitates the action of another without having its structure – for example, a small molecule that activates a receptor like a peptide hormone.

### N

**NCT number**: The identification number of a study in the ClinicalTrials.gov registry, consisting of "NCT" and eight digits.

**Novel food**: In the EU, a food or ingredient that was not consumed to a significant degree before May 15, 1997. Such products require authorization before they can be sold.

**N-terminus**: The beginning of a peptide chain with the free amino group.

**Number needed to treat (NNT)**: The number of people who have to be treated for a certain period so that one person avoids an event. It is calculated from the absolute risk reduction.

### O

**Obesity**: Pathological overweight, usually defined as a body mass index of 30 or more. It is considered a chronic disease with an increased risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and joint disease.

**Off-label use**: The use of an approved drug outside the approved indications, doses, or groups of people.

**Oligopeptide**: A short peptide of about two to ten amino acids.

**Open-label study**: A study without blinding, in which participants and study staff know which treatment is being given.

### P

**Pancreatitis**: Inflammation of the pancreas. It is discussed as a possible, rare side effect of GLP-1 drugs.

**Peptide**: A chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. From about 50 amino acids upward, it is usually called a protein.

**Peptide bond**: The chemical bond between the acid group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next, during whose formation water is split off.

**Pharmacodynamics**: The study of what an active ingredient does in the body – that is, its effects and mechanisms of action.

**Pharmacokinetics**: The study of what the body does with an active ingredient – absorption, distribution, breakdown, and excretion.

**Pharmacovigilance**: The systematic monitoring of drugs after approval in order to detect and assess side effects.

**Pituitary gland (hypophysis)**: A small hormone gland at the base of the skull that releases, among other things, growth hormone and the control hormones for the thyroid, adrenal glands, and gonads.

**Placebo**: A dummy preparation without active ingredient that is used for comparison in studies.

**Placebo effect**: An improvement in symptoms that is due not to an active ingredient but to expectations, attention, or the natural course of the condition.

**PMID**: The PubMed identification number, a unique number for each entry in the PubMed literature database.

**Polypeptide**: A longer peptide chain, usually with more than ten amino acids.

**Preclinical studies**: Investigations in the laboratory and in animals that are carried out before the first studies in humans.

**Preprint**: A scientific paper that is made public before review by experts (peer review).

**Prescribing information**: The officially approved information on an approved drug intended for physicians and pharmacies, with indications, dosage, contraindications, and side effects (in the EU, the summary of product characteristics).

**Protease**: An enzyme that cleaves peptide bonds and thus breaks down proteins and peptides. Also called peptidase.

**Protein**: A large protein molecule usually consisting of more than 50 amino acids with a specific three-dimensional folding.

**Publication bias**: The distortion of the overall picture when studies with positive results are published more often than those with negative results.

**PubMed**: A freely accessible literature database of the US National Library of Medicine with millions of entries from biomedical research.

**p-value**: A statistical measure of how likely a difference at least as large would be if there were in reality no effect. Values below 0.05 are usually considered statistically significant.

### R

**Randomization**: The random assignment of participants to the groups of a study. It ensures that the groups differ systematically only in the treatment.

**RCT (randomized controlled trial)**: A study with random assignment to a treatment group and a control group. It is considered the most reliable way to demonstrate the effect of a treatment.

**Receptor**: A protein molecule, usually on the cell surface, to which a messenger substance binds, thereby triggering a signal in the cell.

**Recombinant**: Produced by genetic engineering, by introducing the blueprint for a peptide or protein into bacteria, yeasts, or other cells.

**Relative risk**: The ratio of event frequency in two groups. A relative risk of 0.5 means a halving – regardless of how common the event is overall.

**Research peptides**: Peptides sold with the notice "for research purposes only" or "not for human use," but often to private individuals. They are not tested drugs.

**Rhabdomyolysis**: A breakdown of muscle tissue in which muscle components enter the blood and can damage the kidneys.

### S

**Secretagogue**: A substance that stimulates the release of another substance. Growth hormone secretagogues increase the release of growth hormone.

**Sequence**: The order of amino acids in a peptide chain. It determines the properties of the peptide.

**SNAC**: An excipient (sodium N-[8-(2-hydroxybenzoyl)amino]caprylate) that enables absorption of the peptide in the stomach in the semaglutide tablet.

**Solid-phase peptide synthesis**: A chemical process in which a peptide chain is built up building block by building block on a solid support material. The basis for manufacturing most short peptides.

**Statistical significance**: The statement that an observed difference is probably not due to chance alone. It says nothing about whether the difference is large or meaningful.

**Sterility**: Freedom from living microorganisms. A sterile product can nevertheless contain endotoxins.

**Study phases**: The stages of drug testing in humans, from phase 1 (tolerability in a few people) to phase 3 (large comparative studies) and phase 4 (observation after approval).

**Study registry**: Public databases in which clinical studies are entered before they begin, for example ClinicalTrials.gov or the EU registry.

**Subcutaneous**: Injected under the skin, into the subcutaneous fat tissue. The most common route of administration for peptide drugs.

**Surrogate endpoint**: A measured value used as a substitute for a clinically meaningful event, for example blood sugar instead of heart attack. Improvements in a surrogate do not automatically mean a benefit for patients.

**Systematic review**: A paper that, according to predefined rules, searches for, assesses, and summarizes all available studies on a question.

### T

**Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE)**: A medical exemption in sport that permits the use of a prohibited but medically necessary drug.

**Type 2 diabetes**: The most common form of diabetes, in which the body uses insulin less effectively and the pancreas does not produce enough to compensate. Often associated with overweight.

### W

**WADA (World Anti-Doping Agency)**: The international organization that publishes the annual list of substances and methods prohibited in sport.
