## DSIP (Delta Sleep–Inducing Peptide)

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** nonapeptide of nine amino acids (Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu); international nonproprietary name emideltide.
- **Origin:** isolated in 1977 by a research group in Basel from the cerebral venous blood of rabbits that had been put into deep sleep by electrical stimulation. A gene, a precursor protein or a receptor for DSIP has not been found to this day.
- **Mechanism of action:** was supposed to promote deep sleep (delta sleep with slow EEG waves) as an endogenous "sleep substance"; later, effects on stress hormones, pain and withdrawal symptoms were suspected.
- **EU approval:** no.
- **US approval:** no. Emideltide was removed from FDA "Category 2" in April 2026; in July 2026, the advisory committee PCAC voted 6 to 7 **against** inclusion in the list of permitted compounding substances (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **Russia and neighboring countries:** DSIP preparations (e.g., Deltaran, Deltalicin) have been used there in studies and in practice.
- **WADA:** not listed by name (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **C** – only very small studies, mostly conducted in the 1980s; the methodologically best of them found no clinically relevant benefit.

### How it is supposed to work

The idea was simple and appealing: if the brain releases a substance into the blood during sleep that induces sleep in other animals, it could be a natural sleeping aid – without the drawbacks of sedatives. The Basel researchers transferred blood from sleeping rabbits to awake animals and isolated from it the peptide they considered responsible for the effect (Monnier 1977, PMID 862769).

The hope has not been borne out. A 2006 review calls DSIP a "still unresolved enigma": its connection to sleep is "extremely poorly documented," because neither the associated gene nor a receptor has been found. In animal experiments, certain artificial modifications promoted deep sleep more than DSIP itself. The authors suspect that a hitherto unknown DSIP-like peptide could be responsible for the observed effects (Kovalzon 2006, PMID 16539679).

Further suspected effects – on stress hormones, pain perception, the opioid system and antioxidant enzymes – come mainly from animal experiments. In sheep, labeled DSIP crossed the barrier between blood and cerebrospinal fluid to a small to moderate extent (Zlokovic 1988, PMID 3420012).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 18 publications classified as human studies; on closer inspection, one of them concerns a different topic (spinal surgery). Almost all date from 1981 to 1998 and each had between 1 and 16 participants in placebo-controlled parts. On ClinicalTrials.gov, the search yielded one hit that does not relate to DSIP; there is no registered DSIP study.

#### Sleep disorders

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schneider-Helmert 1981, PMID 6895513 | 6 healthy people, double-blind, crossover vs. placebo | single infusion | sleep time 59% longer within 130 minutes; no classic sedation |
| Schneider-Helmert 1981, PMID 7028502 | 6 people with chronic insomnia | single dose | longer, less interrupted sleep |
| Schneider-Helmert 1987, PMID 3622582 | 14 people with severe chronic insomnia, double-blind vs. placebo | 7 nights | sleep efficiency and daytime performance at the level of healthy people, according to the authors |
| Monti 1987, PMID 3583493 | people with chronic insomnia, double-blind, crossover | 4 nights | improvements "of little clinical significance"; differences already existed before treatment |
| Bes 1992, PMID 1299794 | 16 people with chronic insomnia, double-blind vs. placebo | 3 nights | weak effects, partly explainable by random fluctuation; "no major therapeutic benefit" |

The first studies came almost entirely from a single Basel research group and were uniformly positive. In six healthy volunteers, a morning infusion prolonged sleep time in the following two-plus hours by 59%, without the participants appearing sedated in the classic sense (Schneider-Helmert 1981, PMID 6895513). In 14 people with severe insomnia, according to the authors, sleep efficiency and daytime performance reached the level of healthy controls after seven nights (Schneider-Helmert 1987, PMID 3622582).

Independent groups could not confirm this. One study did find more total and NREM sleep, but the significant differences from placebo (NREM sleep and sleep stage 2) already existed before treatment; the authors judged the improvement to be of little clinical significance (Monti 1987, PMID 3583493). Another study with 16 people found statistically significant but weak effects on sleep efficiency and sleep onset latency, which were partly explainable by a random change in the placebo group; subjective sleep quality did not change. Its conclusion: short-term treatment with DSIP probably has no major therapeutic benefit (Bes 1992, PMID 1299794).

This pattern – enthusiastic first reports, sobering replications – is typical of substances whose effect is based on very small studies.

#### Withdrawal, pain and other uses

In the 1980s, DSIP was used in 107 inpatients undergoing alcohol or opiate withdrawal; according to the physicians' assessment, withdrawal symptoms disappeared or improved markedly in 97% of the opiate-dependent and 87% of the alcohol-dependent patients. There was no control group, and some of the people could not be evaluated (Dick 1984, PMID 6548969). A later open-label study on opioid detoxification was published only as a short communication without an abstract (Backmund 1998, PMID 9617990). Further individual reports concern seven people with chronic pain (Larbig 1984, PMID 6548970) and a man with narcolepsy (Schneider-Helmert 1984, PMID 6548968). One study examined an intranasal DSIP preparation (Deltalicin) in diabetic retinopathy, in which the electrical responses of the retina improved (Kresiun 2014, PMID 25739189).

#### Hormones and anesthesia

Several small studies in healthy people examined whether DSIP influences stress hormones. A study with eleven men found less ACTH in the blood after an injection but unchanged cortisol levels (Bjartell 1989, PMID 2554357); another with a total of 20 men found no effect on ACTH and cortisol (Späth-Schwalbe 1995, PMID 7777652). DSIP had no effect on growth hormone and prolactin in women (Giusti 1993, PMID 8475226).

A study of 24 women during anesthesia is noteworthy: instead of deepening anesthesia, as would be expected of a "natural sleep substance," DSIP increased heart rate and paradoxically reduced the delta rhythm on the EEG (Pomfrett 2009, PMID 19142086).

### Animal and laboratory studies

- In rabbits and rats, early studies described sleep-promoting effects, but these could not be consistently replicated (Kovalzon 2006, PMID 16539679).
- In mice, the oldest 10% of animals that received a Russian DSIP preparation throughout life lived 16% longer than the control animals; spontaneous tumors appeared later (Voĭtenkov 2009, PMID 20405733). This paper comes from the circle of Russian gerontology that also studies Epitalon (see the chapter Epitalon).
- Other animal studies describe effects on stress resistance, pain perception and antioxidant enzymes.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** In the small studies, DSIP was described as well tolerated; headaches occurred occasionally (Dick 1984, PMID 6548969). Under anesthesia, it increased heart rate and reduced heart rate variability (Pomfrett 2009, PMID 19142086). There are no systematic safety data covering more than a few days.

**Theoretical:** Because neither the receptor nor the natural function has been clarified, effects and interactions – for example with sleeping pills, alcohol or opioids – can hardly be predicted.

**Gray market:** DSIP is sold online as powder for injection and advertised as a sleeping aid. The preparations used in the studies were infused into a vein; data on subcutaneous injection are lacking. The purity and content of gray-market products are unverified.

**Important:** For chronic insomnia, there are effective, well-studied treatments, above all cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved; as a substance with a pharmacological effect, DSIP is considered a medicine, and selling it for use in humans is not permitted (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **USA:** not approved; in July 2026, the advisory committee PCAC narrowly voted against allowing it for pharmacy compounding. The final FDA decision was pending at the time of going to press (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **Sport:** not listed by name on the WADA list (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established:** No study with DSIP is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (search October 2026). Since the 1990s, practically no new clinical data have been added.

**Assessment:** Without clarity about the receptor and natural function and without a modern, sufficiently large sleep study, DSIP is not expected to return as a medicine.

### Conclusion

DSIP was discovered in 1977 as a possible endogenous "sleep substance," but its gene, receptor and natural function remain unknown to this day. Early studies by a Basel group with six to 14 people each reported better sleep; independent placebo-controlled studies found only weak effects of little clinical significance. There are hardly any newer data in humans. The data are not sufficient to regard DSIP as an effective or safe sleeping aid.
