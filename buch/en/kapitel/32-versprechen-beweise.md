## Promises Versus Evidence

If you search the internet for peptides, you will quickly come across shops, videos, and forums in which certain substances are described in grand terms: they are said to repair tendons, slow aging, melt away fat, or sharpen memory. This chapter sets such typical advertising promises against what studies in humans have actually shown.

The peptides selected are those most frequently advertised on German- and English-language websites in 2026. The promises are phrased in general terms and summarize recurring claims; they do not come from any specific seller. The column "What human studies show" is based on the chapters in Part II, where the sources are discussed in detail. Animal experiments and laboratory results are explicitly labeled as such.

A preliminary remark: the fact that a benefit has not been demonstrated does not automatically mean that a substance is ineffective. It means that nobody knows – including the sellers who sell it. Likewise: for barely studied substances, the fact that no side effects have been published does not mean they are safe, only that nobody has systematically looked for them.

The overall picture: of the 16 substances, none has evidence level A. Five have level D, meaning they have not been studied in humans at all. For the substances with level B – ipamorelin, collagen, glutathione – the best studies did not confirm the advertising promises or found only small effects. The only exception is retatrutide, whose strong effect is proven – but not for the product sold on the gray market.

### Six patterns behind the promises

If you read the table row by row, you will recognize recurring patterns. They help in assessing claims about peptides that do not appear in this book.

#### 1. From animals to humans

Almost all promises about BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, Dihexa, AOD-9604, and Epitalon rely on experiments in rats, mice, or cell cultures. Such studies are important for understanding mechanisms of action. They do not predict whether a substance works the same way in humans: a large proportion of agents that succeed in animal experiments fail in human studies. A current example from this book is the memory peptide Dihexa. In rats, it promoted learning and synapse formation; the related agent fosgonimeton, which was supposed to act via the same growth factor system, missed all primary and secondary goals in a study of 287 people with Alzheimer's disease (Porsteinsson 2025, PMID 41393340).

With BPC-157 there is a second problem: the positive animal studies come predominantly from a single research group and have hardly been independently replicated.

#### 2. A measured value is not yet a benefit

CJC-1295 raises growth hormone, Epitalon activates telomerase in cell cultures, glutathione capsules raise the glutathione level in the blood. These are substitute measures ("surrogate endpoints," see Part I). Whether anything that matters to people follows from them – more muscle strength, longer life, fewer diseases – is a separate question that needs its own studies. With growth hormone, the example of ibutamoren shows how large the gap can be: in 65 healthy older people, fat-free mass rose by 1.1 kg over one year, but strength and physical function did not improve (Nass 2008, PMID 18981485).

#### 3. Confusion of substances

Several promises are based on studies of a different substance from the one being sold:

- **TB-500** is a modified fragment. The human studies concern the full protein thymosin beta-4 – and even these hardly concern tendons, ligaments, or muscles.
- **Epitalon** is a synthetic tetrapeptide. The often-cited study on longer life concerned the bovine extract epithalamin, was not randomized, and was never independently replicated.
- **CJC-1295** is mostly sold without the "DAC" group that was used in the human studies.
- **Retatrutide** from the gray market is not the drug from the studies. Laboratory analyses of other gray-market peptides show that vials often contain less, more, or a different active ingredient than stated; for ipamorelin and CJC-1295, laboratories found modified, undeclared molecules (Gajda 2019, PMID 30136411).

#### 4. Negative studies go unmentioned

For several peptides, randomized studies do exist – they just found no benefit. Ipamorelin did not significantly shorten the time to the first meal after bowel surgery (Beck 2014, PMID 25331030). Glutathione infusions did not protect against nerve damage from chemotherapy (Leal 2014, PMID 24619793). With collagen, the skin benefit disappeared as soon as only independently funded studies were considered (Myung 2025, PMID 40324552). Such results practically never appear in advertising copy. A trustworthy presentation names positive *and* negative studies.

#### 5. Small studies from a narrow circle

Semax, Selank, Epitalon, and Pinealon were studied almost exclusively in Russia, mostly by the developers themselves, in small groups, and often without a placebo comparison. That does not automatically make the results wrong. But it does mean they were never tested under the conditions required for approval today: preregistered, blinded, sufficiently large, and confirmed by independent groups.

#### 6. "No side effects" usually means "not studied"

The claim that a peptide is "well tolerated" or "free of side effects" rests, for BPC-157, on a total of 30 people. With so few participants, only common, immediately occurring problems can be detected; a side effect affecting one in a thousand people would remain invisible. For substances that amplify growth signals – growth hormone secretagogues, IGF-1 LR3, peptides that promote new blood vessel formation – there is also a theoretical but untested concern about tumor growth. For Melanotan II, case reports describe new and changed moles and melanomas; a causal relationship has not been proven, but has not been ruled out either. Added to this are the risks of the products themselves: gray-market vials of Melanotan II contained less active ingredient than stated and unknown impurities (Breindahl 2015, PMID 24771717). More on this in Part IV.

### How to tell trustworthy from untrustworthy claims

The following checklist helps you assess claims about peptides – in shops, videos, forums, or magazines. It does not replace professional advice, but it sharpens your eye for how much lies behind a claim.

#### Questions about the source

- **Who is speaking?** Does the person or website sell the substance itself, or offer courses, consultations, or discount codes? A financial interest does not make a claim false, but it is a reason to look more closely.
- **Are studies named – and can they be found?** Trustworthy claims name a verifiable source: a PMID, a DOI, or a registry number (NCT). You can enter any PMID free of charge at PubMed and read the abstract yourself. Phrases such as "studies show" without specific details are a warning sign.
- **Does the cited study fit the claim?** Check whether the study really examined the substance being sold, the advertised use, and humans – or whether it was about a related molecule, a different disease, or rats.

#### Questions about the study

- **Human, animal, or cell culture?** If the abstract says "rats," "mice," "in vitro," or "cell culture," it says nothing about the effect in humans.
- **Was there a comparison group?** Without a placebo or control group, it is impossible to tell whether an improvement comes from the substance, from the passage of time, from other treatments, or from expectation. The placebo effect is particularly large for pain, mood, or sleep.
- **How many participants?** Studies with 10 or 20 people can provide hints, but cannot prove a benefit or detect rare side effects.
- **What was measured?** A laboratory value that rises is not a noticeable benefit. Ask: was what matters to those affected measured – pain, function, disease events, quality of life?
- **Was the study registered in advance?** Registration at ClinicalTrials.gov or in the EU registry before the study begins prevents goals from being chosen after the fact to fit the results.
- **Was the result replicated?** A single positive result from a single research group is a beginning, not proof.

#### Warning signs in the language

- Promises that cover a great many different complaints at once ("for joints, gut, skin, brain, and immune system").
- Terms such as "miracle cure," "insider tip," "what doctors won't tell you," or "suppressed by the pharmaceutical industry."
- The claim "no side effects" for a substance that has hardly been studied in humans.
- Testimonials and before-and-after pictures instead of study data.
- The notice "for research purposes only" or "not for human consumption" on a product that is simultaneously advertised with effects in humans. This contradiction shows that the seller wants to circumvent drug regulations (see Part I).
- Suggestions for doses, courses, or combination packages of several peptides for unapproved substances. There is no study basis for such regimens.

#### Signs of a trustworthy presentation

- Animal and laboratory results are explicitly labeled as such.
- Negative and contradictory studies are named, not just the positive ones.
- Uncertainty is openly acknowledged ("The data are insufficient to assess …").
- Legal status and doping status are mentioned.
- There is a clear statement that drugs belong in a physician's hands and that gray-market products are not tested.

#### Three questions for everyday use

If you have little time, three questions are often enough:

1. Is the substance approved as a drug in the EU or the US for exactly this use?
2. Is there at least one randomized, placebo-controlled study in humans that has shown the claimed benefit?
3. Does the product come from a pharmacy – or from a source whose contents nobody checks?

If the answer to all three questions is "no," the promise is based on hope, not evidence.

### Conclusion

The peptides most popular on the internet are almost without exception the most poorly studied. Their promises rely predominantly on animal experiments, on surrogate measures, or on studies of a different substance; where randomized studies in humans exist, they have mostly not confirmed the advertised benefit. This does not mean that none of these substances could ever have a benefit – but for none of them are the data sufficient to prove it. If you hear a claim about a peptide, ask for the source, check the study, and keep in mind the difference between a tested drug and a vial from the gray market.
