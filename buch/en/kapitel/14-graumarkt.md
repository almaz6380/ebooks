## The Gray Market

If you search for peptides on the internet, you quickly end up at online shops with professional-looking websites, laboratory photos, and purity certificates. On offer are vials of white powder: BPC-157, TB-500, ipamorelin, melanotan II – and increasingly also semaglutide, tirzepatide, or drugs that are not yet approved at all, such as retatrutide. Almost always, somewhere there is the notice "Research Use Only" or "not for human consumption."

This chapter describes what is known about this market: from laboratory analyses of purchased and seized products, from case reports of poisonings, and from analyses of adverse event reports. It is meant to help you assess the risks realistically. As a matter of principle, this book does not name sources of supply.

### What "Research Peptides" Are

"Research peptides" or "research chemicals" are substances that are officially sold only to laboratories for research purposes. The label "not for human use" is meant to provide legal cover for the sale: a product that is not offered as a medicine – according to the vendors' logic – does not need a marketing authorization as a medicine. In practice, however, many of these shops are recognizably aimed at private individuals who want to inject the substances themselves. Whether this construction holds up legally is discussed in the next chapter; in many cases it does not.

One review describes the pattern using semaglutide and tirzepatide as examples: manufacturers sell vials "for research purposes only" or "not for human consumption" directly to consumers, often without the materials and knowledge needed to reconstitute, draw up, and inject the drug. This bypasses physicians and pharmacies – and with them the check for interactions and medical monitoring (Belcourt 2025, PMID 39285774).

How difficult use is for lay people was investigated by a small study at a US university. Twenty adults were asked to carry out the steps needed to reconstitute such a semaglutide powder and prepare a syringe, using information from the internet. Of 34 steps, they performed on average 15 correctly. The researchers observed errors that could lead to incorrect dosing, needlestick injuries, or infections (Sedensky 2026, PMID 42541373).

### What Is in the Vials: Laboratory Analyses

The most reliable information on gray-market products comes from laboratory analyses. These are carried out mainly by anti-doping laboratories, medicines regulators, and individual research groups that purchase products specifically or receive them from customs and police.

#### Semaglutide from Illegal Online Pharmacies

A particularly thorough investigation combined a market analysis with test purchases (Ashraf 2024, PMID 39509151). The researchers evaluated 1,080 links from search engines. 317 led to online pharmacies, 134 of them to 59 different illegal websites. The 30 most important domains linked to illegal pharmacies recorded more than 4.7 million visits in three months of 2023.

Test purchases were made from six illegal vendors. The results:

- Three ordered Ozempic prefilled pens never arrived – these were scams with no delivery.
- Three vials of powder were delivered. All were considered likely substandard or counterfeit; on visual inspection, they failed 59 to 63% of the criteria.
- The semaglutide content was 28.6 to 38.7% **above** the stated amount.
- The measured purity was only 7.7 to 14.4% – instead of the 99% promised on the label.
- No live microorganisms were found, but endotoxins were detectable in **all** samples.

This study shows several typical problems at once: incorrect quantity information, low purity, contamination, and plain fraud.

#### Copycat Products Compared with the Original

Another investigation analyzed 16 injectable and 8 oral semaglutide copycat products as well as 2 liraglutide copycats and compared them with the originator products (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). New impurities were found, including larger protein molecules, trace metals, and solvent residues. Several oral preparations contained considerably less semaglutide than stated. In addition, structures were found that, according to computer predictions, could be recognized by the immune system. Since the study comes from the manufacturer of the originator products, it should be read with appropriate caution; however, the problems described are consistent with independent findings.

#### Melanotan II from Online Shops

For the tanning peptide melanotan II, a research group examined vials from three online shops. All shops stated 10 mg per vial; in fact, the vials contained between 4.32 and 8.84 mg. Products from two shops contained unknown impurities of 4.1 to 5.9% (Breindahl 2015, PMID 24771717).

#### Seized Products from the Doping Scene

The Cologne anti-doping laboratory has been analyzing seized products for years. In a report on the year 2009, it described, among other things, vials labeled as growth hormone with no pharmacologically active content at all, unpurified Long-R3-IGF-1, and dietary supplements containing the growth hormone-releasing peptide GHRP-2 (Kohler 2010, PMID 21204286). In tablets offered as an over-the-counter dietary supplement, about 50 micrograms of GHRP-2 per tablet were measured (Thomas 2010, PMID 20878896) – a substance that is prohibited in sport and for which there is no approval.

Between 2010 and 2013, the same laboratory analyzed 337 further products. Peptide hormones and growth factors accounted for 12.8%, mainly growth hormone and growth hormone-releasing peptides. Notable findings included thymosin beta-4 and a fusion protein with unknown biological activity (Krug 2014, PMID 25168622). In Norway, 28% of the products seized and analyzed between 2011 and 2014 contained peptide- or protein-based doping agents (Hullstein 2015, PMID 26607218).

A later Cologne report describes modified variants of known peptides that do not exist as medicines: Gly-GHRP-6, Gly-GHRP-2, and Gly-ipamorelin – i.e., the respective peptides with an additional glycine amino acid at the beginning – as well as a slightly altered form of growth hormone (Krug 2018, PMID 29864719). Such modifications may be attempts to evade doping tests; their effects and safety in humans are unknown.

BPC-157 has also been identified in seized vials. A research group then developed a detection method for urine samples (Cox 2017, PMID 28035768).

One review summarizes that counterfeit peptide and protein medicines have repeatedly been found to contain the wrong drug, the wrong amount, or no drug at all; that toxic impurities and manufacturing by-products have caused harm to health; and that seizures have also turned up substances that had failed in clinical trials or were still in the animal-testing phase (Janvier 2018, PMID 30165334).

### Overview of the Typical Quality Problems

A recurring picture emerges from these analyses:

| Problem | Consequence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Too much drug | overdose, more side effects | semaglutide up to 38.7% above label |
| Too little drug | lack of effect | melanotan II 4.32 instead of 10 mg |
| Wrong drug | unpredictable, sometimes life-threatening effects | insulin instead of semaglutide |
| No drug | fraud, lack of effect | empty "growth hormone" vials |
| Impurities | immune reactions, toxic effects | synthesis by-products |
| Endotoxins | fever, inflammation, circulatory reactions | in all semaglutide test purchases |

### Purity: What the Percentage Means – and What It Does Not

Many vendors advertise "99% purity" and enclose certificates of analysis. A few points on this:

- **Purity measures only certain impurities.** A typical measurement (high-performance liquid chromatography, HPLC) shows what percentage of the measurable peptide signals is accounted for by the desired substance. Salts, water, solvent residues, metals, or endotoxins are often not captured.
- **Purity says nothing about quantity.** A vial can contain a very pure substance, but in the wrong amount.
- **Purity says nothing about sterility.** A powder can be chemically pure and still be contaminated with bacteria or bacterial components.
- **Certificates cannot be verified.** As a buyer, you cannot determine whether an enclosed certificate actually belongs to the vial delivered. The semaglutide test purchases showed a purity of 7.7 to 14.4% despite a label stating 99% (Ashraf 2024, PMID 39509151).

In addition, there are impurities that typically arise during chemical synthesis: peptides with missing or duplicated building blocks, misfolded or chemically altered variants, residues of protecting groups and reagents. For approved medicines, limits for such impurities are specified and regularly checked.

### Endotoxins: Invisible and Dangerous

Endotoxins are components of the cell wall of certain bacteria. They persist even when the bacteria have been killed and are very heat-stable. If they enter the blood, they can trigger fever, chills, a drop in blood pressure, and in larger amounts a life-threatening shock. For this reason, strict limits apply to injections and infusions, and drug manufacturers test every batch.

A powder that is "sterile" or "germ-free" can nevertheless contain endotoxins – this is exactly what the semaglutide test purchases showed: no live microorganisms, but endotoxins in all samples (Ashraf 2024, PMID 39509151). Reconstituting with non-sterile water, drawing repeatedly from the same vial, or reusing needles can also introduce germs.

### Dosing Errors

Even if a product contains exactly what is on the label, correct dosing on the gray market is difficult. Powder has to be reconstituted with a specific amount of liquid; the dose then results from a conversion. Anyone who miscalculates or confuses units can easily inject many times the intended amount.

A case series from a US poison control center describes three cases involving semaglutide from pharmacies and a beauty salon. Two people had accidentally injected ten times the amount. All had nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain for days. One person had received a vial with syringes without any counseling; another confused milliliters, "units," and milligrams (Lambson 2023, PMID 37392810). The authors emphasize that, unlike prefilled pens, vials offer no safety mechanisms against such errors.

### Counterfeits of Ozempic and Other GLP-1 Medications

With the enormous demand for semaglutide, counterfeits appeared worldwide. In 2024, the World Health Organization warned about counterfeit Ozempic in the global supply chain (Ruder 2024, PMID 39028647).

Counterfeits that contain a different drug are particularly dangerous. In Italy, a 31-year-old woman was brought to the emergency department in a hypoglycemic coma after injecting supposed Ozempic bought on the internet. Toxicological analysis revealed that the vial contained insulin instead of semaglutide (Antonacci 2026, PMID 41125326). Two similar cases were reported from the Netherlands: two people without diabetes and without obesity had injected semaglutide obtained without a prescription and were in a coma with severe hypoglycemia, potassium deficiency, and hypothermia. The cause was poisoning with synthetic insulin. Continuous glucose infusion for up to two days was needed to keep blood sugar stable (Stammers 2026, PMID 42390457).

These cases are so significant because genuine semaglutide alone hardly causes any appreciable hypoglycemia in healthy people. Hypoglycemia after a "weight-loss injection" is therefore a warning sign of a counterfeit product.

An analysis of the European adverse event database EudraVigilance found a total of 234 reports of possibly counterfeit semaglutide from 2018 to 2025. 73.5% concerned women, and 89.3% of the reported side effects were serious. Vomiting, nausea, and hypoglycemia were reported most frequently. Compared with genuine semaglutide, hypoglycemia, use outside the approved indication, malaise, and lack of effect were reported disproportionately often (Zinzi 2026, PMID 42137313). As with all reporting data: they show signals, not frequencies.

### Unapproved GLP-1 Drugs

Drugs that are not yet approved at all, such as the triple agonist retatrutide, are now also being offered on the gray market. For such substances there are studies by the manufacturers, but no tested products – what is actually in a vial labeled "retatrutide" is unknown. In 2026, a medical journal devoted a dedicated report to this black market (Rubin 2026, PMID 42853576).

One case report describes a woman in her mid-twenties who came to the hospital with five days of persistent nausea, repeated vomiting, and diarrhea. Her symptoms initially did not improve despite several anti-nausea medications. Only through repeated questioning did it emerge that a self-styled life coach without medical qualifications had given her four weekly injections of a product that allegedly contained retatrutide. She herself had not regarded the injections as medication (Buzzi 2026, PMID 42843917). The case illustrates another problem: people who do not perceive peptides as medicines do not mention them to their physicians either.

### Dangers Beyond Contamination

Even a perfectly pure gray-market peptide can be dangerous if the substance itself has never been adequately studied in humans. One case report describes a 39-year-old man who injected melanotan II bought on the internet – by his own account, six times the starting amount that had been recommended to him. He developed a racing heart, agitation, sweating, and massive muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) with kidney dysfunction and had to be treated in the intensive care unit. Analysis confirmed that the product actually contained melanotan II (Nelson 2012, PMID 23121206).

Reviews list possible risks of unregulated peptides including contamination, inaccurate dosing, pathological formation of new blood vessels, carcinogenesis, strain on the cardiovascular system, and excessive hormone signals (Moiz 2026, PMID 42757290). Many of these risks are derived theoretically, precisely because there are hardly any human studies. A sports medicine review concludes that many unapproved peptides show favorable effects in animal models, but that robust human safety data are scarce and serious harm is possible (Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639).

### Compounding in the USA

A special feature of the US market is **compounding**: under certain conditions, pharmacies may prepare medicines themselves, for example when an approved preparation is unavailable. During the shortages of semaglutide and tirzepatide, a large market thus emerged for copycat preparations, often distributed via telemedicine providers. These preparations are not approved medicines; their efficacy and safety have not been tested in studies.

An analysis of the US adverse event database FAERS compared reports on compounded and approved GLP-1 preparations. Of 81,078 reports, 707 concerned compounded products. For these, abdominal pain, gallbladder inflammation, compounding errors, and contamination, among others, were reported disproportionately often, as were hospitalizations; administration and dosing errors, on the other hand, were reported less often (McCall 2026, PMID 40285721). The authors emphasize that such data do not prove a cause-and-effect relationship.

Since the end of the shortages, under US law pharmacies may only produce preparations that differ from the approved ones – for example, through a different dosage form or additives. One investigation found 33 such products, including combinations with vitamin B12, glycine, niacinamide, a laxative, or an anti-nausea drug, as well as drops and orally disintegrating tablets for use under the tongue. For vitamins and the laxative, the authors found hardly any rationale. For the addition of the anti-nausea drug and for the new dosage forms, they see an understandable idea, but no evidence of efficacy and safety (Belcourt 2026, PMID 41689811).

Compounding is not a gray market in the narrower sense: the pharmacies are licensed and subject to oversight. For patients, however, the boundary becomes blurred when products are distributed via online platforms with little medical supervision. There is no comparable system in Europe; here, preparation in the pharmacy is subject to considerably narrower conditions (more on this in the next chapter).

### Who Uses the Gray Market – and Why

The reasons for turning to the gray market are varied: high prices and lack of reimbursement for approved medicines, supply shortages, the desire for weight loss without a medical indication, performance enhancement in sport, hope for faster healing of injuries, or for a longer life. One review describes how online advertising, testimonials on social media, self-injection, combinations of several substances, and weak adverse event monitoring interact and normalize products of unclear nature (Hailu 2026, PMID 42437212).

These motives are understandable. The analyses in this chapter show, however, that buyers on the gray market cannot know what they are actually getting.

### If You Have Already Used Gray-Market Peptides

This book gives no instructions for use. However, some general points are important for safety reasons:

- Inform your physician about all injections, peptides, and "research chemicals" you use – even if you do not regard them as medication.
- Unexpected symptoms such as trembling, sweating, confusion, or impaired consciousness after an injection can indicate hypoglycemia and are an emergency.
- Suspicious products and side effects can be reported to the authorities: in Germany to the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM), in Austria to the Federal Office for Safety in Health Care (BASG).

### Key Points in Brief

- The notice "for research purposes only" does not protect against risks; it shifts responsibility to the buyers.
- Laboratory analyses of gray-market peptides found too much, too little, the wrong, or no drug at all, as well as impurities and endotoxins.
- Counterfeit semaglutide products containing insulin led to life-threatening hypoglycemia.
- Compounded preparations from US pharmacies are not tested like approved medicines either.
- Even a pure product remains risky if the drug has never been adequately studied in humans.
