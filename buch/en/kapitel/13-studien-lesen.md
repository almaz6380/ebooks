## Reading Studies Correctly

"Studies show …" – hardly any phrase is used as often in advertising for peptides. It can mean all sorts of things: an experiment with cells in a Petri dish, an experiment on twelve rats, a testimonial from a single patient, or a study with 17,000 participants over more than three years. If you want to evaluate peptides, you need to know these differences. This chapter is a kind of toolbox. It explains the most important study types, the pitfalls in interpreting results, and how you can check for yourself what lies behind a claim.

### The Evidence Pyramid

Experts often arrange study types in a pyramid. At the bottom are study designs that provide many clues but little certainty; at the top are those that show most reliably whether a treatment works in humans. From bottom to top:

1. **Laboratory and cell studies** (in vitro, "in glass"): cells or tissues are exposed to a substance in the laboratory.
2. **Animal studies** (mostly in mice or rats).
3. **Case reports and case series**: descriptions of individual patients or a small number of patients.
4. **Observational studies**: people who take or do not take a substance anyway are observed, and what happens to them is compared.
5. **Controlled studies without random allocation** and **open-label studies**.
6. **Randomized controlled trials** (RCTs).
7. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses**, which summarize all available good studies on a question.

The pyramid is a simplification. A large, careful observational study can be more informative than a tiny, poorly conducted RCT, and a meta-analysis is only as good as the studies that go into it. As a rough guide, however, it is helpful – above all for recognizing how far down the pyramid the evidence for many gray-market peptides lies.

### Laboratory Studies: What Happens in the Petri Dish

Mechanisms can be studied well in cell cultures: Which receptor is activated? Which genes are read? Do cells grow or migrate faster? Such studies are indispensable for basic research.

But their limitations are considerable. Cells in a dish have no circulation, no liver that breaks down substances, no immune system, and no digestion. The concentrations used in the laboratory are often much higher than those that could ever be reached in the human body. A substance that kills cancer cells in a Petri dish may be ineffective or toxic in the body. If an advertising text refers to "studies" and these are cell cultures, this is an indication of a possible mechanism – not of a benefit in humans.

### Animal Studies: Important, but Not Transferable

Animal experiments make it possible to study effects in a whole organism and to clarify safety questions before people are exposed to a new substance. At the same time, mice and rats differ considerably from humans in metabolism, lifespan, body size, and disease development. In addition, diseases in animals are often induced artificially – for example, a tendon injury by an incision – which corresponds only partly to the situation in humans.

How difficult the transfer is was shown by a systematic review that compared treatment effects in animal experiments with those in clinical trials (Perel 2007, PMID 17175568). Some examples from it:

- Corticosteroid preparations improved outcomes after head injury in animal models; in clinical trials in humans, no benefit was seen.
- The drug tirilazad reduced the damaged brain area in animals with stroke by 29% and improved neurological scores by 48%. In people with stroke, it was associated with a worse outcome.
- Other treatments, such as dissolving blood clots in stroke, worked in both animals and humans.

The authors attribute the discrepancies to biases in the animal studies and to the fact that animal models do not adequately reflect human diseases.

For this book, this has a clear consequence: if only animal data are available for a peptide, it receives evidence level D – regardless of how impressive the results in rats look. And the text always states explicitly when a result comes from animals or from the laboratory.

### Case Reports and Observational Studies

**Case reports** describe individual patients. They say little about efficacy, but they can be very valuable for safety: rare or unexpected side effects are often first noticed in case reports. In the chapter on the gray market, you will encounter several such reports, for example on severe hypoglycemia caused by counterfeit preparations.

**Observational studies** compare people who take a substance with people who do not. Their main problem is so-called **confounding**: people who take a particular preparation often also differ in other respects – for example, in income, health awareness, or pre-existing conditions. An association that is found therefore need not be a cause-and-effect relationship.

A special form is the analysis of **adverse event reporting systems** such as the European database EudraVigilance or the US FAERS. They collect reports of suspected side effects from physicians, patients, and manufacturers. Such data reveal signals that should be investigated further, but they do not prove a connection and say nothing about how common a side effect actually is.

### The Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)

The RCT is considered the gold standard for testing the effect of a treatment. It is defined by three elements:

**Control group.** In addition to the group that receives the drug, there is a comparison group – with placebo, a standard treatment, or no treatment at all. Only in this way can one say whether a change is due to the drug or would have occurred without it.

**Randomization.** Chance decides who goes into which group. As a result, known and unknown influencing factors are distributed evenly between the groups. Differences in outcome can then be attributed to the treatment with high probability.

**Blinding.** In a **double-blind** study, neither the participants nor the study staff know who is receiving the drug and who is receiving the placebo. This prevents expectations from influencing the result. In an **open-label** study, everyone involved knows; sometimes this is unavoidable, but it makes the results more susceptible to bias, especially with subjective endpoints such as pain, mood, or skin appearance.

### The Placebo Effect and Why Control Groups Are Indispensable

People in studies often feel better even if they receive only a dummy preparation. This is due to expectations, to the extra attention, to lifestyle changes during a study, and to the fact that many complaints improve on their own anyway.

The STEP 1 trial provides a vivid example: the placebo group also lost on average 2.4% of its body weight over 68 weeks, because all participants additionally received lifestyle counseling (Wilding 2021, PMID 33567185). The actual effect of the drug is the difference between the groups – in this case 12.4 percentage points, not 14.9%.

A review of peptides in sports medicine explicitly points out that the placebo effect could explain part of the reported effects of unregulated peptides and that social media amplify this effect (Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639). Testimonials from forums are therefore no substitute for controlled studies.

### Study Phases

New medicines typically pass through four phases in humans:

| Phase | Aim | Typical size |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | tolerability, behavior in the body | a few dozen healthy people |
| Phase 2 | first efficacy, dose finding | about 100 to a few hundred |
| Phase 3 | efficacy and safety in comparison | hundreds to thousands |
| Phase 4 | monitoring after approval | often very large |

Many drugs fail between phase 2 and phase 3. A promising phase 2 result is therefore no guarantee of later approval.

### Endpoints: What Is Actually Being Measured?

Every study defines a **primary endpoint** in advance – the main goal by which success is measured. In addition, there are secondary endpoints. If a result is highlighted as important only after the fact, caution is warranted: anyone who measures many endpoints will almost always find a "significant" difference somewhere.

The distinction between **hard endpoints** and **surrogate endpoints** is particularly important:

- **Hard endpoints** are events that matter directly to patients: death, heart attack, stroke, bone fracture, blindness, quality of life.
- **Surrogate endpoints** (substitute endpoints) are measurements that are assumed to be related to hard endpoints: blood sugar, HbA1c, cholesterol, blood pressure, bone density, body weight, or laboratory values such as IGF-1.

Surrogate endpoints are practical because they can be measured more quickly and with fewer participants. But they can be misleading. The best-known example is the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST) from the late 1980s. It was assumed that medications that suppress certain cardiac arrhythmias after a heart attack would also prevent deaths. The medications did indeed suppress the arrhythmias – the surrogate endpoint was achieved. But in the randomized study, more people died on two of these medications than on placebo: 43 participants on the drug died of arrhythmias, compared with 16 on placebo (Echt 1991, PMID 1900101).

With peptides, you will encounter surrogate endpoints constantly. For growth hormone secretagogues, often only whether the level of growth hormone or IGF-1 rises is measured – not whether people become healthier, stronger, or live longer as a result. For the GLP-1 medications, by contrast, there are now large studies with hard endpoints such as heart attack and stroke. This is one of the reasons why they receive the highest evidence level in this book.

### Relative and Absolute Risks

Hardly any statistical detail is misunderstood as often as this one. Take the SELECT trial: over a mean of 39.8 months, the composite cardiovascular event occurred in 6.5% of participants on semaglutide and in 8.0% on placebo. The hazard ratio – a measure of relative risk over time – was 0.80 (Lincoff 2023, PMID 37952131).

Two statements can be derived from this, both of which are correct:

- **Relative:** The risk was about 20% lower.
- **Absolute:** The proportion of those affected fell by 1.5 percentage points – from 8.0 to 6.5 out of 100 people.

From the absolute difference, the **number needed to treat (NNT)** can be calculated: how many people must be treated for one of them to avoid an event. Here, arithmetically, that is about 67 people over a good three years. This does not mean that the treatment is unimportant – with millions of people affected, the effect is significant. But it shows that most of those treated would not have had an event even without the medication, and that some have one despite the medication.

Advertising and headlines prefer relative figures because they sound bigger. Good study reports state both. So always pay attention to the baseline frequency: a "halving" of the risk from 2 to 1 in 10,000 is something completely different from 20 to 10 in 100.

The same applies to side effects. In SELECT, 16.6% of participants on semaglutide discontinued treatment because of adverse events, compared with 8.2% on placebo (Lincoff 2023, PMID 37952131). In relative terms, that is a doubling; in absolute terms, a difference of 8.4 percentage points.

### Statistical Significance and Confidence Intervals

A **p-value** indicates how likely a difference at least as large would be if in reality there were no effect. If it is less than 0.05, a result is usually considered "statistically significant." This does not mean that the effect is large or meaningful – in very large studies, even tiny differences become significant.

The **95% confidence interval** is often more informative. It gives a range within which the true effect lies with high probability. In STEP 1, the difference in body weight between semaglutide and placebo was 12.4 percentage points, with a confidence interval of 11.5 to 13.4 (Wilding 2021, PMID 33567185). Such a narrow interval indicates a precise estimate. In small studies, the intervals are often very wide – a sign that the true effect could be large, small, or even zero.

### How to Count: The Question of the Estimand

A detail that has become important in more recent studies: how are people counted who discontinue treatment or take additional medications? In the PIONEER 1 trial, two analyses were reported. If all participants are analyzed, regardless of whether they continued taking the tablets, HbA1c fell at the highest dose by 1.1 percentage points more than on placebo. If only the time during which the tablets were actually taken is considered, the figure was 1.4 percentage points (Aroda 2019, PMID 31186300). Both figures are correct but answer different questions. The first is usually more realistic for everyday life.

### Duration and Size

Many peptide studies are short. A change in laboratory values after two weeks says little about what happens after two years – neither about the benefit nor about risks such as cancer, which often appear only after years. Size is just as important: rare side effects that affect one in a thousand people inevitably remain undetected in a study with 40 participants. The absence of side effects in a small study is not proof of safety.

From theoretical considerations and simulations, the epidemiologist John Ioannidis concluded that research findings are less reliable the smaller the studies and the effects, the more questions are tested without preselection, the more flexible the design and analysis, and the greater the financial interests and prejudices in a field (Ioannidis 2005, PMID 16060722). Many of these characteristics apply to research on poorly studied peptides.

### Conflicts of Interest

The large pivotal trials are almost always funded by the manufacturers. This is provided for by law and is not automatically a flaw – these studies are subject to strict rules, are registered in advance, and are reviewed by the authorities. Nevertheless, it is worth looking at the funding. A Cochrane review concluded that industry-sponsored studies more often reported favorable efficacy results (relative risk 1.27) and more often favorable conclusions (relative risk 1.34) than studies with other funding (Lundh 2017, PMID 28207928).

Conflicts of interest do not exist only at pharmaceutical companies. Researchers who discovered a substance and hold patents, clinics that sell peptide therapies, or influencers with discount codes also have an interest in positive results. It is a warning sign when almost all studies on a substance come from a single research group and have not been confirmed by independent teams.

### The Problem of Vanishing Studies

Not every study is published – and it is mainly the disappointing ones that disappear. How strongly this can distort the picture was shown by an investigation of antidepressants. Of 74 studies registered with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), 31% were not published. According to the published literature, 94% of the studies were positive; according to the FDA's analysis, only 51% (Turner 2008, PMID 18199864). Anyone who reads only the journals gets an overly rosy picture.

### Study Registries

In response to such findings, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors declared in 2004 that clinical trials must be entered in a public registry before they begin in order to be published later (DeAngelis 2004, PMID 15355936). Today, registries are a central tool:

- **ClinicalTrials.gov** (USA) is the largest registry. Each study receives a number in the format "NCT" plus eight digits. The SELECT trial, for example, has the number NCT03574597.
- In the **EU**, clinical trials are recorded in the Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) of the European Medicines Agency; older studies can be found in the EU Clinical Trials Register.
- The **World Health Organization** brings together many national registries in a search portal (ICTRP).

Among other things, the registry lists the planned study design, the number of participants, the primary endpoint, and the status. This makes it possible to check whether a published study reports its originally planned endpoints – and whether studies on a substance were started but never published. For this book, registries were used primarily to find ongoing studies. Important: a registry entry is not a result. The fact that a study is registered says nothing about whether the substance works.

### Other Warning Signs

- **Only a conference abstract or a preprint.** Advance publications (for example on medRxiv) and conference contributions have not yet been fully peer-reviewed.
- **Journals with questionable standards.** Some journals publish almost anything for a fee.
- **Studies that cannot be found.** If a cited study cannot be found in PubMed or a registry, caution is warranted.
- **Transfer from sick people to healthy people.** A substance that helps with a particular disease need not have any benefit in healthy people – the risk-benefit ratio there is completely different.
- **Transfer from one substance to another.** Results on semaglutide do not automatically apply to a gray-market product labeled "semaglutide" – and studies on thymosin beta-4 do not automatically apply to the fragment "TB-500."

### Ten Questions to Ask of Any Study

1. Were humans studied – or animals or cells?
2. Was there a control group, and was allocation random?
3. Was the study blinded?
4. How many participants were there, and how long were they followed?
5. Who was studied – sick or healthy people, young or old?
6. What was the primary endpoint: a hard endpoint or a surrogate?
7. How large was the difference in absolute terms, not just relative terms?
8. How many dropped out, and which side effects occurred?
9. Who paid for the study, and were the results confirmed independently?
10. Was the study registered in advance, and do the reported endpoints match the registry?

### How This Book Grades the Evidence

Each peptide in Part II receives an evidence level:

- **A**: several large randomized studies in humans, approval as a medicine.
- **B**: individual randomized studies in humans.
- **C**: only small or uncontrolled human studies.
- **D**: only animal and laboratory studies.

The level assesses the **quantity and quality of data in humans**, not whether a substance is "good" or "bad." A peptide at level A can have considerable side effects, and a peptide at level D may well prove useful in the future – it simply has not yet been adequately studied. The grading also applies to a specific use: a substance can be well supported for one disease and not at all for another.

### Key Points in Brief

- Laboratory and animal studies provide clues to mechanisms but no proof of benefit in humans.
- The randomized, controlled, blinded comparison is the most reliable way to demonstrate an effect.
- Surrogate endpoints such as laboratory values can be misleading; hard endpoints are what count.
- Relative figures sound bigger than absolute ones. Always ask about the baseline frequency.
- Funding, publication bias, and lack of confirmation by independent groups can distort the picture. Study registries help to check this.
