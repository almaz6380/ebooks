## IGF-1 LR3

IGF-1 LR3 (in full, "Long-R3-IGF-I") is not a medicine but a laboratory reagent. It was developed to help cell cultures grow better in biotechnology and is used in animal experiments. The fact that it is sold on the internet in vials for injection is a prime example of the "research peptide" market: a substance that was never intended for humans, has never been tested in humans, and whose properties amplify precisely the risks that are a concern with natural IGF-1.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** modified human IGF-1: position 3 carries the amino acid arginine ("R3"), and an additional amino acid chain has been attached at the beginning ("Long"). Both greatly reduce binding to the IGF-binding proteins (Bastian 1993, PMID 7693845; Tomas 1993, PMID 7683875).
- **Origin:** described as a research tool in the early 1990s; today commercially available as an additive for cell culture media.
- **Mechanism of action:** binds to the IGF-1 receptor like IGF-1; because it is hardly captured by binding proteins, it circulates in the blood mostly free and therefore directly active.
- **EU approval:** no. **US approval:** no. Not approved as a medicine anywhere, nor ever applied for.
- **WADA:** prohibited, class S2 (IGF-1 analogs as growth factors).
- **Evidence level: D** – not a single study in humans; only animal and laboratory studies.

### How it is supposed to work

In the blood, almost all IGF-1 is coupled to binding proteins, mainly IGFBP-3. These proteins act like a buffer: they extend the lifespan of IGF-1 but also keep it away from cells and thus dampen its effect. In cell experiments, IGFBP-3 inhibited the activation of the IGF-1 receptor by IGF-1 and also by Long-R3-IGF-I by capturing the messengers (Devi 2001, PMID 11735239).

LR3 was modified so that it largely escapes this buffer. In rats, it was present in the blood mostly as free peptide and – in nonpregnant animals – was removed from the blood about ten times faster than IGF-1, meaning it disappears rapidly into the tissues (Bastian 1993, PMID 7693845). In diabetic rats, it was 2.5 to 3 times as effective as IGF-1 in restoring growth (Tomas 1993, PMID 7683875). On the gray market, this gives rise to the expectation that LR3 builds more muscle in humans than natural IGF-1. There is no evidence for this.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** This book's automated search found **no** human study in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`: 0) and **no** entry on ClinicalTrials.gov. A targeted follow-up search for pilot studies or case reports of poisoning or side effects also found no publication in which humans received IGF-1 LR3.

All that was found were:

- **Gray-market analyses:** A doping laboratory found Long-R3-IGF-I with a so-called His-tag in an injection vial from the black market – an appendage of six histidine building blocks used for purification in the production of laboratory proteins. The authors suspect that it was a by-product from biochemical research, not a preparation made for injection; the effect of this variant in humans is completely unknown (Kohler 2010, PMID 20675162). Another research group found numerous signs of low-quality, oxidized peptide forms in black-market products (Mongongu 2021, PMID 33587816).
- **Doping reviews:** IGF-1 and its analogs are advertised on the internet and have been available on the black market for years (Guha 2013, PMID 23934394).

### Animal and laboratory studies

Almost all research on LR3 comes from livestock and developmental biology:

- **Rats:** rapid distribution into tissue, mostly as free peptide (Bastian 1993, PMID 7693845); stronger growth effect than IGF-1 in diabetic rats, without reducing glucose excretion in urine (Tomas 1993, PMID 7683875).
- **Pigs:** A four-day infusion **reduced** weight gain and feed intake in fattening pigs and suppressed the endogenous levels of growth hormone, IGF-1, IGFBP-3 and insulin (Dunaiski 1997, PMID 9488001).
- **Guinea pigs:** Organs such as the intestine, kidneys, spleen and adrenal glands became heavier, but overall body growth did not increase (Conlon 1995, PMID 7561636).
- **Sheep fetuses:** In normally growing fetuses, LR3 lowered insulin secretion; a short infusion reduced insulin levels during a glucose challenge by 66% (White 2023, PMID 37114757). In growth-restricted fetuses, a one-week treatment did not improve growth (White 2025, PMID 39679943).
- **Mice:** In an Alzheimer's model, LR3 given as a nasal spray altered amyloid deposits but did not prevent memory loss (Engel 2025, PMID 39610283).

The animal data are contradictory: depending on the species, LR3 promoted growth, had no effect, or even inhibited growth. Extrapolation to humans is not possible.

### Risks and side effects

Because there are no human studies, the risks can only be inferred from biology and from experience with natural IGF-1 (mecasermin). These inferences are **theoretical** but well founded:

- **Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar):** At higher doses, IGF-1 acts similarly to insulin. With the approved mecasermin, hypoglycemia is the most common side effect, even though the buffer of the binding proteins is largely preserved there (Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294). An analog that specifically escapes this buffer could act more strongly and less predictably. This has not been measured in humans. In the animal experiments, insulin levels sometimes fell (Dunaiski 1997, PMID 9488001; White 2023, PMID 37114757), which could further alter blood glucose regulation.
- **Growth of organs and tissue:** In guinea pigs, internal organs enlarged (Conlon 1995, PMID 7561636). In humans it is unclear whether something similar happens.
- **Cancer theory:** IGF-1 promotes cell division and cell survival. Higher IGF-1 levels are associated in large observational and genetic studies with a somewhat higher risk of breast and prostate cancer (Renehan 2004, PMID 15110491; Murphy 2020, PMID 32169310; Watts 2023, PMID 35726641), and people with congenital IGF-1 deficiency develop cancer strikingly rarely (Guevara-Aguirre 2011, PMID 21325617). An analog that acts more strongly and without buffering at the IGF-1 receptor amplifies precisely this signaling pathway. Whether this measurably increases cancer risk in humans has not been studied – and, given the lack of studies, will not be. The concern is based on plausibility, not on case reports.
- **Product quality:** The black-market preparations found contained laboratory variants with a purification tag and oxidized peptides (Kohler 2010, PMID 20675162; Mongongu 2021, PMID 33587816). Added to this are the general risks of nonsterile injections. Cell culture reagents are not subject to drug quality control.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** not a medicine, no approval, not applied for either. It may be sold as a laboratory reagent for research; supplying it for use in humans is prohibited under medicines laws.
- **USA:** no approval; distributed only as a research chemical.
- **WADA:** prohibited (S2). A detection method for LR3 and related IGF-1 analogs in blood has been developed; in rats, unchanged LR3 disappeared after only about 4 hours, while metabolites were detectable for up to 16 hours (Mongongu 2021, PMID 33587816).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

As of October 2026, no study with IGF-1 LR3 is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. *Author's assessment:* Development into a medicine is not to be expected. LR3 will remain a tool for cell culture and animal research.

### Conclusion

IGF-1 LR3 is a laboratory reagent that has never been studied in humans. In animal experiments, depending on the species, it promoted growth, had no effect, or even inhibited growth. Its special property – the lack of binding to the buffering binding proteins – suggests a higher risk of hypoglycemia and amplified growth signaling. Black-market products have been shown to contain laboratory variants that were never intended for injection.
