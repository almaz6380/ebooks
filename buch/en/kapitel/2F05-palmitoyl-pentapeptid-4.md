## Palmitoyl Pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** pentapeptide of lysine, threonine, threonine, lysine and serine (KTTKS) to which palmitic acid (a fatty acid with 16 carbon atoms) is coupled. Short name Pal-KTTKS; formerly listed as palmitoyl pentapeptide-3. Trade name of the raw material: Matrixyl.
- **Origin:** synthetic. The KTTKS sequence comes from the precursor molecule of type I collagen (procollagen). The fatty acid attachment is supposed to improve penetration into the skin.
- **Mechanism of action:** as a "matrikine" – a fragment of the connective tissue matrix with a signaling effect – it is supposed to stimulate fibroblasts in the skin to produce collagen and other matrix components.
- **Distinction:** "Matrixyl 3000" is a different product, a combination of palmitoyl tripeptide-1 (Pal-GHK, see chapter GHK-Cu) and palmitoyl tetrapeptide-7.
- **Approval EU:** not a medicine; cosmetic ingredient.
- **Approval USA:** not a drug; cosmetic ingredient.
- **WADA:** not listed; as a cosmetic ingredient irrelevant under sports law (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** (weak) – one larger randomized split-face study from 2005 and one very small randomized study; no independent confirmatory studies.

### How it is supposed to work

When connective tissue is injured, enzymes break down collagen and other matrix components into fragments. Some of these fragments act as signals: they indicate to the connective tissue cells that repair is needed. Such signaling fragments are called matrikines.

In 1993, a research group described that the pentapeptide KTTKS – a small piece from the end of type I procollagen – strongly increases the production of type I and type III collagen and fibronectin in fibroblast cultures. KTTKS was the smallest sequence that still triggered this effect (Katayama 1993, PMID 8486721). The cosmetics industry took up the idea: a peptide that signals to the skin that "repair is needed" could boost collagen production, which declines with age.

However, pure KTTKS barely penetrates the skin. Attaching a fatty acid (palmitoylation) makes the molecule more fat-soluble. In cell culture, Pal-KTTKS stimulated collagen production by skin and corneal fibroblasts in a concentration-dependent manner; the effect occurred close to the concentration at which the molecules assemble into ribbon-like nanostructures (Jones 2013, PMID 23320752).

It remains open how much Pal-KTTKS from a cream actually reaches the dermis, where the fibroblasts are located. For the related Pal-GHK, a study in volunteers showed that after application it spreads only between the cells of the stratum corneum and decreases with depth (Trzaska 2026, PMID 42790608). For Pal-KTTKS itself, comparable measurements in humans are lacking in the literature evaluated.

### Human studies

The PubMed search evaluated for this book (palmitoyl pentapeptide or Matrixyl in title or abstract, search date October 2026) yielded only **one** paper classified as a human study; ClinicalTrials.gov lists no registry entry. Manual review found two further controlled studies as well as studies with combination products.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robinson 2005, PMID 18492182 | 93 fair-skinned women (35–55 yrs), moisturizer with Pal-KTTKS on one half of the face vs. the same cream without active ingredient on the other, double-blind | 12 weeks | Significant reduction in wrinkles and fine lines compared with the control side, measured by image analysis and by experts; well tolerated |
| Aruan 2023, PMID 36909866 | 21 Indonesian women (26–55 yrs), cream with palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 vs. acetyl hexapeptide-3 vs. placebo, double-blind | 8 weeks | Improvements "in some participants"; palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 performed better than the comparison groups; no robust effect sizes in the abstract |
| Röper 2004, PMID 15127162 | 20 women with breast cancer undergoing radiotherapy, cream with Matrixyl and other active ingredients vs. dexpanthenol lotion | duration of therapy | No advantage for skin reactions; itching more frequent, one suspected allergic reaction only in the Matrixyl group |

**Robinson 2005** is the central study. The split-face design is clever because each participant is her own control. The concentration in the product was 3 ppm (parts per million). Limitations: the abstract gives no effect sizes; the study was published in a cosmetics journal and has not been repeated independently since. Only fair-skinned women with photoaged skin were studied; whether the results can be transferred to other skin types is open.

**Aruan 2023**, with 21 participants in three groups, was too small for reliable conclusions; the authors themselves describe it as a starting point for larger studies.

**Röper 2004** tested a combination product in a medical context and found no benefit.

**Combination products:** In two split-face studies with 42 and 35 women, care products containing Pal-KTTKS together with niacinamide, another peptide, carnosine and in some cases retinyl propionate improved the smoothness of the skin around the eyes compared with untreated skin (Kaczvinsky 2009, PMID 19735523). Because several active ingredients were tested simultaneously and compared with "no treatment," the effect cannot be attributed to the peptide.

**Meta-analysis:** A meta-analysis of oral and topically applied peptides against skin aging (19 studies, 1,341 participants) found a modest overall effect on wrinkles, which was driven mainly by orally taken peptides (Nukaly 2026, PMID 41924746). This does not provide separate evidence for topically applied peptides such as Pal-KTTKS.

### Animal and laboratory studies

- **Cell culture:** KTTKS and Pal-KTTKS increase the production of collagen and fibronectin in fibroblasts (Katayama 1993, PMID 8486721; Jones 2013, PMID 23320752). In another cell study, Pal-KTTKS at a low concentration reduced the transformation of fibroblasts into scar-forming myofibroblasts; at a higher concentration the effect was not significant (Park 2017, PMID 30603464).
- **Animal experiments:** In experimental animals, patches and creams with Matrixyl improved the healing of skin wounds compared with untreated controls (Kachooeian 2022, PMID 35874243).
- **New variants:** Research groups are coupling KTTKS to other molecules to create antibacterial or more strongly collagen-stimulating variants – so far exclusively in the laboratory.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** In the Robinson study, the peptide was well tolerated. In the radiotherapy study, itching occurred more frequently under the Matrixyl-containing combination product, and a suspected allergic reaction occurred only under this product (Röper 2004, PMID 15127162); which component was responsible is unclear.

**General:** As with any cosmetic ingredient, irritation and contact allergies are possible, but they appear to be rare. A paper from the cosmetics industry that screened peptides for toxic and allergenic potential using bioinformatic tools found similarities between palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 and connective tissue proteins of the skin, but no indications of toxin or allergen structures (Bjerke 2026, PMID 41953401). Such computer analyses do not replace clinical observation.

**Theoretical:** A signaling substance that actually stimulates fibroblasts to produce collagen could be undesirable in people prone to excessive scarring. There are no case reports of this; the low skin penetration argues against a relevant effect.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** Palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 is a permitted cosmetic ingredient under the EU Cosmetics Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009). Cosmetics are not officially tested for efficacy before sale; the manufacturer must prepare a safety assessment and be able to substantiate advertising claims in accordance with Regulation (EU) No 655/2013. Claims such as "works like a wrinkle filler injection" or "builds collagen" are therefore legally delicate if they are not covered by studies on the product.
- **USA:** cosmetic ingredient without prior review by the FDA. Advertising claims that promise a change in skin structure can legally turn a product into a drug.
- **Sport:** irrelevant (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established:** On ClinicalTrials.gov, no study is registered under "palmitoyl pentapeptide" (as of October 2026). Cosmetic efficacy studies are generally not registered but are commissioned by the manufacturers and only partly published.

**Assessment:** Matrixyl is one of the oldest and most widely used "anti-aging peptides" in cosmetics. Its scientific basis – a cell culture study from 1993 and a split-face study from 2005 – is astonishingly narrow for such a widespread product. New developments aim at better skin penetration (microneedles, nanostructures) and at combinations with other peptides. Without independent, sufficiently large studies with the single active ingredient, the state of knowledge will change little.

### Conclusion

Palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 is a fatty acid-coupled fragment of the collagen precursor that stimulates fibroblasts to produce collagen in the laboratory. In humans, its efficacy rests mainly on a split-face study with 93 women from 2005, which showed a reduction in fine lines, and on a very small study from 2023. How much active ingredient actually reaches the deeper skin has not been clarified. As a cosmetic ingredient, it is considered well tolerated. Anyone using Matrixyl products should expect a small, cosmetic effect – not a substitute for UV protection or medically tested active ingredients such as retinoids.
