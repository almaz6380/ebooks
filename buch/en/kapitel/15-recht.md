## Legal Framework

Whether a peptide is a prescription medicine, a dietary supplement, a cosmetic, or a prohibited doping substance does not depend on the molecule alone. What also matters is what it is intended for, how it is offered, and how it acts in the body. The same collagen peptide can sit on the supermarket shelf as a food; a peptide with demonstrable hormone-like activity, by contrast, is almost always a medicine – regardless of what is on the label. This chapter provides an overview of the rules in the European Union, in Austria, in Germany, and in the USA, as well as the anti-doping regulations.

> **Important note:** This chapter reflects the status as of 2026 to the best of our knowledge and does not constitute legal advice. Laws, regulations, administrative practice, and the Prohibited List of the World Anti-Doping Agency change regularly. Please check the current legal situation in each individual case and, for specific questions, consult a lawyer or the competent authority.

### Three Drawers: Medicines, Food, Cosmetics

European law essentially distinguishes three product categories into which peptides can fall. Each has its own rules:

| Category | Regulatory review before sale | Typical peptides |
|---|---|---|
| Medicines | marketing authorization with proof of efficacy and safety | insulin, semaglutide, oxytocin |
| Food and dietary supplements | no authorization, manufacturer responsible | collagen peptides, milk peptides |
| Cosmetics | no authorization, safety assessment by manufacturer | palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, Argireline |

In addition, there are substances that do not lawfully fit into any of these categories – for example, unapproved active substances with pharmacological activity that are sold as "research chemicals." For them, there is no legal route onto the market as a product for human use.

### What a Medicine Is

The definition of a medicinal product is laid down in the EU by a directive (Directive 2001/83/EC) and implemented in the national medicines laws. It has two sides:

- **Medicinal product by presentation**: A product is a medicine if it is **presented** as a means of treating or preventing disease. Even advertising with claims such as "accelerates the healing of tendon injuries" can make a product a medicine.
- **Medicinal product by function**: A product is a medicine if it can restore, correct, or modify human physiological functions through a **pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic action** – regardless of how it is advertised.

If a product falls both under the definition of a medicine and under that of another product, medicines law takes precedence in case of doubt under this directive. For peptides with hormone-like activity – such as growth hormone secretagogues, GLP-1 analogs, or melanotan – this means: they are as a rule medicines, even if they are declared as dietary supplements, cosmetics, or "research chemicals."

### Authorization in the EU

A medicine may only be placed on the market in the EU if it is authorized. For authorization, the manufacturer must submit data on quality, efficacy, and safety – usually from the phase 3 trials described in the chapter "Reading Studies Correctly."

There are two main routes:

- **Centralized procedure** via the European Medicines Agency (EMA). The authorization is then valid in all member states. This route is mandatory for biotechnologically produced medicines and new active substances for certain diseases – including diabetes. Most modern peptide medicines are centrally authorized.
- **National and decentralized procedures** via the authorities of the member states: in Austria the Federal Office for Safety in Health Care (BASG), in Germany the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM) or, for certain biological medicines, the Paul-Ehrlich-Institut.

Together with the authorization, it is determined whether a medicine is **prescription-only**. Almost all peptide medicines are. The exact indications, dosages, contraindications, and side effects are set out in the **summary of product characteristics** (for professionals) and the **package leaflet** (patient information). The authorization applies to specific indications. Under certain conditions, physicians may also prescribe an authorized medicine outside these indications ("off-label use"), but they then bear increased responsibility for informing the patient and weighing the risks; as a rule, the costs are not covered by health insurance.

Some peptides that appear in this book are approved in other countries but not in the EU – for example, some preparations used in Russia. A foreign approval does not make a product marketable in the EU.

### Dietary Supplements

Legally, dietary supplements are foods. They are intended to supplement the normal diet and contain nutrients or other substances with a nutritional or physiological effect (Directive 2002/46/EC). They require **no authorization**. Depending on the country, they must be notified to an authority before first sale; in Germany, for example, to the Federal Office of Consumer Protection and Food Safety. The manufacturer is responsible for safety, and food control authorities carry out spot checks.

Important limits:

- A substance with pharmacological activity cannot be a dietary supplement. If it is nevertheless sold as such, it is an unauthorized medicine.
- **Health claims** such as "supports the joints" or "lowers blood pressure" are only permitted if they have been authorized by the EU after review by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and are included in a list (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006). To our knowledge, there is no authorized claim for many of the advertised effects of peptide foods. Advertising that nevertheless promises such an effect is not permitted.
- **Disease-related claims** (such as "works against osteoarthritis") are generally prohibited for foods.

That dietary supplements are not free of prohibited substances either is shown by a case from an anti-doping laboratory: the growth hormone-releasing peptide GHRP-2 was detected in tablets offered as an over-the-counter dietary supplement (Thomas 2010, PMID 20878896).

### Novel Food

A separate hurdle for foods is the **Novel Food Regulation** (Regulation (EU) 2015/2283). Foods and ingredients that were not used for human consumption to a significant degree in the EU before May 15, 1997, are considered "novel." They may only be sold after a safety assessment and authorization by the EU.

For peptides, this means: collagen hydrolysates from gelatin have a long history of consumption and are not novel foods. A newly synthesized peptide with no history of consumption that is to be sold as a food, on the other hand, would in principle be a novel food and would require authorization – unless it must be classified as a medicine anyway because of its pharmacological activity. Whether a substance counts as a novel food must be examined on a case-by-case basis; the European Commission maintains a catalog for this purpose.

### Cosmetics

Cosmetic products are regulated in the EU by the Cosmetics Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009). They are intended for external application to the skin, hair, nails, lips, teeth, or mucous membranes of the oral cavity – for cleansing, care, protection, or changing appearance.

- Cosmetics require **no authorization**. However, a responsible person in the EU must provide a safety assessment and notify the product in an EU portal before sale.
- Certain substances are prohibited or only permitted with restrictions.
- Advertising claims must be substantiated and must not be misleading. Claims about treating diseases are not permitted.
- **Anything that is injected is never a cosmetic.** Injections do not fall under the definition of cosmetic products.

Cosmetic peptides such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 or acetyl hexapeptide-8 are therefore legal in creams and serums. A peptide such as melanotan II, which is injected under the skin and acts throughout the body, is by contrast not a cosmetic, even if it is advertised as a "tanning agent."

### Austria

In Austria, the **Medicinal Products Act (Arzneimittelgesetz, AMG)** governs the authorization, manufacture, and dispensing of medicines. Responsibility for authorization and surveillance lies with the Federal Office for Safety in Health Care (BASG) together with AGES Medicines and Medical Devices Agency. Prescription medicines are dispensed only in pharmacies against a prescription; mail order from pharmacies is limited to non-prescription medicines.

The import of medicines is governed by the **Medicinal Products Import Act (Arzneiwareneinfuhrgesetz)**. Private individuals may import medicines only under certain conditions and in quantities for personal use; stricter rules apply to orders from countries outside the European Economic Area. Unapproved active substances from "research chemical" shops as a rule do not meet these conditions. Customs can seize such shipments.

For sport, the **Federal Anti-Doping Act 2021 (Anti-Doping-Bundesgesetz 2021)** applies. It refers to the Prohibited List of the World Anti-Doping Agency and contains prohibitions and penal provisions, in particular for placing prohibited substances on the market and passing them on. The National Anti-Doping Agency Austria (NADA Austria) is responsible for testing.

### Germany

In Germany, the **Medicinal Products Act (Arzneimittelgesetz, AMG)** applies. The licensing authorities are the BfArM and the Paul-Ehrlich-Institut. Prescription medicines may only be dispensed against a prescription, including by mail order through licensed mail-order pharmacies.

For imports, there is in principle a ban on bringing unauthorized medicines into Germany. An exception exists for personal use if the medicine is lawfully on the market in the country of origin and is obtained from a member state of the EU or the European Economic Area without commercial intermediation; in addition, there are narrowly limited special rules. In our understanding, this exception does not apply to gray-market peptides that are not authorized as medicines anywhere.

Anyone who manufactures, places on the market, or supplies medicines to others without a license may be liable to prosecution under the AMG. This applies in particular to so-called questionable medicines, for which there is a justified suspicion of harmful effects.

For sport, the **Anti-Doping Act (Anti-Doping-Gesetz, AntiDopG)** applies in addition. Among other things, it prohibits the manufacture, trafficking, and placing on the market of doping substances as well as their use on others. Acquisition and possession of certain substances listed in an annex to the act – including peptide hormones – in not insignificant quantities for doping purposes are punishable. For elite athletes, self-doping is also a criminal offense. The National Anti Doping Agency Germany (NADA) is responsible for testing.

### USA

In the USA, the **Food and Drug Administration (FDA)** is responsible. New medicines require approval (for chemically produced active substances via a "New Drug Application," for biological active substances via a "Biologics License Application").

**Dietary supplements** have been marketable largely without prior review since a law passed in 1994. However, a substance that was first approved as a drug or substantially investigated clinically as a drug may in principle not subsequently be sold as a dietary supplement. For this reason, many peptides cannot legally be marketed as supplements in the USA.

**Compounding**: Under certain conditions, US pharmacies may prepare medicines themselves – individually for specific patients or on a larger scale in specially supervised facilities. During shortages of approved medicines, they may also produce copies. Since the end of the shortages of semaglutide and tirzepatide, under US law they may only produce differing preparations (Belcourt 2026, PMID 41689811). The FDA specifies in lists which active substances may be used as bulk substances for compounding. To our knowledge, the FDA has classified several popular gray-market peptides, including BPC-157, as unsuitable for compounding because of safety concerns. These lists and the political debate about them are changing; please check the current status.

**"Research Use Only"**: In the USA, too, the sale of peptides labeled "for research purposes only" to consumers takes place in a legal gray area that experts describe as a circumvention of prescription requirements (Belcourt 2025, PMID 39285774). The FDA has sent warning letters to individual vendors.

**Import**: Under narrow conditions, the FDA tolerates the import of small quantities of medicines for personal use. There is no legal entitlement to this, and unapproved active substances without medical supervision are generally not covered.

### Doping: The WADA Prohibited List

For athletes who are subject to doping controls, the **Prohibited List of the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA)** is authoritative. It is revised annually and comes into force on January 1 of each year. For peptides, two sections that apply both in and out of competition are particularly important:

**S0 – Non-approved substances.** Prohibited are pharmacologically active substances that are not addressed by any other section of the list and are not approved by any health authority for human use – for example, drugs in preclinical or clinical development, discontinued developments, designer drugs, and substances approved only for veterinary use. Many gray-market peptides fall into this group. To our knowledge, BPC-157 is explicitly named in the list as an example.

**S2 – Peptide hormones, growth factors, related substances, and mimetics.** These include, among others:

- growth hormone and its fragments (for example AOD-9604),
- growth hormone-releasing hormone and its analogs (for example CJC-1295, sermorelin, tesamorelin),
- growth hormone secretagogues and growth hormone-releasing peptides (for example ipamorelin, GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin, macimorelin, and, to our knowledge, also the non-peptide ibutamoren),
- growth factors such as IGF-1 and its analogs, as well as thymosin beta-4 and its derivatives (for example TB-500),
- in men, hormones and releasing factors of the sex hormone axis (for example gonadorelin and kisspeptin).

In addition, section S4 prohibits, among others, insulins as metabolic modulators. To our knowledge, GLP-1 medications such as semaglutide or tirzepatide are currently not on the Prohibited List. The list gives examples but is not exhaustive: substances not named but with a similar chemical structure or similar biological effect can also be prohibited.

Anti-doping law applies **strict liability**: if a prohibited substance is found in a sample, this is a violation – regardless of whether it was taken intentionally or came, for example, from a contaminated dietary supplement. Anyone who needs an approved, prohibited medicine for medical reasons can apply for a **Therapeutic Use Exemption** (TUE). For non-approved substances from section S0, this is practically impossible.

Doping control laboratories are continuously developing detection methods for new peptides, often on the basis of seized products (Cox 2017, PMID 28035768). The fact that a substance was not detectable yesterday does not mean it will not be detectable tomorrow – and frozen samples can be reanalyzed years later.

### Liability: Who Bears the Risk?

With **approved medicines**, manufacturers, pharmacies, and prescribing physicians are embedded in a system of responsibility and liability. In Germany, the Medicinal Products Act provides for special liability of the manufacturer for damage caused by approved medicines that does not depend on fault; in Austria, general tort law and the Product Liability Act apply. Side effects are reported and evaluated and can lead to warnings or market withdrawals.

With **gray-market products**, this system is largely absent:

- The vendors are often based abroad, frequently change their names and websites, and are practically impossible to pin down.
- With the notice "not for human use," they try to disclaim all responsibility.
- Anyone who uses such a product themselves bears the health and financial risk largely alone.
- Anyone who passes on such a product to others or uses it on others – for example in a gym, beauty salon, or wellness business – may be liable to prosecution and may be liable for damages.

Strict standards also apply to **use by physicians**. Anyone who administers unapproved active substances must provide comprehensive information about the uncertain data and is liable for treatment errors. Professional disciplinary consequences are possible.

### The Legal Situation at a Glance

| Situation | Classification (simplified) |
|---|---|
| Approved peptide on prescription from a pharmacy | legal, medically supervised |
| Approved peptide without prescription from the internet | generally illegal, risk of counterfeits |
| Unapproved peptide as a "research chemical" | generally a non-marketable medicine |
| Collagen peptides as food | legal, advertising claims restricted |
| Peptide cream as a cosmetic | legal, provided it is safe and not advertised as a remedy |
| Prohibited peptide in controlled sport | doping violation, sometimes a criminal offense |

### Key Points in Brief

- Whether a peptide is a medicine, a food, or a cosmetic depends on its effect, intended purpose, and presentation. In the case of pharmacological activity, medicines law takes precedence.
- Medicines require a marketing authorization. Most peptide medicines are prescription-only.
- The label "for research purposes only" does not make sale to private individuals legal and does not protect them.
- Many gray-market peptides are prohibited in sport, often via sections S0 and S2 of the WADA list.
- With gray-market products, users bear the risk largely alone.
