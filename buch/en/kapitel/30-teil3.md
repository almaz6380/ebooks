# Part III – The Big Comparison

In Part II, each peptide was considered individually. This part places the results side by side and answers three questions: How well studied is a peptide compared with the others? How do the weight-loss and diabetes medications compare with each other? And what remains of the promises from the internet if only human studies are allowed to count?

The tables are condensations. The sources and limitations of each figure can be found in the chapters of Part II. All information on approval and doping status reflects the status as of 2026 and should be checked for updates.

## All Peptides at a Glance

### How to Read the Tables

For each group from Part II, two tables follow: the first with evidence level, approval and size of the evidence base, the second with the most important study result and the main risk.

**Evidence level** (as in Part II):

- **A**: several large randomized studies in humans, usually with approval
- **B**: individual randomized studies in humans
- **C**: only small or uncontrolled human studies
- **D**: only animal and laboratory studies

The level describes *how well* something has been studied, not *whether* it works – and applies only to the use studied.

**Human studies / registry:** The figures come from the automated search for this book (October 2026, methodology in the appendix): publications classified as human studies in PubMed and entries in the ClinicalTrials.gov study registry. They show only the order of magnitude, depend heavily on the search term and partly contain irrelevant hits, while older papers under other names may be missing. Where a figure is clearly misleading, it is marked "(note)" and explained below the table. Registry entries from a sponsor whose entries proved on review to be apparently fictitious sample or test entries are not counted.

### Analysis: How Well Studied Are Peptides Overall?

#### Distribution of Evidence Levels

The 58 entries in this book – 56 peptides and two comparison substances that are not peptides (orforglipron and ibutamoren) – are distributed as follows:

| Evidence level | Number | Share | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 14 | 24% | Semaglutide, tirzepatide, tesamorelin, afamelanotide |
| B | 25 | 43% | Retatrutide, thymosin alpha-1, glutathione, collagen peptides |
| C | 12 | 21% | BPC-157, CJC-1295, Semax, melanotan II |
| D | 7 | 12% | AOD-9604, KPV, Dihexa, MOTS-c, IGF-1 LR3 |

Note: TB-500 is included in the count with level B for thymosin beta-4; for the TB-500 fragment alone, level D applies.

Three patterns:

1. **Level A is reached almost exclusively by drugs that have gone through a regular approval program by a pharmaceutical manufacturer.** Nine of the 14 entries with level A come from group A (metabolism and weight). The others are tesamorelin, macimorelin and mecasermin from the growth hormone axis as well as bremelanotide and afamelanotide. None of the substances described in group B (healing) and group D (brain and longevity) reaches level A.
2. **Level B often means "tested and not confirmed."** For many of the 25 entries, the largest randomized study missed its primary endpoint: thymosin alpha-1 in sepsis, LL-37 in leg ulcers, ipamorelin after bowel surgery, elamipretide in myopathy, oxytocin in autism, glutathione against chemotherapy-induced nerve damage.
3. **One third has level C or D.** 19 of 58 entries are based on small, uncontrolled human studies or only on animal and laboratory experiments – including many of the substances most heavily promoted on the internet.

Viewed by group:

- A. Metabolism and weight (15 entries): 9 × A, 5 × B, 1 × D
- B. Healing and immune system (8): 5 × B, 2 × C, 1 × D
- C. Growth hormone axis (11): 3 × A, 4 × B, 3 × C, 1 × D
- D. Brain and longevity (12): 3 × B, 5 × C, 4 × D
- E. Hormones and pigmentation (6): 2 × A, 3 × B, 1 × C
- F. Collagen and cosmetics (6): 5 × B, 1 × C

Group D has the weakest evidence: four of its twelve peptides have never been administered in a published human study, and five others only in small, mostly non-randomized studies.

Only around a quarter of the entries are approved as medicines in the EU or the USA – mostly for narrowly defined uses: afamelanotide for a rare light intolerance (erythropoietic protoporphyria, EPP), not for tanning; elamipretide only for Barth syndrome; oxytocin only for obstetrics, not as a nasal spray; tesamorelin only for a fat distribution disorder in HIV. When something is described as "approved," it is therefore always worth asking: where, for what and in what form?

The 28 entries not approved anywhere fall into two groups. Retatrutide, survodutide, CagriSema, amycretin, maridebart cafraglutide and orforglipron are in regular development with phase 3 trials; approval is likely for them, but not certain (assessment). Substances such as BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, AOD-9604, Epitalon, MOTS-c, Dihexa or IGF-1 LR3, on the other hand, have no recognizable path to approval and are mostly sold as "research peptides." Gray-market offerings of the first group are not to be equated with the tested medicines.

#### Doping Status According to WADA

For competitive sport, the Prohibited List of the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is what counts. The most important classes:

- **S0** – non-approved substances: all substances without approval as a medicine for humans, even if not named explicitly.
- **S2** – peptide hormones, growth factors and related substances, including growth hormone, its releasing factors and fragments, IGF-1, thymosin beta-4 and, in men, releasing factors for luteinizing hormone (LH).
- **S4** – hormone and metabolic modulators, including activators of the enzyme AMPK.
- **M2** – prohibited methods, including infusions of more than 100 ml within twelve hours.

If in doubt, the national anti-doping agencies can provide information.

### What the Overview Shows

The well-studied peptides are almost exclusively approved medicines from large, preregistered studies. Most of the peptides popular on the internet have evidence level C or D, are not approved anywhere and are frequently prohibited in sport. In between are substances that have been seriously studied in humans but whose studies have not confirmed the hoped-for benefit. The two following chapters explore this in more depth for the GLP-1 medications and for the most popular internet peptides.
