## Maridebart Cafraglutide (MariTide)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** a so-called antibody-peptide conjugate. A fully human monoclonal antibody is linked via short amino acid linkers to two GLP-1-like peptides. Strictly speaking, MariTide is therefore not a pure peptide but a hybrid of antibody and peptide. Development code AMG 133, short name MariTide.
- **Origin:** developed by Amgen.
- **Mechanism of action:** The GLP-1 component **activates** the GLP-1 receptor, the antibody **blocks** the GIP receptor (GIPR antagonist). This is the opposite of tirzepatide, which activates the GIP receptor.
- **Special feature:** Thanks to the antibody component, MariTide acts for a very long time; dosing every four weeks or less often is being studied.
- **Approval EU / USA:** no (as of October 2026).
- **WADA:** prohibited as a non-approved substance under S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – one large randomized Phase 2 trial, Phase 3 ongoing.

### How it is supposed to work

GIP presents a remarkable paradox. Tirzepatide *activates* the GIP receptor and, together with GLP-1, thereby achieves strong weight loss. At the same time, genetic studies and mouse experiments showed that *blocking* the GIP receptor can also protect against overweight. One possible explanation: continuous activation of the receptor causes the cell to switch it off – in effect, the result then resembles a blockade. This has not been definitively resolved; a comparative study in mice found that both approaches lowered food intake and weight in obesity but differed in their metabolic effects: the GIP agonist improved glucose tolerance, while the antagonist tended to reduce insulin sensitivity (Davies 2026, PMID 41287212).

Amgen is betting on blockade. The antibody against the GIP receptor at the same time ensures that the molecule stays in the blood for a very long time – antibodies are broken down slowly. In obese mice and monkeys, AMG 133 lowered body weight and improved metabolic values (Véniant 2024, PMID 38316982). The chemical development up to the final molecule was described in 2026 (Wu 2026, PMID 41941715).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 2 human studies: one Phase 1 and one Phase 2 trial. On ClinicalTrials.gov, 28 trials were registered, including the Phase 3 program **MARITIME**.

#### Phase 1

In the first-in-human study (Véniant 2024, PMID 38316982; NCT04478708), AMG 133 showed dose-dependent weight loss with acceptable tolerability. The long duration of action was striking: in the multiple-dose groups, weight loss persisted for up to 150 days after the last dose.

#### Phase 2

The Phase 2 trial (Jastreboff 2025, PMID 40549887) was broadly designed: eleven groups in two cohorts with different doses, dosing intervals (every four or eight weeks) and with or without slow dose escalation.

- **Without diabetes** (465 participants, mean BMI 37.9): After 52 weeks, weight loss was 12.3 to 16.2% depending on the group, and 2.5% on placebo. This analysis includes all participants regardless of treatment discontinuation.
- **With type 2 diabetes** (127 participants): weight loss of 8.4 to 12.3% compared with 1.7%; HbA1c fell by 1.2 to 1.6 percentage points (placebo +0.1).

Gastrointestinal side effects were common, but less so when treatment started at a lower dose and was increased slowly. According to the authors, no unexpected safety signals occurred. A weight loss of around 16% after one year is in the range of semaglutide, but below that of tirzepatide. The potential advantage of MariTide is therefore less the extent of weight loss than the infrequent dosing.

### Animal and laboratory studies

Cell systems confirmed that AMG 133 blocks the GIP receptor and activates the GLP-1 receptor. In obese mice and cynomolgus monkeys, it reduced body weight and improved metabolic markers (Véniant 2024, PMID 38316982; Wu 2026, PMID 41941715). Comparative mouse studies of GIP blockade versus GIP activation show different effects on insulin sensitivity and adipose tissue (Davies 2026, PMID 41287212). All of these findings come from animal experiments.

### Risks and side effects

**Documented in studies:**

- **Gastrointestinal complaints** (nausea, vomiting) were common in the Phase 2 trial, especially at a high starting dose without dose escalation (Jastreboff 2025, PMID 40549887). Exact percentages are not included in the abstract.

**Specific open questions:**

- **Long duration of action:** What many patients regard as an advantage is a disadvantage when side effects occur: a medicine that remains in the body for months cannot be stopped quickly. In the Phase 1 trial, the effect persisted for up to 150 days after the last dose (Véniant 2024, PMID 38316982). This is relevant, for example, in the case of an unplanned pregnancy or before surgery.
- **GIP blockade:** The long-term consequences of permanent blockade of the GIP receptor – for example on bone, adipose tissue or insulin sensitivity – are unknown in humans. In mouse studies, a GIP antagonist reduced insulin sensitivity compared with control animals given the same amount of food (Davies 2026, PMID 41287212).
- **Antibody formation:** With antibody-based drugs, the immune system can form antibodies against the drug. Data on this from large trials are pending.
- The known class risks of GLP-1 drugs (gallbladder, pancreas) have not yet been adequately studied for MariTide.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved; use only in clinical trials. Since it is a complex biotechnological product, imitation on the gray market is considerably more difficult than with small peptides.
- **USA:** not approved.
- **Sport:** WADA class S0 (non-approved substance), as of 2026, please check for updates.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed (registered trials, as of October 2026):**

- **Weight management without diabetes** (NCT06858839): Phase 3 with 3,853 participants, planned completion 2027.
- **Weight management with type 2 diabetes** (NCT06858878): Phase 3 with 1,105 participants.
- **Cardiovascular outcome trial** (NCT07037433) with 12,800 participants and **heart failure trial** (NCT07037459) with 5,056 participants, each running until 2030.
- **Sleep apnea** without and with a breathing mask (NCT07226765, NCT07225686).
- **Comparison with semaglutide** in type 2 diabetes (NCT07866066), switching from other GLP-1 drugs (NCT07575399), Japan (NCT06987695), fatty liver (NCT07441252, Phase 2).
- Phase 1 trials on gastric emptying (NCT07429032), heart rhythm (NCT07229157) and interactions with the contraceptive pill (NCT07523711).

**Assessment:** MariTide stands for a different concept from the tirzepatide family – fewer injections rather than maximum weight loss. For people who need long-term treatment, monthly or even less frequent dosing could improve adherence. Whether the concept of GIP blockade is safe in the long term must be shown by the large Phase 3 trials. Approval is realistic from 2028 at the earliest.

### Conclusion

Maridebart cafraglutide is an antibody-peptide conjugate that activates the GLP-1 receptor and blocks the GIP receptor, and is given only once a month or less often. In the Phase 2 trial, body weight fell after 52 weeks by up to 16.2% without and by up to 12.3% with type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal complaints are common and depend strongly on dose escalation; the long duration of action is both an advantage and a risk. The Phase 3 program, with outcome trials on cardiovascular disease and heart failure, is ongoing.
