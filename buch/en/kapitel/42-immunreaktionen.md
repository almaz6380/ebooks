## Immune Reactions and Antibodies

The immune system's job is to recognize what is foreign. Peptides stand at a boundary here: many are modeled on the body's own messenger substances but chemically modified so that they act longer. Others come from animals, from bacteria, or from the laboratory. In addition, most peptides are injected under the skin – a place where particularly large numbers of immune cells lie in wait for intruders.

Immune reactions to peptides range from harmless redness at the injection site through antibodies that can only be detected in the laboratory to rare life-threatening allergic reactions. This chapter categorizes these reactions, compares the peptides, and shows the role that impurities play.

### Why peptides can trigger an immune reaction

Whether the immune system classifies a peptide as foreign depends on several factors:

- **Origin and similarity to the body's own substances.** Exenatide was developed from a peptide in the saliva of a lizard and differs considerably from human GLP-1. Liraglutide and semaglutide, by contrast, are closely modeled on the human hormone.
- **Size and modifications.** Longer molecules, attached fatty acids, linkers, or antibody components (as with maridebart cafraglutide) offer more surface for attack.
- **Aggregation.** Peptides can clump together into larger aggregates that the immune system recognizes more easily.
- **Impurities.** Even small amounts of incorrectly manufactured peptide fragments or bacterial components can trigger a defensive reaction (more on this below).
- **Frequency and duration of use.** Someone who injects a peptide repeatedly over months gives the immune system many opportunities to react to it.

For the assessment of antibodies against drugs – experts speak of anti-drug antibodies (ADA) – a uniform terminology has become established (Shankar 2014, PMID 24764037). Four questions are important: How many of those treated form antibodies? How high is the amount (the titer)? Do the antibodies inhibit the effect (neutralizing antibodies)? And do they also react with the body's own hormone (cross-reactivity)? The last point would be the most concerning: antibodies that block not only the drug but also the body's own hormone could leave a permanent disorder.

### Anti-drug antibodies: what the studies show

Systematic studies are available for the approved GLP-1 drugs. They show how differently often antibodies arise – and that a high frequency does not automatically mean a clinical problem.

| Agent | Antibodies in | Consequences | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exenatide | 61% after 26 weeks (LEAD-6) | high titers: smaller HbA1c reduction | Buse 2011, PMID 21450987 |
| Liraglutide | 8.3 to 8.7%, low titers | no weakening of effect | Buse 2011, PMID 21450987 |
| Dulaglutide | 1.6%, neutralizing 0.9% | no hypersensitivity in antibody-positive patients | Milicevic 2016, PMID 26847401 |
| Tirzepatide | 51.1%, neutralizing about 2% | no weakening of effect; more skin and allergic reactions | Mullins 2024, PMID 37700637 |
| Sermorelin | 14 of 18 children | no discernible consequences | Ross 1987, PMID 2879138 |
| ARA-290 | none in 12 weeks | – | Lois 2020, PMID 32674280 |

**Exenatide** is the prime example of a peptide that frequently provokes antibodies because of its non-human origin. In the LEAD-6 trial, 113 of 185 treated people had antibodies against exenatide after 26 weeks; high antibody levels were associated with a significantly smaller reduction in long-term blood sugar. After switching to liraglutide, these antibodies did not impair the further effect (Buse 2011, PMID 21450987).

**Tirzepatide** shows that even a peptide modeled on the human hormone can frequently be recognized. In seven phase 3 trials with 5,025 evaluable participants, 51.1% formed antibodies during treatment. Neutralizing antibodies against the effect at the GIP and GLP-1 receptors were found in 1.9% and 2.1%, respectively, and those that also neutralized the body's own hormones in less than 1%. This had no influence on blood levels or effect. However, antibody-positive participants more frequently experienced hypersensitivity reactions and injection-site reactions, mostly mild to moderate (Mullins 2024, PMID 37700637).

For the newer agents – retatrutide, survodutide, mazdutide, cagrilintide, amycretin, and the antibody-based maridebart cafraglutide – comparable pooled analyses have not yet been published. For maridebart cafraglutide this is particularly relevant, because antibody drugs can have their own immunogenicity (chapter Maridebart Cafraglutide).

For most gray-market peptides there are no antibody data at all. This applies to BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, MOTS-c, humanin, Epitalon, and many others. The chapters on MOTS-c, humanin, and FOXO4-DRI point out that antibodies are conceivable with repeated injection but have never been studied. Anyone who injects such substances for months therefore cannot know whether their body is forming antibodies and what effects these have.

### Injection-site reactions

Redness, itching, swelling, small bruises, or palpable nodules at the injection site are the most common immune and tissue reactions to injected peptides. A meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials with 4,861 participants found an approximately three-and-a-half-fold increased risk of such reactions with GLP-1 receptor agonists compared with the comparison groups (relative risk 3.55). In the US adverse event reporting database, exenatide and dulaglutide stood out in particular for bleeding at the injection site (Taj 2026, PMID 41395692).

The frequency varies considerably between peptides:

| Peptide | Injection-site reactions | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Setmelanotide | 96% (meta-analysis, 7 studies) | Sridharan 2026, PMID 39924461 |
| Elamipretide (SS-31) | 80%, mostly mild | Karaa 2020, PMID 32096613 |
| Mecasermin | fatty tissue changes in 32% | Chernausek 2007, PMID 17192294 |
| Tirzepatide | 2 to 7% | Gupta 2026, PMID 42930571 |
| Bremelanotide | 5.4% vs. 0.5% on placebo | Clayton 2022, PMID 35147466 |

With the weekly formulation of exenatide, small nodules often form because the microspheres that release the active ingredient slowly are broken down under the skin. In the five-year analysis of DURATION-1, such reactions became less frequent over time (Wysham 2015, PMID 25744115). With mecasermin, the finding was a thickening of the fatty tissue at the injection site (lipohypertrophy).

It is important to distinguish these from **infection**. Redness that increases over days, becomes warm, is painful, forms pus, or is accompanied by fever is not a harmless reaction. After Argireline injections into the forehead and temples, a 45-year-old woman developed nodules and abscesses; the cause was the difficult-to-treat bacterium *Mycobacterium abscessus*, and treatment lasted five months (Chen 2021, PMID 33748252). Such infections usually do not originate from the peptide but from non-sterile products or procedures – more on this in the chapter "Gray-Market Products."

### Allergic reactions and anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction affecting the whole body, with skin rash, swelling, shortness of breath, and circulatory collapse. It is rare with peptide drugs, but it does occur.

For the GLP-1 drugs, there are large database studies on this. In three US insurance databases with 696,089 new users, the incidence of anaphylaxis ranged, depending on the agent, from 1.0 (lixisenatide) to 6.0 (exenatide) cases per 10,000 person-years; the authors consider anaphylaxis with GLP-1 drugs to be rare (Anthony 2024, PMID 38363873). A study from the United Kingdom and the US compared GLP-1 receptor agonists with two other groups of diabetes drugs. Compared with DPP-4 inhibitors, the risk was not significantly increased (hazard ratio 1.15); compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, it was 38% higher (40.7 versus 29.4 cases per 100,000 person-years) (Pradhan 2022, PMID 35136902). This is a relative increase in an absolutely very rare reaction.

Individual cases show what such reactions can look like:

- Case reports describe anaphylaxis with the weekly formulation of exenatide (Ornelas 2018, PMID 30350785) and an allergic reaction to exenatide and lixisenatide, but not to liraglutide (Shamriz 2019, PMID 31296642) – an indication that the lizard-derived agents are recognized differently from the human-like ones.
- With tirzepatide, a biphasic anaphylaxis has been described in which the symptoms recurred after initial improvement (He 2023, PMID 38186543).
- A 50-year-old woman developed massive swelling of the lips, tongue, and throat four hours after her first injection of semaglutide from a compounding pharmacy and had to be mechanically ventilated for four days. Only afterward did it emerge that she had accidentally injected ten times the amount (Kraft 2026, PMID 42194666).
- After a Cerebrolysin infusion, an 85-year-old man developed life-threatening anaphylaxis that was confirmed in the laboratory (Trimmel 2024, PMID 39055722). Cerebrolysin is a mixture of peptides from pig brain; animal extracts such as this or thymalin generally bring along more foreign proteins (chapters Cerebrolysin, Thymalin).

Allergies are also possible with cosmetic peptides, albeit rarely. In a small study of a cream containing, among other ingredients, palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, a suspected allergic reaction occurred; which ingredient was responsible remained unclear (Röper 2004, PMID 15127162).

For gray-market peptides there are no frequency data. The risk of a severe allergic reaction is particularly high there, however, because use usually takes place without medical supervision and nobody is on hand with emergency medication in a crisis. Shortness of breath, swelling of the face or throat, and circulatory symptoms after an injection are always an emergency.

### Inflammatory and autoimmune reactions

Some peptides are themselves part of the immune system. This makes them interesting, but also risky.

**LL-37** is an endogenous defense peptide. In psoriasis, it plays a key role: it binds to the body's own genetic material and makes it visible to certain immune cells, which then release inflammatory messenger substances (Lande 2007, PMID 17873860). In two-thirds of people with moderate to severe psoriasis, T cells were found that specifically recognize LL-37 – so there it acts like an autoantigen (Lande 2014, PMID 25470744). After injections of LL-37 into skin metastases, a patient developed inflammatory, wart-like skin changes (Dolkar 2018, PMID 29665030). For people with psoriasis, rosacea, or other autoimmune diseases, this is an indication to be taken seriously (chapter LL-37).

**Immunostimulating peptides** such as thymosin alpha-1 and thymalin are intended to stimulate the body's defenses. Whether this is harmful in autoimmune diseases or after organ transplants has not been studied (chapters Thymosin Alpha-1, Thymalin).

For the GLP-1 drugs, a systematic review describes, in addition to the common mild skin reactions, rare but potentially serious immune-mediated conditions, including angioedema, blistering autoimmune diseases, drug eruptions, and vasculitis (Gupta 2026, PMID 42930571). How common they are cannot be determined from case reports.

### Impurities as triggers

Some immune reactions are not due to the peptide itself but to what additionally ends up in the vial during its manufacture.

**Defective peptides.** Chemical synthesis produces molecules in which a building block is missing, occurs twice, or is chemically altered. Such impurities can contain new structures that the immune system's T cells recognize. For this reason, the US drug regulatory agency requires information on every new impurity compared with the reference product for generic versions of synthetic peptides (De Groot 2023, PMID 37467878). What such an assessment looks like is shown by a study of generic versions of the osteoporosis peptide teriparatide, in which impurities were evaluated using computational methods, binding assays, and experiments with human immune cells (Mattei 2025, PMID 41445733).

**GLP-1 copies.** A manufacturer-affiliated analysis of 16 injectable and 8 oral semaglutide copies found new impurities, including larger protein molecules, and new recognition sites for the immune system predicted by computational methods; liraglutide copies showed a greater tendency to aggregate (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). Whether these differences actually provoke more antibodies in humans was not measured. That a carefully manufactured synthetic copy is not automatically more problematic is shown by another study: a synthetic liraglutide generic did not stimulate human immune cells in the laboratory more strongly than the genetically engineered original (Tang 2025, PMID 40398094). What matters, then, is not the manufacturing method but quality control.

**Endotoxins and germs.** Endotoxins, cell wall components of bacteria, directly activate the innate immune system and can trigger fever, chills, and circulatory reactions. In test purchases of semaglutide from illegal online pharmacies, they were detectable in all samples (Ashraf 2024, PMID 39509151; details in Part I). A control laboratory detected the bacterium *Bacillus cereus*, which can destroy red blood cells, in two illegal peptide preparations for injection (Janvier 2018, PMID 28941153).

For the gray market, this means: even if a peptide itself is only weakly immunogenic, the product may be. And because neither impurity profiles nor endotoxin limits are tested for gray-market goods, a reaction can hardly be attributed to a specific component after the fact.

### What this means in practice

- Antibodies against peptide drugs are common but usually without consequences. With exenatide, high titers can weaken the effect; with tirzepatide, they are associated with somewhat more skin and hypersensitivity reactions.
- Injection-site reactions are the most common immune reaction and almost the rule with some peptides. Increasing redness, warmth, pus, or fever, on the other hand, indicate an infection.
- Anaphylaxis is very rare with approved GLP-1 drugs – on the order of a few cases per 10,000 treatment years. There are also case reports for tirzepatide, Cerebrolysin, and an accidental overdose.
- For most gray-market peptides, data on antibodies and allergies are completely lacking. Impurities and endotoxins are an additional, incalculable risk there.
- Anyone who has already had an allergic reaction to a peptide should mention this at every medical treatment. Whether related agents are tolerated can only be clarified by a physician.
