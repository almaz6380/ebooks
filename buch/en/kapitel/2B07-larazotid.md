## Larazotide

Larazotide is the only peptide in this group that made it into a Phase 3 approval trial without previously being approved anywhere. It was meant to become the first medicine for celiac disease – a condition for which, to this day, only a lifelong gluten-free diet helps. The concept behind it, sealing a "leaky" gut wall, also makes larazotide interesting to the "leaky gut" scene. The history of the substance shows how difficult it is to turn a plausible idea into an effective medicine.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** synthetic peptide of 8 amino acids (Gopalakrishnan 2012, PMID 22401908), as an acetate salt (larazotide acetate); development code AT-1001.
- **Origin:** derived from research on the zonula occludens toxin of the cholera bacterium, which opens the connections between gut cells; larazotide is intended to act in the opposite way.
- **Mechanism of action:** regulator of the "tight junctions" (sealing strips between gut cells); considered an antagonist of the endogenous signaling substance zonulin. Acts locally in the gut and is hardly absorbed into the blood.
- **Approval EU/USA:** no.
- **WADA:** not listed; without any discernible performance-enhancing effect (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level: B** – several randomized Phase 2 trials with mixed results; the Phase 3 trial was stopped, without published results.

### How it is supposed to work

In celiac disease, the immune system of genetically predisposed people reacts to gluten, a protein in wheat, rye and barley. Fragments of gluten pass through the intestinal mucosa and trigger inflammation there that destroys the intestinal villi. Some of these fragments presumably travel through the gaps between gut cells. These gaps are closed by the tight junctions – a kind of molecular zipper.

The signaling substance zonulin can open this zipper; gliadin, a component of gluten, releases zonulin. Larazotide is intended to block this pathway. In cultures of human gut cells, larazotide prevented the rearrangement of the sealing proteins and reduced the passage of a gliadin fragment through the cell layer; in genetically modified mice with gluten sensitivity, the structure of the sealing strips was preserved (Gopalakrishnan 2012, PMID 22401908). Newer studies additionally suspect inhibition of the enzyme myosin light chain kinase, which puts the cell junctions under tension (Slifer 2021, PMID 33881350).

Larazotide was never intended as a replacement for the gluten-free diet. The aim was an add-on agent for those affected who have symptoms despite the diet – for example due to unnoticed traces of gluten.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** The automated search found **4** human studies in PubMed and **29** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov. Of these 29 entries, however, 19 concern a completely different medicine: migalastat for Fabry disease formerly had the same development code AT1001. Only **10** registry entries actually relate to larazotide. A 2022 meta-analysis summarized four randomized trials with 626 people (Hoilat 2022, PMID 34339872).

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gluten challenge (Leffler 2012, PMID 22825365) | 86 people with celiac disease, RCT with 4 doses and placebo | 14 days | permeability test without significant difference; fewer symptoms at some low doses |
| Gluten challenge (Kelly 2013, PMID 23163616) | 184 people, RCT with 3 doses and placebo, 2.7 g gluten daily | 6 weeks | permeability unchanged; fewer symptoms only with 1 mg; smaller rise in celiac antibodies |
| Symptoms despite diet (Leffler 2015, PMID 25683116; NCT01396213) | 342 people, RCT with 3 doses and placebo | 12 weeks | primary goal reached only with the lowest dose (0.5 mg); 1 mg and 2 mg no better than placebo |
| CeDLara, Phase 3 (NCT03569007) | 307 people, randomized, blinded | 12 weeks planned | stopped by the sponsor in 2022; no results published |
| MIS-C in children (Yonker 2025, PMID 40737433) | 12 children with inflammatory syndrome after COVID-19, Phase 2a, RCT | 3 weeks | faster resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms; no side effects from larazotide |

#### Celiac disease: Phase 2

In the two studies with targeted gluten challenge, larazotide was meant to prevent the rise in intestinal permeability. This was measured with a sugar test in urine (lactulose-mannitol ratio). In practice, this test fluctuated so much that no effect could be demonstrated (Leffler 2012, PMID 22825365; Kelly 2013, PMID 23163616). An unusual pattern emerged for symptoms: low doses sometimes worked better than high ones. In the study with 184 people, only the 1 mg dose significantly limited gluten-induced symptoms; at the same time, celiac antibodies rose considerably less under all larazotide doses than under placebo (Kelly 2013, PMID 23163616).

The largest Phase 2 trial, with 342 people who had symptoms despite a gluten-free diet, reached its primary goal only with the lowest dose of 0.5 mg. At this dose, there were 26 percent fewer days with celiac symptoms. The higher doses were not better than placebo on any endpoint; the authors themselves describe the results as "mixed" (Leffler 2015, PMID 25683116). A medicine in which only the lowest dose works is pharmacologically unusual and leaves doubt as to whether it is a real effect or chance.

The meta-analysis found no effect on intestinal permeability, and an improvement in symptoms only under gluten challenge, but not in people on a gluten-free diet – that is, precisely not in the group for which larazotide was intended (Hoilat 2022, PMID 34339872).

#### Celiac disease: the Phase 3 trial CeDLara

On the basis of the 0.5 mg results, the Phase 3 trial CeDLara started in 2019 with larazotide in enteric-coated beads (NCT03569007). According to the registry, 307 people were enrolled; the trial was "terminated by the sponsor" in July 2022. To the author's knowledge, the manufacturer at the time, 9 Meters Biopharma, justified the termination by stating that an interim analysis had shown that considerably more participants would have been needed to demonstrate a difference from placebo. There is no scientific publication of the results in PubMed. Reviews note that to date no celiac drug has successfully completed a Phase 3 trial (D'heedene 2024, PMID 38648685) and that no treatment has been shown to be an effective alternative to the gluten-free diet (Girbal-González 2025, PMID 41010485).

#### New application: inflammatory syndrome after COVID-19

One research group suspects that in multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) after COVID-19, viral components pass from the gut into the blood. In a first case series with four children, the viral protein disappeared from the blood on larazotide, and gastrointestinal symptoms resolved faster than in 22 comparison children (Yonker 2022, PMID 35211683). A randomized Phase 2a trial with only 12 children confirmed faster improvement of symptoms; no side effects from larazotide were observed (Yonker 2025, PMID 40737433). The trial was stopped because of declining case numbers (NCT05022303). A trial in long COVID with 107 children and adults has been completed since June 2026 according to the registry (NCT05747534); results had not been published as of October 2026.

### Animal and laboratory studies

Besides celiac disease, larazotide has been tested in animal models of other diseases with an impaired intestinal barrier. In mice prone to joint inflammation, larazotide reduced the onset of arthritis (Tajik 2020, PMID 32332732). In pigs, it promoted the recovery of small intestinal tissue after impaired blood flow (Slifer 2021, PMID 33881350). In rats, effects in acute pancreatitis and acute liver failure have been described (Karahan 2024, PMID 38441784; Caliskan 2021, PMID 34791921). These are animal experiments; no conclusions about rheumatism, the pancreas or the liver in humans can be drawn from them.

### Risks and side effects

#### Documented

Larazotide was well tolerated in all studies. In the study with 86 people, no serious side effects occurred; the most common were headache and urinary tract infections (Leffler 2012, PMID 22825365). In the largest Phase 2 trial, safety was comparable with placebo (Leffler 2015, PMID 25683116). Overall, the meta-analysis found no excess of side effects compared with placebo (Hoilat 2022, PMID 34339872). Since larazotide hardly enters the blood, a favorable safety profile is plausible.

#### Points to note

- **No alternative to the diet:** Even in the positive studies, larazotide did not protect against damage from larger amounts of gluten. Anyone who relaxes their gluten-free diet because of such an agent risks intestinal damage.
- **Gray market:** Larazotide is occasionally offered as a "leaky gut peptide." The studies used a special enteric-coated formulation; whether freely sold products deliver the active substance to the site of action at all is unknown. There are no studies with larazotide for the concept of "leaky gut" without celiac disease.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** not approved.
- **USA:** not approved. To the author's knowledge, the developer had received an accelerated review designation ("Fast Track") from the FDA for celiac disease; after the Phase 3 trial was stopped, no application for approval was submitted.
- **WADA:** not listed.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed:** As of October 2026, no ongoing trial with larazotide is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The long COVID trial (NCT05747534) has been completed; results are pending.

**Author's assessment:** For celiac disease, larazotide is unlikely to have a future after the termination of CeDLara unless a new developer takes over the rights. Other approaches – gluten-degrading enzymes, transglutaminase 2 inhibitors or methods for immune tolerance – are currently considered more promising (D'heedene 2024, PMID 38648685). In MIS-C and long COVID, further development depends on the as yet unpublished results.

### Conclusion

Larazotide is a peptide of eight amino acids intended to seal the connections between gut cells, and was the first agent against celiac disease to reach a Phase 3 trial. In the Phase 2 trials, the results were mixed: no effect on intestinal permeability could be demonstrated, and for symptoms despite diet, only the lowest dose worked (Leffler 2015, PMID 25683116). The Phase 3 trial CeDLara was stopped in 2022, and results were not published. Larazotide is well tolerated, but neither approved nor proven effective – and no substitute for the gluten-free diet.
