# Part I – Fundamentals

Before Part II turns to individual substances, this part lays the foundation. You will learn what a peptide actually is chemically, how an extract from dog pancreases became one of the world's highest-grossing groups of medicines within a century, why most peptides have to be injected, how to read a study without being misled by headlines, what is actually in the vials on the gray market, and which legal rules apply.

If you only want to look up a specific peptide, you can jump straight to Part II. However, the chapters in this part explain the terms and standards that are used constantly there – such as "randomized," "surrogate endpoint," "evidence level B," or "research peptide." A brief look is therefore worthwhile even for readers in a hurry.

## What Peptides Are – and What They Are Not

The word "peptide" has had a remarkable career in recent years. It appears on skin creams and protein powders, in advertisements for weight-loss injections, and in online forums where "healing peptides" are discussed. Yet the term initially refers only to a particular chemical structure – nothing more and nothing less. It says nothing about whether a substance works, whether it is safe, or whether it even gets into the body. This chapter clarifies the basic terms and clears up some common misconceptions.

### Amino Acids: The Building Blocks

All peptides and proteins are made of amino acids. An amino acid is a small molecule with two characteristic components: an amino group (it contains nitrogen) and an acid group (carboxyl group). Between them sits a central carbon atom to which a so-called side chain is attached. This side chain distinguishes the amino acids from one another: it can be small and inconspicuous, as in glycine; oily and water-repellent, as in leucine; electrically charged, as in lysine; or sulfur-containing, as in cysteine.

The human body builds its proteins essentially from 20 standard amino acids. It can produce some of them itself; others – the "essential" amino acids – must be obtained from food. In addition, there are hundreds of other amino acids that occur in nature or are produced in the laboratory. Such "non-natural" amino acids are important for drug development because they can make a peptide more stable. You will find an example below in the case of semaglutide.

Important for understanding many advertising claims: individual amino acids are not peptides. A dietary supplement containing arginine, glutamine, or "BCAAs" contains free amino acids, i.e., loose building blocks – not a peptide.

### The Peptide Bond

When two amino acids join, the acid group of one reacts with the amino group of the other. A water molecule is split off in the process, and a stable link is formed: the peptide bond. The result is called a dipeptide. If another amino acid is added, a tripeptide is formed, then a tetrapeptide, and so on. Such a chain always has a direction: it begins with a free amino group (the "N-terminus") and ends with a free acid group (the "C-terminus"). In scientific articles, amino acids are therefore always written from left to right, from the N-terminus to the C-terminus – often using one-letter abbreviations. The tripeptide "GHK," for example, stands for glycine–histidine–lysine.

The order of the amino acids is called the sequence or primary structure. It is crucial: two peptides made of the same building blocks in a different order are different substances with different properties – just as "rose" and "eros" consist of the same letters.

### Peptide or Protein? A Question of Length

There is no sharp scientific boundary between peptides and proteins, only a convention. Roughly speaking:

- **Oligopeptides**: about 2 to 10 amino acids, for example the tripeptide glutathione or the dipeptide carnosine.
- **Polypeptides**: longer chains of up to about 50 amino acids.
- **Proteins**: larger molecules, usually with more than 50 amino acids and a fixed three-dimensional fold.

Insulin, with 51 amino acids, lies right at this boundary. It consists of two chains connected by sulfur bridges and, depending on the textbook, is called either a peptide or a small protein. Human growth hormone, by contrast, is clearly a protein: its most common form consists of 191 amino acids and weighs around 22,000 daltons (Krug 2018, PMID 29864719). The dalton is the unit for the mass of molecules; an average amino acid in a chain weighs a little more than 100 daltons.

Some examples from this book for orientation:

| Substance | Length | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| Carnosine | 2 amino acids | found in muscle |
| Glutathione | 3 amino acids | endogenous protective molecule |
| Oxytocin | 9 amino acids | hormone, ring-shaped structure |
| GLP-1 (active form) | 30 amino acids | gut hormone |
| Exendin-4 (exenatide) | 39 amino acids | from lizard venom |
| Insulin | 51 amino acids | two chains |
| Growth hormone | 191 amino acids | protein |

The length of exendin-4 is given in its first description (Eng 1992, PMID 1313797); the other figures reflect textbook knowledge.

In practice, length matters for several reasons. Short peptides are easier and cheaper to produce chemically. Long chains fold into complex shapes that can be indispensable for their effect. And the larger a molecule, the harder it is for it to pass through the skin or the intestinal wall – more on this in the chapter on dosage forms.

### How Peptides Work in the Body

The human body uses peptides primarily as messengers. A peptide hormone is produced in one cell, released into the blood or tissue, and docks onto a matching receptor on another cell. Receptors are protein molecules in the cell membrane that function like a lock: only the matching key triggers a signal inside the cell.

Because peptides are usually too large and too water-soluble to slip through the fatty cell membrane, most of them act via receptors on the cell surface. Many of these belong to the large family of G protein-coupled receptors. The GLP-1 receptor, through which semaglutide or liraglutide act, is such a receptor. It is found not only in the pancreas but also in the gastrointestinal tract, heart, lungs, kidneys, and brain (Knudsen 2019, PMID 31031702) – which explains why these drugs have so many different effects.

Peptides perform very different tasks in the body:

- **Hormones** such as insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, oxytocin, or vasopressin regulate metabolism, fluid balance, and reproduction.
- **Releasing factors** from the diencephalon, such as gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), give commands to the pituitary gland.
- **Neuropeptides** such as endorphins act as signaling substances in the nervous system.
- **Antimicrobial peptides** such as LL-37 are part of the innate immune defense.
- **Structural components and breakdown products**: when collagen or milk protein is digested, short peptides are formed, some of which can be detected in the blood.

This diversity explains why such different substances are grouped under the term "peptide": a highly potent prescription diabetes medication as well as a collagen powder from the supermarket.

### Short Lifespan: The Fundamental Problem of Natural Peptides

The body's own peptides are usually built for a short, precise effect. The body keeps a wide range of enzymes on hand that cleave peptide bonds – so-called peptidases or proteases. This makes sense: a hormone signal is meant to come after a meal and then go away again.

For a medication, however, this short lifespan is a problem. Natural GLP-1 is rapidly cut up by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4). In human blood plasma in the test tube, its half-life was around 20 minutes (Deacon 1995, PMID 7883856); in the body, GLP-1 is additionally cleared rapidly from the circulation. Unmodified GLP-1 would therefore hardly be usable as a medication.

The solution found by pharmaceutical research was to modify the molecule in a targeted way. In semaglutide, two amino acids of natural GLP-1 were exchanged – at position 8 there is the non-natural amino acid Aib, which protects the molecule from DPP-4 – and a fatty acid was attached to the chain via a linker. The fatty acid causes semaglutide to bind reversibly to the transport protein albumin in the blood (Lau 2015, PMID 26308095). This creates a depot from which the drug is released slowly. The half-life in humans is about one week (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186) – which is why one injection per week is sufficient.

This principle – a natural model, specifically stabilized – runs through modern peptide medicine. But it also means that a peptide drug is usually **not** a "natural" substance in the sense of being unchanged and endogenous. It is a molecule inspired by nature but optimized in the laboratory.

### How Peptides Are Produced

There are three basic ways to obtain peptides:

**Chemical synthesis.** Since the 1960s, peptides have been built up building block by building block on a solid support material. This process of solid-phase peptide synthesis was developed by Robert Bruce Merrifield (Merrifield 1969, PMID 4307033), who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it in 1984. Today, such syntheses are largely automated. They are the basis of almost all short peptides, including those offered on the gray market. However, errors can occur at every coupling step – such as missing or duplicated amino acids. A careful purification step is therefore indispensable.

**Genetic engineering (recombinant) production.** Here, the blueprint for the peptide is introduced into bacteria or yeast cells, which then produce it in large tanks. Human insulin was the first medication produced in this way; it was used in clinical studies in humans from 1980 onward (The 1989, PMID 2690608). Larger peptides and proteins are still predominantly obtained in this way today, sometimes combined with chemical modification steps.

**Extraction from natural sources.** Early insulins were obtained from the pancreases of cattle and pigs. Collagen peptides are produced today by breaking down collagen from skin, bones, or fish scales into smaller pieces using enzymes (hydrolysis). Such hydrolysates are mixtures of many different peptides – not a uniform active substance.

The production route is significant for safety. One investigation compared copycat products of semaglutide and liraglutide with the originator products and found new impurities in some of the copycat active substances, including higher-molecular-weight proteins, trace metals, and solvent residues (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). Whether such differences are clinically relevant remains open – but they show that "the same active substance" does not automatically mean "the same product." Incidentally, the investigation was carried out by the manufacturer of the originator products, which should be taken into account in the assessment.

### Peptides as Medicines: A Growing Group

Peptides occupy a niche between classic "small molecules" (such as aspirin or metformin) and large biologics (such as antibodies). They can bind very specifically to a receptor, which often leads to few undesirable side effects elsewhere. At the same time, they are harder to put into tablet form and often expensive to produce.

According to one review, more than 80 peptide drugs have reached the market since the introduction of insulin, for example for diabetes, cancer, osteoporosis, multiple sclerosis, HIV infection, and chronic pain (Muttenthaler 2021, PMID 33536635). Another analysis counted over 150 peptides in active clinical development (Lau 2018, PMID 28720325). The figures differ depending on how they are counted, but they point in the same direction: peptides are an established and growing part of medicine.

You may know many peptide drugs without having perceived them as peptides: insulin; the osteoporosis drug teriparatide (a fragment of parathyroid hormone consisting of 34 amino acids); prostate cancer drugs that influence the GnRH system; the labor-inducing drug oxytocin; or the antibiotic vancomycin, a glycopeptide.

### What Peptides Are Not

Precisely because the term has become so popular, it is worth looking at common misconceptions.

**"Peptide" is not a mark of quality.** The chemical structure says nothing about benefit or risk. Insulin saves lives every day – in the wrong amount it can be fatal. A peptide is not automatically gentler than a "chemical" medication.

**"Endogenous" does not mean "harmless."** Many gray-market peptides are advertised as "endogenous" or "bioidentical." But even substances produced by the body can cause harm in unnaturally high amounts, in the wrong place, or over long periods. One example is the growth hormone system: a deficiency makes you ill, and so does a permanent excess. In addition, many supposedly endogenous peptides are in fact modifications or fragments that do not occur in this form in the body at all. BPC-157, for instance, is a sequence of 15 amino acids (Cox 2017, PMID 28035768) that its discoverers derived from gastric juice; it is not established as a human hormone in its own right.

**Peptides are not steroids.** Anabolic agents such as testosterone are steroid hormones with a completely different chemical framework made of carbon rings. They act via receptors inside the cell. In the fitness world, the two groups are sometimes lumped together; chemically and pharmacologically, they have little in common.

**Not everything that acts similarly is a peptide.** Orforglipron, for example, activates the GLP-1 receptor like semaglutide but is a small, non-peptide molecule. Ibutamoren (MK-677) stimulates growth hormone release like some peptides but is not one itself. Both are therefore covered in this book only for comparison.

**A peptide in a cream is not the same as a peptide in a syringe.** Cosmetic peptides such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 are specifically designed to act in the uppermost layer of the skin. Whether and how deeply they penetrate is a separate question. Conversely, a peptide that is injected as a medication does not simply pass through the skin when applied to it.

**Peptides that are eaten are usually digested.** The gastrointestinal tract is designed to break down proteins into amino acids and very short fragments. A peptide hormone that is swallowed is therefore, as a rule, broken down before it can act. Exceptions – such as very small peptides from collagen that can be detected in the blood, or the semaglutide tablet with its excipient – are explained in the chapter after next.

### Natural, Nature-Identical, Modified

A simple distinction helps to classify the peptides in Part II:

1. **Nature-identical peptides** correspond exactly to an endogenous molecule, such as oxytocin or glutathione.
2. **Modified analogs** are derived from the natural model but deliberately altered – by exchanged amino acids, attached fatty acids, or ring-shaped structures. Almost all modern peptide drugs belong to this group.
3. **Foreign or newly designed peptides** come from other organisms (such as exendin-4 from the saliva of the Gila monster) or were newly developed in the laboratory (such as the cosmetic peptides or the dual and triple agonists tirzepatide and retatrutide).

This classification alone is not decisive for safety. But it helps to understand why human studies are indispensable for all three groups: even a nature-identical molecule behaves differently when it is supplied from outside in large amounts.

### Why Peptides Are Getting So Much Attention Right Now

Three developments explain the boom of recent years:

**First, the GLP-1 medications.** The results of drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide in overweight have brought peptides into the headlines. In the pivotal trial STEP 1, participants on semaglutide lost on average 14.9% of their body weight, compared with 2.4% on placebo (Wilding 2021, PMID 33567185). Such figures had never before been achieved with medications.

**Second, easy availability.** Short peptides can now be synthesized cheaply in many countries and are offered worldwide over the internet – often labeled "for research purposes only." What this means is covered in the chapter on the gray market.

**Third, social media.** Personal testimonials, before-and-after pictures, and influencer recommendations spread faster than scientific data. One review describes how digital advertising, gray-market access, and self-injection interact and normalize products whose identity, purity, and safety are unclear (Hailu 2026, PMID 42437212).

This book aims to offer a sober counterweight here: for each peptide, it examines what has actually been studied in humans – and what has not.

### Key Points in Brief

- Peptides are chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. From about 50 amino acids upward, they are usually called proteins.
- In the body, peptides act primarily as messengers via receptors on the cell surface.
- Natural peptides are broken down quickly. Modern peptide drugs are therefore almost always deliberately modified molecules.
- Peptides are chemically synthesized, produced by genetic engineering, or obtained from natural sources. The production route and purity influence safety.
- "Peptide" is a chemical description, not a seal of quality. Only good studies can show whether a particular peptide works in humans and is safe.
