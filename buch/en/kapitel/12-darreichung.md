## How Peptides Get into the Body – and Why That Is Difficult

Almost every peptide medication you know is injected: insulin, semaglutide, tirzepatide, teriparatide. This is neither a coincidence nor a preference of the pharmaceutical industry, but a consequence of biology. The human body is extremely good at breaking down proteins and peptides – after all, it lives by splitting the proteins in food into their building blocks. This chapter explains which hurdles a peptide must overcome on its way to its site of action, which routes medicine has found, and why many promises about drops, capsules, and creams should be viewed with caution.

### The Fundamental Problem: Bioavailability

A key term in this chapter is **bioavailability**. It indicates what proportion of an administered amount actually arrives unchanged in the bloodstream. With an infusion directly into a vein, this is by definition 100%. With all other routes, part of it is lost – through breakdown, through barriers, or because the substance is simply excreted again.

For peptides, bioavailability is particularly critical because three properties come together:

1. **They are sensitive to enzymes.** Peptide bonds are specifically cleaved by digestive enzymes such as pepsin in the stomach or trypsin in the small intestine, as well as by numerous enzymes in the blood and tissues.
2. **They are large.** Compared with classic drugs such as ibuprofen, most peptides are huge. Even a peptide of ten amino acids weighs more than 1,000 daltons.
3. **They are mostly water-soluble and often electrically charged.** This makes it difficult for them to pass through the fatty membranes of cells.

Every route of administration is an attempt to get around these hurdles. A review of oral peptides summarizes that protein and peptide drugs generally have to be administered by bypassing the digestive tract, which can impair treatment adherence (Drucker 2020, PMID 31848464).

### The Injection: Standard Route with Drawbacks

Most peptide medications are injected **subcutaneously**, i.e., into the fatty tissue under the skin – typically in the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm. From there, the drug enters the circulation via small blood vessels and lymphatic vessels. Bioavailability is high, and patients can administer the injection themselves after training. Less often, peptides are given into the muscle (**intramuscularly**) or directly into a vein (**intravenously**), for example in the hospital.

Although injection bypasses the digestive tract, it does not bypass the enzymes in the blood. Pharmaceutical research has therefore put a great deal of effort into making peptides last longer:

- **Exchanging amino acids** at the sites where enzymes attack.
- **Attaching fatty acids** so that the peptide binds to the blood protein albumin. Liraglutide and semaglutide use this principle (Knudsen 2019, PMID 31031702). In semaglutide, this results in a half-life of about one week (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186).
- **Coupling to larger molecules**, for example to parts of antibodies or to polymers such as polyethylene glycol. Dulaglutide is an example of a GLP-1 molecule bound to an antibody fragment.
- **Depot formulations**, in which the drug is embedded in tiny microspheres or gels from which it is released slowly.

For users, injections also come with obligations: the product must be sterile, the needle may only be used once, and many preparations must be stored refrigerated. Prefilled pens with set dose increments considerably reduce the risk of errors. How important this is is shown by a case series from a US poison control center: with semaglutide that had been dispensed by pharmacies as vials with syringes, tenfold overdoses occurred, partly because milliliters, "units," and milligrams were confused (Lambson 2023, PMID 37392810). More on this in the chapter on the gray market.

### The Tablet: The Most Difficult Route

For most people, a tablet would be the most convenient solution. For peptides, it is also the most demanding. Several obstacles lie in wait on the way from the mouth to the blood:

- **Stomach acid** can destroy the three-dimensional structure of peptides.
- **Pepsin** in the stomach and **pancreatic enzymes** in the small intestine break peptides down into small fragments.
- A **mucus layer** over the intestinal lining holds back large molecules.
- The **intestinal cells** themselves are tightly joined to one another. Large, water-soluble molecules can pass neither through the cells nor between them.
- Whatever does make it into the blood first passes through the **liver**, which can break down another portion.

Drucker's review names as possible solutions substances that increase permeability, inhibitors of digestive enzymes, and mucus- and cell-penetrating carrier peptides. In clinical studies, oral forms of insulin, calcitonin, parathyroid hormone, and vasopressin, among others, have been tested (Drucker 2020, PMID 31848464). The breakthrough for a widely used drug, however, came only with the semaglutide tablet.

### The SNAC Technology: How the Semaglutide Tablet Works

In addition to the drug, the semaglutide tablet contains an excipient called **SNAC** (sodium N-[8-(2-hydroxybenzoyl)amino]caprylate). A study that combined data from investigations in humans and dogs clarified what happens (Buckley 2018, PMID 30429357):

- Surprisingly, absorption does not take place in the intestine but **in the stomach**.
- It is limited to a small area **directly at the tablet surface**.
- There, SNAC locally buffers the stomach acid and thus **protects** semaglutide from breakdown by enzymes.
- SNAC increases the permeability of the gastric mucosa only **briefly**.
- Semaglutide passes **through the cells** into the blood without opening the junctions between cells.

A more recent review adds that SNAC raises the pH in the immediate vicinity, dampens the activation of pepsin, and helps semaglutide to exist as single molecules instead of clustering into larger assemblies that are less easily absorbed (Vahora 2026, PMID 42687069).

Despite this technology, the yield is low. The same review gives the absolute bioavailability of oral semaglutide as only about 0.4 to 1% – and that under optimized conditions: on an empty stomach and with little water. After a meal, practically no semaglutide can be detected in the blood (Vahora 2026, PMID 42687069). For this reason, strict rules for taking the tablet apply; they are described in the prescribing information and should be discussed with the treating physician.

The low bioavailability has a practical consequence: the tablet requires many times more drug. In the SUSTAIN-6 trial, participants received 0.5 or 1.0 mg semaglutide once a week as an injection (Marso 2016, PMID 27633186). In the tablet trial PIONEER 1, by contrast, daily doses of 3, 7, or 14 mg were studied (Aroda 2019, PMID 31186300). The fact that the long-term effect is nevertheless steady is due to the long half-life: day-to-day fluctuations in absorption largely even out over the week (Vahora 2026, PMID 42687069).

The tablet works, as the studies show. In PIONEER 1 with 703 people with type 2 diabetes, HbA1c fell after 26 weeks at the highest dose by 1.1 percentage points more than on placebo (Aroda 2019, PMID 31186300). In the OASIS 1 trial, a higher dose of 50 mg daily was tested in 667 adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes. After 68 weeks, mean weight loss was 15.1%, compared with 2.4% on placebo (Knop 2023, PMID 37385278). Gastrointestinal complaints occurred in 80% of participants on semaglutide and in 46% on placebo.

An important note for practice: the SNAC technology is tied to the exact composition of the tablet. An investigation of copycat products found considerably less drug than stated in several oral semaglutide preparations, as well as a different release behavior of semaglutide and SNAC, which could reduce absorption (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). A tablet that contains "semaglutide" is therefore not automatically as effective as the approved preparation.

In parallel with the peptide tablets, small non-peptide molecules are being developed that activate the same receptor and can be taken as ordinary tablets, such as orforglipron. They are presented for comparison in Part II and discussed in Part V in connection with the future of tablet therapies.

### The Food Exception: Short Peptides from Collagen and Milk

If peptides are broken down in the gastrointestinal tract, how can collagen peptides or milk peptides that are consumed as powder or drinks have any effect? The answer is nuanced.

Digestion produces not only individual amino acids but also very short fragments of two or three building blocks. Some of these are relatively resistant to enzymes and are absorbed via their own transport systems. For example, after consumption of collagen hydrolysate by five healthy volunteers, the dipeptides prolyl-hydroxyproline and hydroxyprolyl-glycine were measured in blood plasma (Sugihara 2012, PMID 22040651).

However, it does not automatically follow that these fragments exert a particular effect in the body – for example on the skin or the joints. Whether this is the case can only be clarified in controlled studies with clinical endpoints. These data are discussed in Part II in the chapter on collagen peptides. For larger peptides such as hormones, on the other hand, the rule is: if swallowed, they are digested.

### Under the Tongue: Little Evidence

The oral mucosa, especially under the tongue, has a good blood supply. Substances absorbed here bypass the stomach and liver. This works well for some small drugs, but for peptides the mucosa is a considerable barrier.

Nevertheless, semaglutide and tirzepatide are offered, especially in the USA, as drops or orally disintegrating tablets for use under the tongue. An analysis of offerings from US compounding pharmacies found 33 products that differ from the approved preparations. Of the 17 products with only one active ingredient, 82% were intended for use under the tongue and the rest as orally disintegrating tablets. The authors consider such a dosage form understandable in principle, but note that an advantage over the approved preparations has not been demonstrated and that data on safety and efficacy are lacking (Belcourt 2026, PMID 41689811).

### The Nasal Spray: Fast but Limited

The nasal mucosa has a large surface area with a good blood supply, and substances pass from there directly into the blood. Some peptide medications have used this route for a long time, such as the vasopressin analog desmopressin. Oxytocin is also being studied as a nasal spray.

The limitations, however, are clear:

- Absorption is low for larger peptides and varies greatly – depending on spray technique, a cold, or the amount of mucus.
- A large part of each spray is swallowed or carried away by the cilia.
- Frequently used irritants or excipients can strain the mucosa.

A particularly popular notion is that peptides could reach the brain "directly" via the nose. For some substances there are indications of such routes via the olfactory and facial nerves, but the data are thin. A much-cited critical review concluded for oxytocin that only very little of the oxytocin given via the nose arrives in the cerebrospinal fluid, while blood concentrations rise to values above the natural level – with possible effects on the gastrointestinal tract, heart, and reproductive organs (Leng 2016, PMID 26049207). The authors urge skepticism toward studies reporting behavioral effects and call for preregistered studies. This is relevant for all peptides marketed as a "nasal spray for the brain," such as Semax or Selank, which are covered in Part II.

### Inhalation: An Instructive Failure

The lungs offer an enormous surface area with a very thin barrier to the blood. For this reason, intensive work was done on inhalable insulin. A first product came onto the market but was soon withdrawn; other developments were stopped. A commentary from that time describes the advantages and disadvantages from the perspective of patients, physicians, industry, and payers – including the size of the inhalation devices and the costs (Heinemann 2008, PMID 19885220). Later, a different inhalable insulin was approved in the USA. For most other peptides, inhalation has so far played no role.

### The Skin: A Very Good Barrier

The skin is one of the densest organs of the body. Its outermost layer, the stratum corneum, consists of dead cells embedded in lipids – a kind of brickwork with mortar. A well-known rule of thumb from dermatology states that a substance must be smaller than about 500 daltons to pass through the skin to any significant extent. The authors justify this, among other things, by the fact that practically all known contact allergens and all drugs used in transdermal patches lie below this limit (Bos 2000, PMID 10839713).

For peptides this means: from about four to five amino acids upward, they generally lie above this limit. A tripeptide such as GHK can fall below it, while peptides such as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 or acetyl hexapeptide-8 lie well above it. Cosmetic manufacturers try to improve penetration through fatty acid attachments (as with the "palmitoyl" peptide) or special carrier systems. Whether the peptides reach their presumed target cells in deeper skin layers in sufficient quantities is a question that would have to be investigated separately for each product. The study data on this are discussed in Part II.

A rule of thumb is only a rule of thumb: there are exceptions, and aids such as microneedle patches can overcome the barrier. Such patches carry tiny needles that penetrate only the uppermost layer of skin. For peptides, they are still largely under development.

For drug peptides such as GLP-1 analogs, growth hormone secretagogues, or melanotan, the following applies: a simple cream or gel does not get them into the blood in any appreciable quantity. You should view internet offers claiming otherwise with great skepticism.

### Stability: The Underestimated Problem

Peptides are sensitive outside the body as well. They can decompose, change chemically (for example through oxidation), or clump into larger assemblies. Such clumping – experts speak of aggregation or fibril formation – can reduce efficacy and irritate the immune system. One investigation found a greater tendency to fibril formation and lower physical stability in copycat preparations of liraglutide than in the original (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664).

For this reason, approved peptide medications are manufactured, packaged, transported under refrigeration, and labeled with expiration dates under precisely defined conditions. Gray-market peptides, on the other hand, are usually sold as freeze-dried powder that has to be reconstituted by the user before use. Each of these steps – transport without refrigeration, storage, reconstitution, drawing up – is a possible source of error.

### Immune Reactions

Peptides are proteinaceous substances and can be recognized by the immune system as foreign, especially if they do not correspond exactly to an endogenous molecule or contain impurities. Possible consequences are reactions at the injection site, allergic reactions, or the formation of antibodies that can weaken the effect. In the analysis of copycat semaglutide, computationally predicted new binding sites for the immune system were found, indicating a possible risk of immune reactions (Hach 2024, PMID 39379664). Part IV covers immune reactions in detail.

### Overview: Routes of Administration Compared

| Route | Main problem | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Injection under the skin | needle, sterility, refrigeration | insulin, semaglutide, tirzepatide |
| Tablet | breakdown in the gastrointestinal tract | semaglutide with SNAC |
| Under the tongue | mucosa as a barrier | no robust data |
| Nasal spray | low, variable absorption | desmopressin, oxytocin |
| Inhalation | devices, lung safety | inhalable insulin |
| Skin | stratum corneum, molecular size | cosmetic peptides |

### Key Points in Brief

- Peptides are broken down in the digestive tract, are large, and pass poorly through membranes. That is why most peptide medications are injected.
- A long duration of action is achieved through targeted modifications of the molecule, such as fatty acid attachments for binding to albumin.
- The semaglutide tablet uses the excipient SNAC, which enables absorption in the stomach. At about 0.4 to 1%, bioavailability remains very low.
- With nasal sprays and creams, absorption of larger peptides is limited. Promises such as "directly into the brain" or "through the skin into the blood" are mostly unsupported.
- With peptides, stability, sterility, and purity are not minor matters but prerequisites for safety and efficacy.
