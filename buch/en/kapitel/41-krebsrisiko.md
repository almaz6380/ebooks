## Cancer Risk and Growth Signals

Many peptides are advertised precisely because they are supposed to stimulate growth: of muscles, tendons, blood vessels, nerve cells, or hair. But growth is not selective. The signaling pathways through which tissues heal and renew themselves are also used by cancer cells. This is why the same question comes up with almost every "healing," "anti-aging," or growth hormone peptide: can it promote cancer?

This chapter summarizes what is known about this – separated according to the four mechanisms that come up again and again in Part II: the growth hormone–IGF-1 axis, the formation of new blood vessels, the stimulation of pigment cells, and the effect of GLP-1 drugs on the thyroid and pancreas. At the end is an overview of the latest large analyses of GLP-1 drugs and cancer.

### How to assess the cancer question

Four points help in reading the following data.

**Initiating or promoting.** A substance can initiate cancer by damaging the genetic material. With peptides, another possibility is discussed more often: a growth signal could make an already existing, still undetected tumor grow faster – much as fertilizer acts on a plant that is already in the soil. For this second possibility, it is enough for the substance to stimulate cell division, cell survival, or the formation of new blood vessels.

**Time.** Cancer develops over years to decades. Most peptide studies run for weeks to a few years. Even large approval trials can hardly capture a slowly emerging risk. That requires registries and observational studies over long periods – which in turn are prone to bias.

**Detection bias.** People who are examined more often because of a drug also have tumors found more often. An example: a meta-analysis found slightly more colorectal cancer diagnoses in shorter GLP-1 trials and suggested as an explanation that gastrointestinal complaints led to more colonoscopies (Silverii 2025, PMID 40437949).

**The opposite direction.** Overweight increases the risk of at least 13 types of cancer. A drug that strongly reduces weight could therefore lower overall cancer risk, even if there is a small signal for a single organ. The two can only be considered separately.

### Growth hormone and IGF-1

Growth hormone acts to a large extent via IGF-1, insulin-like growth factor 1. IGF-1 stimulates cells to divide and protects them from programmed cell death. All peptides in Group C – sermorelin, tesamorelin, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, the GHRP family, mecasermin, and IGF-1 LR3 – as well as ibutamoren, discussed for comparison, raise IGF-1 directly or indirectly. The evidence on the cancer question comes from five sources.

**Naturally higher IGF-1 levels.** A meta-analysis of 21 studies found that people with high IGF-1 levels had a higher risk of prostate cancer (odds ratio 1.49 when comparing an IGF-1 level at the 75th with one at the 25th percentile) and of premenopausal breast cancer (1.65); the authors explicitly called the associations "modest" (Renehan 2004, PMID 15110491). In the UK Biobank with 206,263 women, breast cancer risk rose by 11% per 5 nmol/L more IGF-1 (Murphy 2020, PMID 32169310); for colorectal cancer, the same group found a similar association in almost 400,000 participants (Murphy 2020, PMID 31884074).

**Genetic analyses.** So-called Mendelian randomization uses gene variants that slightly raise IGF-1 levels throughout life as a natural experiment. Here, too, genetically higher IGF-1 was associated with somewhat more breast cancer (odds ratio 1.05 per 5 nmol/L) (Murphy 2020, PMID 32169310). This suggests that the association is not based solely on confounding circumstances.

**Too much growth hormone due to disease.** In acromegaly, an overproduction of growth hormone by a tumor of the pituitary gland, a meta-analysis of 23 studies found a cancer rate about half again as high (standardized ratio 1.5), with particularly high values for colorectal and thyroid cancer. Smaller and older studies, however, showed higher values than large ones, suggesting selection bias (Dal 2018, PMID 29590449).

**Too little IGF-1 due to disease.** Among people with Laron syndrome, who produce hardly any IGF-1 because of a genetic defect, only a single, non-fatal cancer occurred in Ecuador over 22 years of observation; among unaffected relatives, the proportion was 17% (Guevara-Aguirre 2011, PMID 21325617).

**Growth hormone as a drug.** Among 23,984 children treated with growth hormone in eight European countries, there was no clearly increased cancer risk in those without serious pre-existing conditions. Notable were individual cancer types (bone, bladder) and a rise in cancer mortality with higher daily doses in children who had previously had cancer (Swerdlow 2017, PMID 28187225). In 8,418 treated adults with pituitary insufficiency, cancer risk was not increased; the cancer rate was even below the population average (Child 2015, PMID 25810462).

What follows from this? People who have a genuine deficiency and receive replacement of a missing hormone do not, according to current knowledge, carry a clearly increased cancer risk. People who raise a normal level over years, on the other hand, move into the range in which observational and genetic studies show a moderately higher risk. For exactly this situation – healthy adults who use secretagogues for performance enhancement or "rejuvenation" – there are no long-term studies. Ibutamoren, for instance, raised IGF-1 in healthy older people to the level of young adults (Nass 2008, PMID 18981485); no cancer signal was reported, but the study was neither large nor long enough for that.

Two special cases deserve mention. With the approved mecasermin, benign and malignant neoplasms in treated children were reported after market launch; the prescribing information contains corresponding warnings, and it must not be used in active cancer (as of 2026, please check for current status; chapter Mecasermin). The laboratory product IGF-1 LR3 was modified so that it no longer binds to the binding proteins in the blood that normally buffer IGF-1. As a result, it acts more strongly and for longer at the receptor – theoretically exactly the unfavorable direction. This has never been studied in humans (chapter IGF-1 LR3).

### New blood vessel formation and other growth signals

A tumor that is to grow larger than a few millimeters needs its own blood vessels. This process is called angiogenesis. Some approved cancer drugs work precisely by blocking the most important signaling pathway of new blood vessel formation, the VEGF system. Conversely, substances that promote new blood vessel formation are suspected of favoring tumors.

**BPC-157.** In laboratory experiments, BPC-157 increased vessel density in chicken egg membranes, accelerated blood flow in a poorly supplied leg in rats, and increased the amount of VEGF receptor 2 in human vascular cells (Hsieh 2017, PMID 27847966). The Zagreb research group, from which most BPC-157 studies come, counters that BPC-157 regulates vessel formation rather than blindly boosting it, and has even shown anti-tumor effects in animal and cell experiments (Sikiric 2025, PMID 40573323). Long-term carcinogenicity studies, as required for drugs, have not been published for BPC-157. The question is open. See the chapter BPC-157.

**TB-500 and thymosin beta-4.** Thymosin beta-4 promotes cell migration and new blood vessel formation. In tissue samples from 138 people with bladder cancer, strong thymosin beta-4 expression was associated with advanced stage, lymph node involvement, and shorter survival (Wang 2016, PMID 27596300). In cultures of breast cancer cells, added thymosin beta-4 increased the cell count (Larsson 2007, PMID 17495241). This does not prove that externally supplied TB-500 promotes cancer, but it is a reason for caution in people with cancer or a high cancer risk. See the chapter TB-500.

**Other peptides with growth signals.** For several peptides from Part II, similar questions arise from the mechanism of action or from laboratory data:

| Peptide | Mechanism | Type of evidence | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dihexa | amplifies c-Met signaling, a known cancer driver | theoretical only, no human data | Dihexa |
| Epitalon | said to activate telomerase | cell culture: telomeres of breast cancer cells also lengthened | Epitalon |
| Humanin | protects cells from cell death | mice: faster tumor growth, more metastases | Humanin |
| LL-37 | inflammatory and growth signals | laboratory: promoting or inhibiting depending on cancer type | LL-37 |
| GHK-Cu | new blood vessel formation in animal experiments | theoretical only | GHK-Cu |
| ARA-290 | acts on the EPO receptor | theoretical, by analogy with EPO | ARA-290 |

Some of these indications are concrete. In mice with triple-negative breast cancer, administered humanin reduced cell death in the tumor, weakened chemotherapy, and accelerated tumor growth and lung metastases (Moreno Ayala 2020, PMID 32444831). In cell cultures, Epitalon lengthened the telomeres of healthy cells via the enzyme telomerase, and in breast cancer cells via a different pathway (Al-Dulaimi 2025, PMID 40908429); in a mouse study, by contrast, the number of spontaneous tumors did not increase (Anisimov 2003, PMID 14501183). For LL-37, a review describes promoting effects on tumors of the ovary, lung, breast, prostate, pancreas, and skin, but inhibiting effects on colorectal and gastric cancer (Chen 2018, PMID 29843147). For Dihexa, the connection to the c-Met system has been described (Wright 2015, PMID 25649658); there are no carcinogenicity studies.

All of these findings come from animal experiments, cell cultures, or the mechanism of action. None proves a cancer risk in humans. But they show that "natural," "endogenous," or "regenerative" does not automatically mean "harmless" – and that for none of these substances do the long-term data exist with which the question could be answered.

### Pigment cells: Melanotan, setmelanotide, and afamelanotide

The melanocortin peptides stimulate the pigment cells (melanocytes) of the skin. This is the desired effect with afamelanotide, a side effect with setmelanotide and bremelanotide, and the actual selling point with Melanotan II. Since melanoma arises from pigment cells, the question of skin cancer is obvious.

For the unapproved **Melanotan II**, there are several case reports of melanomas discovered during or shortly after use. One review counted four cases in which melanomas arose from existing moles, while emphasizing that conclusive proof of a connection is lacking (Habbema 2017, PMID 28266027). Among 179 published cases of suddenly appearing moles, some of which were attributed to Melanotan, five were associated with a melanoma (Burian 2019, PMID 31119650). Interpretation is difficult: many of those affected also used tanning beds or sunbathed intensively, which independently increases melanoma risk. And case reports cannot provide frequencies. See the chapter Melanotan II.

For the approved **afamelanotide**, more systematic data are available. In a study with 15 treated people and 103 moles, dermoscopy showed temporary changes that had regressed after one year; there were no signs of malignancy or new moles (Arisi 2021, PMID 33721252). With **setmelanotide**, no malignant skin changes occurred in an observation over up to 46 months (Kanti 2021, PMID 34058738). These data are reassuring, but small and short relative to the time it takes a melanoma to develop. The prescribing information provides for regular skin checks. They cannot be transferred to Melanotan II: a different substance, untested products, often combined with UV radiation.

### GLP-1 and the thyroid C cells

In rats and mice, GLP-1 receptor agonists led in long-term experiments to growths and tumors of the C cells of the thyroid. The mechanism is well understood: the C cells of rodents carry the GLP-1 receptor; its activation increases the release of calcitonin and cell division. In humans and monkeys, the receptor is considerably rarer on the C cells. Monkeys that received liraglutide for 20 months at concentrations more than 60 times higher than in humans did not develop C-cell hyperplasia (Bjerre Knudsen 2010, PMID 20203154).

This does not completely rule out an effect in humans. In human thyroid tissue, one study found the GLP-1 receptor in all samples of medullary thyroid carcinoma and C-cell hyperplasia, in 18% of papillary carcinomas, and in some of the C cells of healthy thyroids (Gier 2012, PMID 22031513).

The studies in humans do not yield a consistent picture:

- In the LEADER trial, calcitonin did not rise with liraglutide over up to five years, and no medullary carcinomas occurred (Hegedüs 2018, PMID 29279300).
- A French case-control study with 2,562 cases found, after one to three years of treatment, an increased risk of thyroid cancer overall (hazard ratio 1.58) and of medullary carcinoma (1.78) (Bezin 2023, PMID 36356111).
- A Scandinavian cohort study with 145,410 people on GLP-1 receptor agonists found no increased risk (hazard ratio 0.93; 1.33 versus 1.46 cases per 10,000 person-years). For the rare medullary carcinoma, the estimate was very uncertain (hazard ratio 1.19; confidence interval 0.37 to 3.86) (Pasternak 2024, PMID 38683947).
- A meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials lasting at least one year showed a signal for thyroid cancer (odds ratio 1.55), which was more pronounced in longer trials (Silverii 2025, PMID 40437949).

How does this fit together? Part of the signal could be due to more frequent examinations: people who are closely monitored for diabetes or obesity more often receive an ultrasound in which small, harmless papillary carcinomas are discovered. A small genuine risk, however, cannot be ruled out. For people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or with MEN 2 syndrome, a contraindication therefore continues to apply (as of 2026, please check for current status). See the chapter Liraglutide.

### Pancreas

After the early reports of pancreatitis with exenatide and liraglutide, an increased risk of pancreatic cancer was also feared. The data have not confirmed this so far. A meta-analysis of 62 randomized trials found no established increase in risk (relative risk 1.30; confidence interval 0.86 to 1.97); in a subgroup of studies with additional medications it was increased, without additional medications it was not (Wen 2025, PMID 40988099). The authors point out that many studies without a single event were not included in the calculation at all. In a large US cohort study, pancreatic cancer was even less common with GLP-1 receptor agonists than with insulin (hazard ratio 0.41) (Wang 2024, PMID 38967919) – although insulin is often used in longer-standing, more severe diabetes.

For neuroendocrine tumors, a rare group of tumors that arise in the pancreas among other places and in some cases carry the GLP-1 receptor, a review concludes that the available data show no general increase, but that important gaps exist, for example regarding hereditary predisposition (Rajan 2026, PMID 42541759).

### What new meta-analyses and large cohorts show on GLP-1 and cancer

With the wide use of GLP-1 drugs, several very large analyses have been published since 2024. They paint a predominantly reassuring picture, though one that is unclear on individual points.

| Study | Data | Result for cancer overall | Notable findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silverii 2025, PMID 40437949 | 50 RCTs, at least 52 weeks | no difference (odds ratio 1.05) | thyroid 1.55; uterus in obesity 0.24 |
| Wang 2024, PMID 38967919 | 1,651,452 people with type 2 diabetes | vs. insulin fewer for 10 of 13 cancer types | vs. metformin more kidney cancer (1.54) |
| Dai 2025, PMID 40839273 | 86,632 adults with obesity | less cancer (hazard ratio 0.83) | kidney cancer borderline increased (1.38) |
| Hsu 2026, PMID 42252247 | 161,798 adults with obesity without diabetes | fewer obesity-related cancers (0.59) | median follow-up 2 years |

How should these figures be read?

- **The randomized trials** show no overall influence on cancer risk. This is the most reliable result, but the trials are too short for cancer.
- **The large observational studies** tend to show less cancer with GLP-1 drugs. This fits with the idea that weight loss reduces obesity-related cancers. But it may also be because people who receive such drugs differ in many ways from the comparison groups. A follow-up of two years is very short for a statement about cancer prevention.
- **Kidney cancer** appears as a possible signal in two cohort studies. Whether this is a genuine effect remains to be seen.
- **Thyroid cancer** remains the open question, as described above.

Whether GLP-1 drugs actually protect against cancer cannot be said with the available data. On the question of whether they cause cancer, current knowledge suggests: for most cancer types no, for thyroid and kidney not conclusively resolved. For the new multi-agonists such as retatrutide, survodutide, or mazdutide, there are still too few treatment years to make separate statements.

### Key points in brief

- The best data on cancer risk exist, of all things, for the peptides for which the concern seems least justified: the approved GLP-1 drugs. For most cancer types they show no increased risk; the thyroid signal is unresolved.
- For the growth hormone–IGF-1 axis, a moderate association between persistently higher IGF-1 levels and some cancer types is well established. For healthy people who artificially raise IGF-1 over years, studies are lacking.
- For BPC-157, TB-500, Epitalon, humanin, Dihexa, and similar substances, there are only theoretical and animal-experimental indications – in both directions. Long-term data on carcinogenesis do not exist.
- For Melanotan II, there are melanoma case reports, but no frequency data. For the approved afamelanotide, small studies found no malignant changes.
- For people with active or past cancer, all growth-promoting peptides are particularly problematic without medical supervision.
