## Liraglutide

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** Modified human GLP-1 of 31 amino acids. It is 97% identical to the body's own hormone; at one position an amino acid has been exchanged, and at another a fatty acid with 16 carbon atoms is attached via a short linker.
- **Origin:** developed by Novo Nordisk (Knudsen 2019, PMID 31031702), produced by genetic engineering in yeast.
- **Mechanism of action:** GLP-1 receptor agonist. The fatty acid causes the molecule to bind to the blood protein albumin; as a result, one injection per day is sufficient.
- **Products:** Victoza (type 2 diabetes, up to 1.8 mg daily), Saxenda (weight management, up to 3.0 mg daily), Xultophy (fixed combination with insulin degludec). Copycat products have been available since 2024.
- **EU approval:** yes – type 2 diabetes (since 2009) and weight management (since 2015), in each case also for adolescents.
- **US approval:** yes – type 2 diabetes (since 2010, from age 10), reduction of cardiovascular risk in type 2 diabetes, and weight management (since 2014, from age 12). Generics are approved for both indications.
- **WADA:** not listed (status as of 2026; please check for current information).
- **Evidence level:** **A** – two large approval programs (LEAD, SCALE), one large outcomes trial (LEADER), studies in children and adolescents.

### How It Is Supposed to Work

Liraglutide acts like the body's own gut hormone GLP-1: it enhances glucose-dependent insulin release, inhibits glucagon, which raises blood sugar, slows gastric emptying, and dampens appetite in the brain. The basic mechanism of the class is described in the chapter Exenatide.

The difference lies in the structure. Whereas exenatide comes from the venom of a lizard and is only half identical to the human hormone, liraglutide is almost identical to the body's own GLP-1. The trick that makes it long-lasting is the attached fatty acid. It causes the molecule to attach to albumin in the blood and to cluster into small assemblies, thereby protecting it from breakdown by the enzyme DPP-4 and from excretion via the kidneys. As a result, the half-life increases from a few minutes to about half a day.

Effects in the brain are primarily responsible for weight loss. In mice and rats, labeled liraglutide reaches certain regions of the hypothalamus, the control center for hunger and satiety, and activates nerve cells there that promote satiety (Secher 2014, PMID 25202980). In humans, this manifests as reduced food intake; a meaningful increase in energy expenditure has not been demonstrated.

Semaglutide later emerged from liraglutide: the same basic idea, but with stronger albumin binding that allows one injection per week (Knudsen 2019, PMID 31031702).

### Human Studies

Liraglutide is among the best-studied peptide drugs. The PubMed query evaluated for this book (search term "liraglutide" in title or abstract, query date October 2026) yielded 613 human studies – more than for any other peptide in this book. ClinicalTrials.gov lists 517 registry entries. The following focuses on the approval and outcomes trials; in addition, there are hundreds of smaller studies on the liver, heart, brain, fertility, and eating disorders.

#### Type 2 Diabetes: The LEAD Program

For the diabetes approval, liraglutide was tested in six trials under the name LEAD (Liraglutide Effect and Action in Diabetes), each with 1.2 or 1.8 mg per day. Two are particularly revealing because they competed against therapies established at the time. In addition, there is the comparison with the DPP-4 inhibitor sitagliptin.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| LEAD-3 Mono (Garber 2009), PMID 18819705 | 746 people with early type 2 diabetes, double-blind vs. glimepiride | 52 weeks | HbA1c −1.14 (1.8 mg) vs. −0.51 percentage points |
| LEAD-6 (Buse 2009), PMID 19515413 | open-label vs. exenatide twice daily | 26 weeks | HbA1c −1.12 vs. −0.79; weight −3.24 vs. −2.87 kg |
| Pratley 2010, PMID 20417856 | 658 people on metformin, open-label vs. sitagliptin | 26 weeks | HbA1c −1.50 (1.8 mg) vs. −0.90; nausea 27% vs. 5% |

In these trials, long-term blood sugar (HbA1c) fell by 1.1 to 1.5 percentage points on liraglutide. In LEAD-3 (Garber 2009, PMID 18819705), liraglutide was the first diabetes drug to prove superior to the sulfonylurea glimepiride, which lowers blood sugar independently of meals and more often leads to hypoglycemia. Against sitagliptin (Pratley 2010, PMID 20417856), the advantage was 0.6 percentage points, albeit with considerably more nausea.

#### Head-to-Head Comparisons Within the GLP-1 Class

Liraglutide is the most frequently used comparator in its class. The results yield a clear ranking: liraglutide is stronger than exenatide, roughly as strong as dulaglutide, and weaker than semaglutide.

| Study | Design | Duration | Result (liraglutide vs. comparator) |
|---|---|---|---|
| LEAD-6 (Buse 2009), PMID 19515413 | vs. exenatide twice daily | 26 weeks | HbA1c −1.12 vs. −0.79; target < 7% in 54% vs. 43% |
| DURATION-6 (Buse 2013), PMID 23141817 | 911 people, vs. exenatide weekly | 26 weeks | HbA1c −1.48 vs. −1.28; nausea 21% vs. 9% |
| AWARD-6 (Dungan 2014), PMID 25018121 | 599 people, vs. dulaglutide weekly | 26 weeks | HbA1c −1.36 vs. −1.42; equivalent |
| PIONEER 4 (Pratley 2019), PMID 31186120 | 711 people, vs. semaglutide tablet and placebo | 52 weeks | HbA1c −1.1 vs. −1.2 after 26 weeks; weight −3.1 vs. −4.4 kg |
| SUSTAIN 10 (Capehorn 2020), PMID 31539622 | liraglutide 1.2 mg vs. semaglutide 1.0 mg weekly | 30 weeks | HbA1c −1.0 vs. −1.7; weight −1.9 vs. −5.8 kg |
| STEP 8 (Rubino 2022), PMID 35015037 | 338 people with obesity without diabetes, liraglutide 3.0 mg vs. semaglutide 2.4 mg | 68 weeks | Weight −6.4% vs. −15.8% |

In LEAD-6 (Buse 2009, PMID 19515413), liraglutide lowered fasting glucose considerably more than exenatide twice daily, but lowered the rise in glucose after breakfast and dinner less – an indication that short-acting forms act more strongly via gastric emptying. Nausea lasted for a shorter time with liraglutide, and mild hypoglycemia was less frequent (25.5% vs. 33.6% of participants).

Against dulaglutide, liraglutide was equivalent in AWARD-6 (Dungan 2014, PMID 25018121), with similar tolerability; nausea occurred in 18% versus 20%.

Against semaglutide, liraglutide lost in all comparisons. This was most evident for weight in STEP 8 (Rubino 2022, PMID 35015037): with the dose approved for obesity in each case, participants on semaglutide lost on average 15.8%, those on liraglutide 6.4% (placebo: 1.9%). A loss of at least 15% was achieved by 55.6% versus 12.0%. Treatment had been discontinued prematurely by 27.6% on liraglutide and 13.5% on semaglutide. With the semaglutide tablet, the difference in PIONEER 4 (Pratley 2019, PMID 31186120) was smaller: for HbA1c, the tablet was not worse according to the main analysis method, and for weight it was superior by 1.2 kg. Details of these trials can be found in the chapter Semaglutide.

#### LEADER: Heart, Circulation, and Kidneys

LEADER was the first large trial to show protection against cardiovascular events for a GLP-1 receptor agonist. It changed the role of the entire class in diabetes guidelines.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| LEADER (Marso 2016), PMID 27295427 | 9,340 people with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk, RCT vs. placebo | median 3.8 years | Major cardiovascular events 13.0% vs. 14.9%; HR 0.87 (0.78–0.97) |
| LEADER, kidney analysis (Mann 2017), PMID 28854085 | the same 9,340 people, prespecified analysis | median 3.84 years | Kidney endpoint 268 vs. 337 cases; HR 0.78, mainly less new macroalbuminuria |

The primary endpoint – death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or stroke – occurred in 608 of 4,668 people (13.0%) on liraglutide and in 694 of 4,672 (14.9%) on placebo (Marso 2016, PMID 27295427). This corresponds to a relative risk reduction of 13%. The effect was driven mainly by fewer cardiovascular deaths (4.7% vs. 6.0%; HR 0.78). All-cause mortality was also lower (8.2% vs. 9.6%; HR 0.85). The individual components of myocardial infarction, stroke, and hospitalization for heart failure were not significantly reduced on their own.

In absolute numbers, this means: over almost four years, about two in a hundred people treated had one fewer major cardiovascular event. For a high-risk group, this is a relevant effect.

The kidney analysis (Mann 2017, PMID 28854085) showed fewer cases of newly occurring heavy protein excretion in the urine (macroalbuminuria). Hard kidney endpoints such as the need for dialysis were rare and did not differ clearly. Acute kidney failure occurred about as often as on placebo.

Not every cardiac group benefits. In the FIGHT trial with 300 people who had recently been hospitalized for severe heart failure with markedly reduced pump function, liraglutide did not improve the course (Margulies 2016, PMID 27483064). There were even somewhat more rehospitalizations for heart failure (41% vs. 34%), although the difference was not statistically significant. The authors saw no basis for its use in this situation.

#### Obesity: The SCALE Program

At 3.0 mg per day – almost twice the highest diabetes dose – liraglutide was approved in 2014/2015 as the first GLP-1 medication for weight reduction. The basis was the SCALE study program.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| SCALE Obesity and Prediabetes (Pi-Sunyer 2015), PMID 26132939 | 3,731 adults with obesity without diabetes, RCT vs. placebo | 56 weeks | −8.4 kg vs. −2.8 kg; ≥ 5% loss in 63.2% vs. 27.1% |
| SCALE, 3-year part (le Roux 2017), PMID 28237263 | 2,254 people with prediabetes | 160 weeks | New diabetes in 2% vs. 6%; weight −6.1% vs. −1.9% |
| SCALE Diabetes (Davies 2015), PMID 26284720 | 846 people with type 2 diabetes and overweight | 56 weeks | −6.0% (3.0 mg) vs. −2.0% |
| SCALE Maintenance (Wadden 2013), PMID 23812094 | 422 people after a diet with ≥ 5% loss | 56 weeks | additional −6.2% vs. −0.2% |
| SCALE Sleep Apnea (Blackman 2016), PMID 27005405 | adults with obesity and moderate to severe sleep apnea | 32 weeks | Breathing pauses −12.2 vs. −6.1 per hour; weight −5.7% vs. −1.6% |

In the largest trial (Pi-Sunyer 2015, PMID 26132939), participants on liraglutide lost on average 8.4 kg, compared with 2.8 kg on placebo – in each case with diet and exercise counseling. One third (33.1%) lost more than 10% of their body weight, compared with 10.6% on placebo. Serious adverse events occurred in 6.2% versus 5.0%.

Participants with prediabetes (a precursor of diabetes) were treated for three years (le Roux 2017, PMID 28237263). On liraglutide, 2% developed type 2 diabetes, compared with 6% on placebo. However, the study has an important weakness: only half of the participants (50%) remained until the end, and those who dropped out were not followed further. The results should therefore be read with caution.

In people with type 2 diabetes, weight loss was smaller (Davies 2015, PMID 26284720) – a pattern that is repeated with all GLP-1 medications. In SCALE Maintenance (Wadden 2013, PMID 23812094), liraglutide helped people maintain their weight after a diet phase: 81.4% maintained the weight loss previously achieved, compared with 48.9% on placebo.

In retrospect, the weight effect of liraglutide seems modest now that semaglutide and tirzepatide achieve on average 15 to over 20%. For its time, it was a breakthrough: no previously approved drug had shown similar values in trials of this size.

#### Children and Adolescents

Liraglutide is one of the few drugs in this class that have been specifically studied in children and adolescents.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| ELLIPSE (Tamborlane 2019), PMID 31034184 | 134 children and adolescents (10 to under 17 years) with type 2 diabetes, RCT vs. placebo | 52 weeks | HbA1c −1.06 percentage points vs. placebo after 26 weeks, −1.30 after 52 weeks |
| SCALE Teens (Kelly 2020), PMID 32233338 | 251 adolescents (12 to under 18 years) with obesity, RCT vs. placebo | 56 weeks | BMI reduction ≥ 5% in 43.3% vs. 18.7%; weight −4.50 kg vs. placebo |
| SCALE Kids (Fox 2025), PMID 39258838 | 82 children (6 to under 12 years) with obesity, RCT vs. placebo | 56 weeks | BMI −5.8% vs. +1.6% |

In SCALE Teens (Kelly 2020, PMID 32233338), BMI rose again after discontinuation, more so than on placebo. Gastrointestinal complaints affected 64.8% versus 36.5%; 10.4% discontinued treatment because of side effects. There was one suicide in the liraglutide group, which the investigators classified as probably unrelated to the treatment.

In SCALE Kids (Fox 2025, PMID 39258838), the body weight of the growing children increased on average by 1.6% on liraglutide and by 10.0% on placebo. Gastrointestinal complaints occurred in 80% versus 54%, serious adverse events in 12% versus 8%. In children, the questions of how long such treatment should last and what long-term effects there are on growth and bones arise in particular. Among other things, a study on bone metabolism in 12- to 21-year-olds on GLP-1 therapy is ongoing (NCT06903923).

#### Other Areas of Use in Studies

- **Fatty liver inflammation (NASH/MASH):** In the small phase 2 trial LEAN with 52 participants, the inflammation on liver biopsy resolved in 39% on liraglutide and in 9% on placebo; progression of scarring (fibrosis) was less frequent (9% vs. 36%) (Armstrong 2016, PMID 26608256). Larger trials were later conducted with semaglutide.
- **Alzheimer's disease:** In the phase 2b trial ELAD with 204 people without diabetes, the primary endpoint, glucose metabolism in the brain, did not change (Edison 2026, PMID 41326666). In a secondary endpoint on executive function, liraglutide performed better, but not for activities of daily living or dementia severity. Secondary endpoints without correction for multiple testing are only indications.
- **Alcohol and other addictions:** In a Swedish registry study of 227,866 people with alcohol dependence, the use of liraglutide was associated with a 28% lower risk of alcohol-related hospital admissions (Lähteenvuo 2025, PMID 39535805). Observational data of this kind show associations, not cause-and-effect relationships.

### Animal and Laboratory Studies

Much of today's understanding of GLP-1 action in the brain comes from animal experiments with liraglutide. In rats and mice, it was shown that, despite its size, the molecule reaches certain brain regions without a tight blood-brain barrier and stimulates appetite-suppressing nerve cells there (Secher 2014, PMID 25202980).

The findings on the thyroid had particularly far-reaching consequences. In long-term experiments with rats and mice, liraglutide led to proliferation of the C cells (which produce the hormone calcitonin) and to C-cell tumors. Investigation of the mechanism revealed that rodent C cells carry abundant GLP-1 receptors, whereas those of humans and monkeys hardly do (Bjerre Knudsen 2010, PMID 20203154). In cynomolgus monkeys, 20 months of liraglutide at an exposure more than 60 times higher than in humans did not lead to C-cell proliferation. Nevertheless, since then a warning about medullary thyroid carcinoma has been included in the prescribing information for the entire class, especially in the USA.

Further animal studies have dealt with cardioprotection, neuroprotection (Alzheimer's and Parkinson's models), fatty liver, and kidney damage. As with exenatide, transferability to humans is limited: the Alzheimer's trial ELAD, for example, missed its primary endpoint even though the animal data were positive.

### Risks and Side Effects

**Established from randomized trials:**

- **Gastrointestinal complaints:** Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and constipation are the most common side effects and usually the reason for discontinuing therapy (Marso 2016, PMID 27295427). They occur mainly at the start and after dose increases. At the higher obesity dose, they are more frequent than at the diabetes dose (Davies 2015, PMID 26284720).
- **Gallbladder:** A meta-analysis of 76 randomized trials with 103,371 participants found a 37% increased relative risk for gallbladder and biliary diseases such as gallstones and cholecystitis for the GLP-1 class (He 2022, PMID 35344001). The risk was greater at higher doses, with longer use, and in weight-loss trials (relative risk 2.29). Part of this is likely related to the rapid weight loss itself.
- **Hypoglycemia:** rare on its own, more frequent in combination with sulfonylureas or insulin.
- **Heart rate:** Liraglutide slightly increases the resting pulse by a few beats per minute; in view of the LEADER results, the clinical significance is considered small.

**Investigated but uncertain or not confirmed:**

- **Pancreatitis:** In LEADER, it did not occur more frequently on liraglutide than on placebo (Marso 2016, PMID 27295427). An older meta-analysis likewise found no increased risk (Alves 2012, PMID 23010561). The prescribing information nevertheless lists it as a rare side effect; severe persistent abdominal pain requires medical evaluation.
- **Thyroid cancer:** The animal findings are described above. In humans, the data are contradictory. A French case-control study with 2,562 cases found an increased risk of thyroid cancer overall (HR 1.58) and of the rare medullary carcinoma (HR 1.78) after one to three years of GLP-1 therapy (Bezin 2023, PMID 36356111). A meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials also showed a signal (odds ratio 1.55) (Silverii 2025, PMID 40437949). It is being debated whether part of this is due to more frequent examinations in those treated. According to the prescribing information, people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma should not use GLP-1 receptor agonists.
- **Psychiatric side effects and suicidal thoughts:** Following individual reports, the European and US authorities examined a possible connection in 2023/2024 and found no evidence of a causal relationship (status as of 2026; please check for current information). In a Scandinavian cohort study with 124,517 people on GLP-1 receptor agonists, the risk of suicide was not increased compared with SGLT2 inhibitors (HR 1.25; confidence interval 0.83–1.88) (Ueda 2024, PMID 39226030). A 2026 meta-analysis likewise found no association in randomized trials (Choudhury 2026, PMID 41862354). The observation remains subject to further monitoring.

**Notes for practice:** Liraglutide is not indicated during pregnancy. Like all GLP-1 receptor agonists, it slows gastric emptying, which can play a role during anesthesia and in the absorption of other medications. The prescribing information and medical supervision are authoritative, including for the gradual dose escalation.

### Legal Status

- **EU:** Victoza has been approved since 2009 for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, Saxenda since 2015 for weight management in adults and adolescents. Both are prescription-only.
- **Germany:** For diabetes, statutory health insurers reimburse liraglutide. Weight-reduction drugs, by contrast, are in principle excluded from reimbursement under Section 34 of Book V of the Social Code (SGB V); insured persons generally have to pay for Saxenda themselves (status as of 2026; please check for current information).
- **Austria:** prescription-only; coverage of costs for weight management is restricted and subject to conditions (please check for current information).
- **USA:** Victoza and Saxenda approved, Victoza additionally for reducing cardiovascular risk. Generics of the diabetes form have been on the market since 2024, and of the obesity form since 2025.
- **Generics in Europe:** Since liraglutide is a synthetically or recombinantly producible peptide whose patent protection has expired, copycat products are also coming onto the market in Europe. The situation differs from country to country; please check for current information.
- **Sport (WADA):** not listed (status as of 2026; please check for current information).

The generics make liraglutide a special case in its class: it is the first GLP-1 drug with proven cardiovascular benefit that can become considerably cheaper. This is particularly important in countries with limited health budgets, even though the daily injection and the smaller effect compared with semaglutide remain disadvantages. More on patent expirations and prices in Part V.

### Ongoing Studies and Outlook

**Established (registered studies):** There are hardly any new approval trials by Novo Nordisk; research is mainly carried out by hospitals and universities. Examples:

- A study with 890 women with obesity, unfulfilled desire for children, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is testing liraglutide before fertility treatment (NCT06742710).
- A phase 3 trial with 260 older people with diabetes is investigating whether liraglutide can prevent delirium after heart surgery (NCT06361238).
- In a hereditary form of diabetes (MODY), liraglutide is being studied in a phase 2 trial (NCT07029009).
- A large pragmatic trial with 6,000 participants is comparing treatment strategies for preventing heart and kidney disease in type 2 diabetes (NCT05390892).
- Long-term follow-up of the SCALE Kids participants is continuing (NCT04775082).

**Assessment:** In wealthy countries, liraglutide is increasingly being displaced by weekly drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. Thanks to generics, however, it is likely to enjoy a second career as a low-cost alternative – especially where the newer drugs are unaffordable or unavailable. Scientifically, it remains an important comparator standard.

### Conclusion

Liraglutide was the first almost human-identical GLP-1 drug and the first for which a large trial showed protection against cardiovascular events (LEADER, 9,340 participants, relative risk reduction of 13%). As Saxenda, it was the first GLP-1 medication for weight reduction; on average, participants without diabetes lost about 8 kg in one year. In direct comparisons, it is superior to exenatide, on a par with dulaglutide, and clearly inferior to semaglutide. The most common side effects are gastrointestinal complaints, along with an increased gallbladder risk; the question of thyroid cancer has not been conclusively resolved. With the expiry of patent protection, it is becoming the first low-cost option in its class – treatment nevertheless belongs in the hands of a physician.
