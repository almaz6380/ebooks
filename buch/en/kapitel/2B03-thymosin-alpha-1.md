## Thymosin Alpha-1 (Thymalfasin)

Thymosin alpha-1 is a special case in this group. Unlike BPC-157 or TB-500, it is an approved medicine in a number of countries (brand name Zadaxin), it has been tested in dozens of randomized trials in thousands of people, and in China it is part of everyday practice in some hospitals. At the same time, this very peptide shows how a picture changes when small, older studies are superseded by large, carefully conducted ones. In Europe and the USA it is not approved, but it is marketed as an immune booster in the "longevity" and biohacking scene.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** peptide of 28 amino acids, acetylated at the beginning (Iino 2005, PMID 15850471); fragment of the larger protein prothymosin alpha.
- **Origin:** isolated in the 1970s from an extract of the thymus gland ("thymosin fraction 5"); today chemically synthesized. Active substance name thymalfasin.
- **Mechanism of action:** immunomodulator – intended to promote the maturation and activity of T cells and dendritic cells.
- **Approval EU:** no centralized approval. **USA:** not approved; the FDA restricted its use in compounding pharmacies in 2023 (Dinetz 2024, PMID 38308608). **Other countries:** approved as Zadaxin, mainly in China and other Asian countries, usually for chronic hepatitis B and as adjunctive therapy to strengthen the immune system.
- **WADA:** not explicitly listed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level: B** – formally, many randomized trials are available; however, the large, methodologically best trials missed their primary endpoints, and the positive results come mainly from small studies with a high risk of bias.

### How it is supposed to work

The thymus gland behind the breastbone is the "school" of T cells: here these white blood cells learn to distinguish foreign from self. The thymus shrinks with age. Early on, it was suspected that messengers from the thymus control the body's defenses, and they were sought in thymus extracts. Thymosin alpha-1 was one of the first substances identified in this way.

In laboratory and animal experiments, thymosin alpha-1 activates dendritic cells – the "scouts" of the immune system – via so-called Toll-like receptors, increases the expression of recognition molecules on cells and enhances the effect of interferon (Garaci 2007, PMID 17600290). From this, the three major application ideas were derived:

1. **Chronic viral infections** (hepatitis B and C), in which the immune system does not eliminate the virus on its own.
2. **Sepsis and severe infections**, in which an initial overreaction is often followed by a phase of immune weakness.
3. **Cancer**, as a supplement to chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy.

The half-life in the blood is about two hours; after 24 hours the level is back at baseline (Ancell 2001, PMID 11381492).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** The automated search found **90** human studies in PubMed and **66** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov, plus 15 meta-analyses and systematic reviews. A large proportion of the studies come from China and are small. A 2024 review summarizing over 30 studies with more than 11,000 people was explicitly written to argue against the FDA restriction (Dinetz 2024, PMID 38308608) – it should therefore not be read as a neutral assessment.

#### Hepatitis B and C

The approvals in Asia are based mainly on studies of chronic hepatitis B from the 1990s. A randomized trial with 98 people found a complete virological response 18 months after the start in 40.6 percent after six months of treatment compared with 9.4 percent in the untreated control group (Chien 1998, PMID 9581695). The double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 3 trial could not confirm this: 14 percent vs. 4 percent, not statistically significant; the authors themselves write that the results did not confirm earlier observations (Mutchnick 1999, PMID 10607256).

A meta-analysis of four small trials with 199 people found that thymosin alpha-1 performed worse than interferon alpha at the end of treatment but better six months afterward (Yang 2008, PMID 18078676). Today the question is largely obsolete: modern tablets against hepatitis B (such as entecavir) reliably suppress the virus. In the large trial with 690 people with liver cirrhosis, adding thymosin alpha-1 to entecavir brought no advantage in the primary endpoint of liver decompensation, liver cancer or death (Wu 2018, PMID 30063860). A meta-analysis of Chinese studies on the same combination did find better response rates after 24 weeks, but no difference anymore after 48 to 52 weeks (Peng 2020, PMID 33076834).

In a particularly severe form – acute-on-chronic liver failure in hepatitis B – transplant-free 90-day survival in an open-label trial with 120 people was 75.0 percent on thymosin alpha-1 compared with 53.4 percent; new infections were less frequent (Chen 2022, PMID 35616850). The trial was not blinded, and in an analysis that took competing risks into account, the survival difference was not significant.

In hepatitis C, combination with interferon improved values at the end of treatment in smaller trials (Sherman 1998, PMID 9537454; Andreone 2004, PMID 14738560), but sustained response did not differ substantially. Since direct-acting tablets became available that eliminate hepatitis C in the vast majority of cases, thymosin alpha-1 no longer plays a role here.

#### Sepsis

Sepsis illustrates the development of the evidence particularly well. Early meta-analyses of predominantly small Chinese studies found markedly lower mortality (Li 2015, PMID 25532482: relative risk 0.68 in 12 trials with 1,480 people), but themselves pointed to the poor quality of the studies. The first larger randomized trial (ETASS) with 361 people found a 28-day mortality of 26.0 vs. 35.0 percent – just at the threshold of statistical significance, single-blind (Wu 2013, PMID 23327199).

The same research group then planned the largest and best trial to date: TESTS, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 1,106 adults in 22 centers. Result: 28-day mortality was 23.4 percent on thymosin alpha-1 and 24.1 percent on placebo – no difference. No secondary endpoint differed either (Wu 2025, PMID 39814420). In subgroups, there were hints of benefits in people over 60 and in people with diabetes, but even disadvantages in those under 60 – such subgroup findings are uncertain.

A 2025 meta-analysis with 11 randomized trials found lower mortality overall, but not when only the high-quality or only the multicenter trials were considered; the available data were too limited for a definitive conclusion (Gu 2025, PMID 40969554). Many Chinese studies also combine thymosin alpha-1 with ulinastatin, another agent used in China; network meta-analyses rate the certainty of these results as predominantly low (Luo 2026, PMID 42205841).

#### COVID-19

During the pandemic, thymosin alpha-1 was widely used in China. An early retrospective analysis of 76 severely ill patients in Wuhan reported a mortality of 11.1 vs. 30.0 percent (Liu 2020, PMID 32442287). A larger retrospective study in five hospitals with over 2,200 people found the opposite: after adjustment for confounders, treatment was associated with a higher rate of non-recovery (Liu 2021, PMID 34408744). A small randomized pilot study with 49 people found faster increases in T helper cells, but no significant difference in recovery (Shehadeh 2023, PMID 36056913).

Two meta-analyses reached opposite conclusions: one with 9 studies and 5,352 people found no effect on mortality and advises against use in hospitalized adults (Shang 2023, PMID 36527881); another with 8 studies found lower mortality, but with very high heterogeneity among the studies (Soeroto 2023, PMID 37845598). Almost all data come from non-randomized observational studies. A benefit in COVID-19 is therefore not established.

#### Cancer

The largest randomized cancer trial tested thymosin alpha-1 in metastatic melanoma in addition to chemotherapy with dacarbazine. In two Tα1 groups, more tumors responded than in the control group (10 and 12 vs. 4 responses); median overall survival was 9.4 versus 6.6 months, but the difference was not significant (Maio 2010, PMID 20194853). No approval followed, and melanoma therapy has since changed fundamentally through immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Meta-analyses of Chinese studies on lung and gastric cancer report better response rates, better quality of life and fewer chemotherapy side effects when thymic peptides were added (Zeng 2019, PMID 31326719: 27 studies, 1,925 people; Wang 2026, PMID 42827862: 20 studies, 1,704 people). However, both papers rate the quality of the evidence as moderate to very low, and data on long-term survival are largely lacking. A review of cancer studies from 2016 to 2026 found predominantly retrospective observational studies and case reports from China and inconsistent recording of side effects (Kim 2026, PMID 42797537). Newer Phase 2 trials combine thymosin alpha-1 with immune checkpoint inhibitors, for example before surgery for gastric cancer with 30 people (Xu 2026, PMID 41749205); without a control group, the contribution of thymosin alpha-1 cannot be read from this.

#### Vaccine enhancement

As early as 1989, 90 men between 65 and 99 years of age received thymosin alpha-1 or placebo in addition to influenza vaccination; no side effects were observed (Gravenstein 1989, PMID 2642497). This idea is being taken up again today, for example in dialysis patients in connection with COVID-19 (Tuthill 2023, PMID 36881981) and in an ongoing trial on booster vaccination in older people (NCT06821100). Robust outcome data – i.e., fewer illnesses or deaths – are lacking.

### Animal and laboratory studies

Since thymosin alpha-1 has been studied in humans for decades, animal studies play a lesser role in the assessment. They were important for the cancer idea: in mouse models of lung cancer, leukemia and melanoma and in a rat model of liver metastases, the combination of thymosin alpha-1 with interferon or interleukin-2 and chemotherapy worked better than the individual treatments (Garaci 2007, PMID 17600290). These findings have so far not been convincingly replicated in humans.

### Risks and side effects

#### Documented

Thymosin alpha-1 is considered well tolerated in the studies. In the older studies, usually only local irritation at the injection site was observed (Ancell 2001, PMID 11381492). In the large sepsis trial TESTS, the safety endpoints did not differ from placebo (Wu 2025, PMID 39814420). In hepatitis B, there were sometimes fluctuations in liver values, which were interpreted as an expression of the immune response (Iino 2005, PMID 15850471).

#### Points to note

- **Subgroups with possible harm:** In TESTS, mortality in people under 60 was higher on thymosin alpha-1 than on placebo (hazard ratio 1.67) (Wu 2025, PMID 39814420); in COVID-19, treatment was associated with a worse course in a large observational study (Liu 2021, PMID 34408744). Both may be chance or bias, but they show that "harmless immune booster" is not an established description.
- **Autoimmune diseases and transplantation:** A substance intended to stimulate the immune system could theoretically aggravate autoimmune diseases or favor the rejection of transplanted organs. Systematic data on this are lacking.
- **Combination with immunotherapy:** One review refers to a case report in which a severe immune reaction in several organs occurred under combination treatment, without thymosin alpha-1 being established with certainty as the cause (Kim 2026, PMID 42797537).
- **Gray market:** Outside the countries with approval, thymosin alpha-1 is sold as a "research peptide." The studies on Zadaxin do not apply to these products – purity, content and sterility are unknown.

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** no approval as a medicine. A prescription in an individual case via individual import from a country with approval may be possible under national law under narrow conditions; sale as a "research peptide" for use in humans is not permitted.
- **USA:** no FDA approval. In 2023, the FDA placed thymosin alpha-1, together with other peptides, on the list of substances with safety concerns for compounding; proponents criticize this (Dinetz 2024, PMID 38308608). The classification of peptides continues to be discussed in the FDA advisory committee (Willis 2026, PMID 42752426).
- **China and other countries:** approved as Zadaxin or thymalfasin; in China there are also a number of generic preparations.
- **WADA:** not explicitly on the Prohibited List. Athletes should nevertheless check with their national anti-doping agency before use.

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed (according to the registry):**

- **Colorectal cancer after surgery (NCT05086614):** Phase 3 trial by Fudan University in Shanghai, 2,500 people with stage II or III colorectal cancer planned, primary endpoint disease-free survival after three years; estimated completion March 2027. The trial is only single-blind.
- **Cancer and immunotherapy:** numerous smaller Chinese Phase 2 trials combining thymosin alpha-1 with immune checkpoint inhibitors and chemotherapy or radiotherapy, for example in triple-negative breast cancer with 572 people planned (NCT07884201).
- **Sepsis:** a smaller trial on recovery of immune function in 60 people (NCT07866118).
- **Vaccine response in older people (NCT06821100):** Phase 1, 75 people planned.

**Author's assessment:** The large colorectal cancer trial is the most important open question. If it is positive, it would be the first major evidence of a benefit in a cancer – though with reservations because of the lack of double-blinding. In sepsis, TESTS is likely to have answered the question for broad use for the time being; future studies will focus on subgroups with demonstrated immune weakness. Approval in the EU or the USA is not to be expected in the next few years.

### Conclusion

Thymosin alpha-1 is the best-studied peptide in this group and an approved medicine in some countries. The early, small studies looked promising, but the large, carefully blinded trials showed no benefit in their primary endpoints in sepsis (Wu 2025, PMID 39814420), severe pancreatitis (Ke 2022, PMID 35713670) and hepatitis B cirrhosis (Wu 2018, PMID 30063860). In COVID-19 and cancer, positive claims are based predominantly on observational studies and low-quality studies. The peptide is well tolerated in studies – but a proven benefit as a general "immune booster" for healthy people does not exist.
