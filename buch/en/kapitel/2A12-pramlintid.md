## Pramlintide

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** synthetic analog of the human hormone amylin consisting of 37 amino acids. Amino acids were substituted at three positions (modeled on rat amylin) so that the molecule does not clump like natural human amylin. It acts only briefly and is injected before main meals.
- **Origin:** developed by Amylin Pharmaceuticals; today part of AstraZeneca's portfolio. Brand name in the USA: Symlin.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at amylin receptors – slows gastric emptying, dampens glucagon release after meals and promotes the feeling of satiety.
- **Approval EU:** no – pramlintide was never approved in the EU.
- **Approval USA:** yes, since 2005 as an add-on to mealtime insulin in type 1 and type 2 diabetes.
- **WADA:** not listed by name; because of its US approval, it does not fall under S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **A** – several large randomized trials, approval in the USA.

### How it is supposed to work

The beta cells of the pancreas release not only insulin but, in the same rhythm, also amylin. In type 1 diabetes, the beta cells are destroyed – so both hormones are missing, but as a rule only insulin is replaced. In advanced type 2 diabetes, amylin secretion is also reduced.

After a meal, amylin acts in three ways: it slows gastric emptying so that sugar enters the blood more slowly; it dampens glucagon release, which in diabetes is often inappropriately high after meals; and it promotes the feeling of satiety via the brainstem. Pramlintide was intended to replace this missing effect and thus smooth out blood glucose spikes after meals. That it delays gastric emptying was shown, among others, in a small study with twelve people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes: the time until half of a solid meal had left the stomach increased from 91 to 268 to 329 minutes (Vella 2002, PMID 11975712).

Pramlintide is historically important: it was the first amylin drug and is considered the forerunner of today's long-acting amylin agents such as cagrilintide and amycretin (see there). Its great practical disadvantage is the short duration of action, which requires several injections per day.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 76 human studies, mostly from 1996 to 2010, as well as several meta-analyses. On ClinicalTrials.gov, 64 trials were registered, almost all completed; new trials mainly concern "artificial pancreas" systems and migraine research.

#### Type 1 diabetes

The large approval trials ran for one year. In the study by Whitehouse (2002, PMID 11919132) with 480 people with type 1 diabetes, HbA1c fell by 0.67 percentage points after 13 weeks compared with 0.16 on placebo; the difference remained statistically significant until week 52. On average, participants lost weight instead of gaining it, and the overall rate of severe hypoglycemia was not increased. In the study by Ratner (2004, PMID 15498087) with 651 participants, the effect was smaller: −0.29 and −0.34 percentage points, respectively, compared with −0.04.

These effects are modest. A network meta-analysis of all add-on drugs in type 1 diabetes with 58 trials rated the certainty of the evidence as predominantly moderate to very low and found that pramlintide – like GLP-1 agonists and metformin – makes nausea more frequent (Avgerinos 2021, PMID 33300282).

The study by Edelman (2006, PMID 17003291) provided an important lesson: when the mealtime insulin dose was reduced by 30 to 50% as a precaution at the start of pramlintide treatment, HbA1c had fallen equally in both groups after 29 weeks (−0.5 each). On pramlintide, however, post-meal blood glucose spikes were smaller, weight fell by 1.3 kg (placebo +1.2 kg), and 28% less insulin was needed. Nausea occurred in 63% (placebo 36%).

#### Type 2 diabetes

In insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, pramlintide lowered HbA1c by 0.62 percentage points after one year, with a weight loss of 1.4 kg compared with a 0.7 kg gain on placebo (Hollander 2003, PMID 12610038). As an add-on to basal insulin, it was similarly effective in an open-label trial with 113 participants as a rapid-acting mealtime insulin, but did not lead to weight gain (0.0 kg vs. +4.7 kg) and led to fewer mild to moderate hypoglycemic episodes; on the other hand, 21% complained of nausea (Riddle 2009, PMID 19502544).

A meta-analysis of eight randomized trials found an HbA1c reduction of 0.33 percentage points and a weight loss of 2.57 kg compared with control in type 2 diabetes; nausea occurred 1.8 times more often (Singh-Franco 2011, PMID 21199269).

#### Obesity

Between 2005 and 2010, pramlintide was also tested as a weight-loss drug. In a 16-week trial with 204 people with obesity, placebo-adjusted weight loss was 3.7% (Aronne 2007, PMID 17504894). Combined with a lifestyle program, almost all dose groups maintained their weight loss over twelve months; placebo-adjusted, this amounted to up to 7.2 kg or 6.8% (Smith 2008, PMID 18753666). A combination with metreleptin (a leptin analog) achieved 12.7% weight loss after 20 weeks compared with 8.4% with pramlintide alone (Ravussin 2009, PMID 19521351). This development was not pursued up to approval.

#### Newer applications: artificial pancreas

Since about 2015, pramlintide has experienced a small renaissance in research on automated insulin pump systems. In a randomized crossover trial, time in range increased from 74% to 84% with a system that delivers pramlintide in addition to rapid-acting insulin (Haidar 2020, PMID 31974099). A small pilot study examined whether such a system can make carbohydrate counting unnecessary; the differences from standard treatment were not statistically significant (Odabassian 2025, PMID 41174925). A fixed mixture of pramlintide and an insulin (ADO09) led, in a 16-week trial with 80 people with type 1 diabetes, to 2.1 kg more weight loss than insulin lispro, with unchanged HbA1c (Andersen 2024, PMID 39109464).

### Animal and laboratory studies

The foundations of amylin research come from animal and laboratory studies that are not presented in detail here. In rodents, the combination of amylin and leptin led to a more-than-additive weight loss (described as the starting point in Ravussin 2009, PMID 19521351). In mouse models, amylin, similar to the migraine messenger peptide CGRP, triggered hypersensitivity and light aversion (Ghanizada 2021, PMID 33772845).

### Risks and side effects

**Documented in studies:**

- **Nausea** is the most common side effect, especially in the first weeks; between 9% and 63% depending on the study (Smith 2008, PMID 18753666; Edelman 2006, PMID 17003291).
- **Severe hypoglycemia** is the most important risk. Pramlintide itself hardly lowers blood glucose, but it enhances the effect of the insulin injected at the same time, because the sugar from the meal arrives with a delay. In the study by Edelman, severe hypoglycemia occurred more frequently on pramlintide than on placebo (0.57 vs. 0.30 events per patient-year), particularly in participants who remained on a low dose (Edelman 2006, PMID 17003291). In the USA, the drug therefore carries a prominent warning; a reduction of the mealtime insulin dose is provided for before starting treatment – details are set out in the prescribing information, and implementation belongs in the hands of a physician.
- **Headache and migraine:** In an experimental study with 36 people with migraine, 88% developed headache and 41% a migraine-like attack after a pramlintide infusion (Ghanizada 2021, PMID 33772845). This study used an infusion for research purposes; however, it shows that amylin receptors play a role in migraine. This finding is also relevant for the new, long-acting amylin drugs.
- **Interactions:** Since pramlintide delays gastric emptying, tablets for which a rapid onset of action is important may be absorbed with a delay (prescribing information).

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved. In Europe, pramlintide is conceivable only via individual import for specific patients and plays practically no role.
- **USA:** approved since 2005 (Symlin) as an add-on to mealtime insulin in type 1 and type 2 diabetes; prescription only. It is not approved for obesity without diabetes.
- **Sport:** not named on the WADA list (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed (registered trials, as of October 2026):** New trials with pramlintide are predominantly small basic research studies, for example on the role of amylin in migraine without aura (NCT07340788), in headache after head injury (NCT07340775) and on satiety (NCT07506369). The authors of the pilot study on the pramlintide insulin pump announce a larger trial (Odabassian 2025, PMID 41174925).

**Assessment:** As a medicine, pramlintide has remained a niche product – too many injections, too much nausea, too small an effect on HbA1c. Its most important role is likely to be that of a pioneer: the new generation of long-acting amylin analogs (cagrilintide, amycretin, eloralintide and others) build on its experience. It could retain a small role in research on dual-hormone systems in type 1 diabetes.

### Conclusion

Pramlintide is the first amylin drug and has been approved since 2005 in the USA, but not in the EU, as an add-on to insulin. In large trials it lowered HbA1c by only about 0.3 to 0.7 percentage points, but prevented the weight gain typical of insulin and smoothed out blood glucose spikes after meals. Nausea is common, and in combination with insulin there is a relevant risk of severe hypoglycemia. Today, pramlintide is significant mainly as the forerunner of the new long-acting amylin drugs.
