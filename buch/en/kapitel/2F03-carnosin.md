## Carnosine

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** dipeptide of the amino acids beta-alanine and histidine (β-alanyl-L-histidine). Related substances are anserine (in poultry and fish) and homocarnosine (in the brain); together they are called histidine-containing dipeptides.
- **Origin:** produced by the body, in high concentration in skeletal muscle, and also in the heart and brain. Carnosine is taken in with food mainly via meat and fish; plant foods contain practically none. For food supplements, it is produced synthetically.
- **Mechanism of action:** buffer against acidification in muscle; in the laboratory, also scavenging of reactive breakdown products of sugars and fats (anti-glycation) and antioxidant activity.
- **Related substances:** beta-alanine (the precursor, a widespread sports supplement); zinc carnosine (polaprezinc, a medicine for stomach ulcers in Japan); N-acetylcarnosine (in eye drops).
- **Approval EU:** no approval as a medicine for carnosine; sold as a food supplement. No authorized health claim.
- **Approval USA:** no approval as a drug; available as a dietary supplement.
- **WADA:** carnosine and beta-alanine are not listed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – several small randomized studies, one larger study with just under 300 participants; many results inconsistent.

### How it is supposed to work

During short, very intense exertion – a 400-meter run or a rowing race – the muscle produces more acid than it can remove. The acidity rises and muscle performance declines. Carnosine is one of the buffers in muscle that neutralize this acid. In 18 elite Belgian rowers, the carnosine content of the calf muscles was closely related to speed over 100 to 6,000 meters (Baguet 2010, PMID 20671038).

In addition, carnosine has further properties in the laboratory: it scavenges reactive molecules that arise with high blood sugar or during the breakdown of fats and can damage proteins (so-called glycation and lipid peroxidation). From this come the hypotheses that carnosine could be beneficial in diabetes, vascular and kidney damage, brain aging or skin aging.

#### The Absorption Problem: Carnosinase

Human blood contains a very active enzyme, serum carnosinase (CN1), which rapidly splits carnosine into beta-alanine and histidine. Rodents have practically none of this enzyme in their blood – an important reason why results from animal experiments are only partly transferable. As early as 1991, a study showed that part of the swallowed carnosine is absorbed unchanged from the gut (up to 14% was found in the urine), but was only detectable in the blood if carnosinase was blocked in the sample tube. The amount in the urine varied greatly and was lower the more active the person's carnosinase was (Gardner 1991, PMID 1910085).

In the largest study to date, with 2 g of carnosine per day, carnosine levels in red blood cells roughly doubled after 6 and 12 weeks, and in the urine rose about sevenfold (Baba 2024, PMID 39215872). So carnosine does reach the body – but how much of it gets into muscle, brain or kidney is largely unknown.

#### Beta-Alanine: the Detour via the Precursor

For muscle, a different route has become established. The body builds carnosine itself in the muscle cell from beta-alanine and histidine; beta-alanine is the limiting building block. Taking beta-alanine markedly increases the carnosine content in muscle. In an early study, four weeks of beta-alanine increased muscle carnosine content by an estimated 42 to 64%; isomolar amounts of carnosine had a similar effect (Harris 2006, PMID 16554972). After stopping, it took 12 to 16 weeks for the level to return to baseline (Yamaguchi 2021, PMID 33148972). Practically everyone responds to beta-alanine (Rezende 2020, PMID 32922303).

Beta-alanine is not a peptide but a single amino acid. It is covered only briefly here because it raises muscle carnosine content more reliably than carnosine itself and therefore plays by far the larger role in sport.

### Human studies

The PubMed search evaluated for this book (carnosine in the title, combined with supplementation or oral administration, search date October 2026) yielded 57 human studies; ClinicalTrials.gov lists 53 registry entries. Some of these actually concern beta-alanine or zinc carnosine. The studies are spread across many small fields of application.

#### Sport and Physical Performance

In athletes, the evidence for **beta-alanine** is considerably better than for carnosine. A meta-analysis of 40 double-blind, placebo-controlled studies with 1,461 participants found a small overall effect (effect size 0.18). It was most pronounced for exercise lasting 0.5 to 10 minutes and for tests of exercise capacity to exhaustion, and less so for competitive performance (Saunders 2017, PMID 27797728). An example: in 15 sprinters, beta-alanine increased the carnosine content in calf muscles by 37 to 47% and attenuated fatigue during repeated strength sets, but did not improve 400-meter time (Derave 2007, PMID 17690198). Even a doubling of muscle carnosine did not improve one-hour time in cyclists (Chung 2014, PMID 24457999) – which is logical, since acid buffering plays a smaller role in endurance exercise.

For **carnosine itself**, there are only a few performance studies. In the NEAT study (Nucleophilic Defense Against PM Toxicity, NCT03314987), 299 adults received 2 g of carnosine or placebo for twelve weeks. There were no differences in grip strength, walking speed and most tests; significant improvements were seen only in individual subgroups (certain age and sex groups) in calf raises and the step test (O'Toole 2026, PMID 42308284). Such subgroup findings are prone to chance results and need to be confirmed.

#### Diabetes and Metabolism

This is the most intensively studied medical field:

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| de Courten 2016, PMID 27040154 | 30 adults with overweight without diabetes, 2 g vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Rise in fasting insulin and insulin resistance slowed under carnosine (pilot study) |
| Houjeghani 2018, PMID 29420997 | 54 people with type 2 diabetes, 1 g vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Fasting glucose −13.1 mg/dl, HbA1c −0.6 percentage points vs. placebo |
| Hariharan 2024, PMID 38172006 | 43 adults with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, 2 g vs. placebo | 14 weeks | Lower blood glucose 90 and 120 minutes after glucose load; insulin unchanged |
| Saadati 2023, PMID 38004228 | 49 adults with prediabetes or well-controlled type 2 diabetes, 2 g vs. placebo | 14 weeks | No effect on vascular function, arterial stiffness, blood lipids, blood pressure, liver and kidney values |
| Elbarbary 2018, PMID 28744992 | 90 children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes and kidney damage, 1 g vs. placebo | 12 weeks | Urinary albumin, HbA1c and oxidation markers improved |

Several meta-analyses summarize these studies. An analysis of four studies with 184 participants found a reduction in HbA1c of 0.92 percentage points, but no effect on fasting glucose, insulin resistance or blood lipids (Peng 2020, PMID 31987255). A Bayesian meta-analysis that considered carnosine and beta-alanine together estimated an HbA1c reduction of 0.91 percentage points in humans – based on only four human studies (Matthews 2021, PMID 34333586). A more recent meta-analysis with eight studies and 377 participants with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes found improvements in fasting glucose and HbA1c, but not in insulin or body weight (Li 2025, PMID 40999397).

The effects look considerable on paper but rest on a very narrow basis: few, small, short studies, often from single centers, with partly contradictory results (compare the similarly designed 14-week studies by Hariharan 2024 and Saadati 2023, one of which found favorable effects on blood glucose, while the other found no effects on blood vessels and metabolic values). Whether carnosine prevents long-term complications of diabetes has never been studied.

#### Inflammation and Oxidative Stress

A meta-analysis of nine studies with 350 participants found lower levels of the inflammatory marker CRP and the oxidation marker malondialdehyde under carnosine or histidine-containing dipeptides, but no change in several other markers (Saadati 2024, PMID 38086332). Laboratory values, however, are surrogate parameters – whether they are associated with a health benefit is open.

#### Brain, Memory and Mental Health

- **Older people:** In Japanese studies by one research group, older people received a mixture of anserine and carnosine (3:1). In 39 healthy older people, delayed recall of verbal content was better preserved than under placebo (Hisatsune 2016, PMID 26682691). In 54 people with mild cognitive impairment, a global dementia severity rating was more favorable under the mixture; most other tests did not differ (Masuoka 2019, PMID 31319510).
- **Younger adults:** In a post hoc analysis of the NEAT study, speed and efficiency in cognitive tests improved only in the youngest age group (23–35 years) (O'Toole 2025, PMID 39919936).
- **Summary:** A 2026 meta-analysis included 13 studies. Most cognitive tests showed no difference from placebo; favorable pooled effects were based in part on three studies by the same research group. The authors rate the strength of evidence as low (Hsiao 2026, PMID 42123986). Another meta-analysis found a reduction in depression scores (moderate certainty), but emphasizes small studies with a moderate to high risk of bias (Kabthymer 2025, PMID 38545720).
- **Autism:** An early study with 31 children reported improvements on a behavior scale (Chez 2002, PMID 12585724). A later randomized study with 63 evaluable children, by contrast, found no difference on any endpoint (Abraham 2020, PMID 33170378).

#### Other Applications

- **Chemotherapy-induced nerve damage:** In a randomized pilot study with 65 patients with colorectal cancer, grade 2 nerve damage occurred less frequently under oxaliplatin chemotherapy plus carnosine (1 of 30 versus 19 of 31 without carnosine) (Yehia 2019, PMID 30592963). The study was not blinded and had no placebo; confirmation is pending.
- **Heart failure:** In 50 patients with severe heart failure, walking distance, maximal oxygen uptake and quality of life improved under carnosine compared with standard treatment (Lombardi 2015, PMID 25287762) – likewise without placebo.
- **Gulf War illness:** In a small study with 25 veterans, one cognitive test improved; fatigue and pain remained unchanged (Baraniuk 2013, PMID 23618477).
- **Skin:** A study with a "non-hydrolyzable" carnosine preparation reported skin improvements; it comes from the inventor of the patented formulation and mixes laboratory experiments with a clinical trial (Babizhayev 2012, PMID 21756141).

#### Related Substances: Zinc Carnosine and N-Acetylcarnosine

**Zinc carnosine (polaprezinc)** is a compound of zinc and carnosine used in Japan as a medicine for stomach ulcers. In a multicenter study with 224 patients, it was similarly effective after eight weeks as the comparator drug rebamipide (81.5% versus 74.3% healing rate, difference not significant) (Shen 2022, PMID 35999163). This effect is probably closely tied to the zinc and cannot be transferred to pure carnosine.

**N-acetylcarnosine eye drops** are promoted on the internet against cataracts. A Cochrane review found only two possible studies with 114 participants whose methodology could not be traced and concluded that there is no convincing evidence that the drops reverse cataracts or prevent their progression (Dubois 2017, PMID 28245346). The only effective treatment remains surgery.

### Animal and laboratory studies

In cell cultures and in rodents, carnosine acts as an antioxidant, inhibits protein glycation and binds reactive aldehydes. In mice and rats, it improved blood glucose, diabetic kidney damage, wound healing and vascular function in many experiments. The Matthews 2021 meta-analysis included 16 rodent studies in addition to four human studies and rated the certainty of the animal data as very low (Matthews 2021, PMID 34333586).

A fundamental transfer problem remains: rodents have practically no carnosinase in their blood. In mice genetically engineered to carry the human enzyme, diabetes and kidney damage were more severe (Qiu 2020, PMID 32803273). This suggests that in humans the enzyme could considerably limit the effect of supplemented carnosine.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** In the studies so far – with daily amounts of up to 2 g for up to 14 weeks – no relevant side effects were reported. In the NEAT study with just under 300 participants, there were no changes in kidney, liver or heart function or blood electrolytes (Baba 2024, PMID 39215872).

**Beta-alanine:** The only regularly reported side effect is a harmless but unpleasant tingling of the skin (paresthesia), usually on the face and hands, shortly after intake. A risk assessment based on 101 human studies estimated the odds of this to be about nine times higher under beta-alanine than under placebo (odds ratio 8.9); discontinuation rates did not differ, and a slight rise in one liver value remained within the normal range (Dolan 2019, PMID 30980076).

**Theoretical or unclear:**

- Long-term data covering more than one year are lacking.
- There are no safety data for pregnancy, breastfeeding and children (apart from the small studies mentioned).
- With diabetes medications, an enhancement of blood glucose lowering is conceivable but not established; blood glucose monitoring and consultation with a doctor are advisable.
- As with all food supplements, quality depends on the manufacturer.

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** Carnosine is sold as a food supplement. There is no authorized health claim for carnosine under the Health Claims Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006); advertising claims such as "anti-aging" or "protects cells" are therefore not permitted. Whether individual preparations are to be classified as novel foods may vary from product to product – please check for updates. Zinc carnosine is not approved as a medicine in the EU; N-acetylcarnosine eye drops have no approval as a medicine for cataracts.
- **USA:** available as a dietary supplement, without approval as a drug.
- **Japan:** Polaprezinc (zinc carnosine) is an approved stomach medicine.
- **Sport:** Neither carnosine nor beta-alanine is on the WADA Prohibited List (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established (registered studies, as of October 2026):**

- **Peripheral artery disease:** A phase 1/2 trial with a planned 144 participants is investigating carnosine in circulatory disorders of the legs (NCT06480760); earlier smaller studies on the same topic have been completed or are listed with unknown status.
- **Gerotherapy:** A small phase 3 trial with 30 participants is testing combinations of substances to improve healthy lifespan, including carnosine (NCT07475546).
- The NEAT study (NCT03314987) has been completed; further analyses on blood vessels and air pollution are possible.

**Assessment:** Carnosine is biologically interesting, but the human studies are small and inconsistent. For sport, the precursor beta-alanine is the better-supported route. For diabetes and brain aging, larger, longer studies with clinical endpoints would be needed. One research approach is carnosinase-resistant derivatives that are not immediately broken down in the blood – but they are still at the laboratory stage.

### Conclusion

Carnosine is a dipeptide produced by the body that buffers acid in muscle and in the laboratory protects cells from breakdown products of sugars and fats. In humans, it is rapidly broken down in the blood by the enzyme carnosinase, which is why animal results are only partly transferable. Small studies and meta-analyses suggest a slight improvement in long-term blood glucose in diabetes and effects on inflammatory markers; for performance, memory and skin aging, the data are weak or contradictory. For athletic performance, the precursor beta-alanine has been better studied and is associated with small advantages during short, intense exertion. Carnosine is considered well tolerated in the amounts studied.
