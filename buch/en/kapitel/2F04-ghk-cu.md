## GHK-Cu (Copper Peptide)

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** tripeptide of glycine, histidine and lysine (Gly-His-Lys, GHK for short) that tightly binds a copper ion. In cosmetic ingredient lists it is usually called *Copper Tripeptide-1*. A related form without copper but with a fatty acid residue is palmitoyl tripeptide-1 (Pal-GHK), a component of "Matrixyl 3000."
- **Origin:** produced by the body. GHK was discovered in human blood plasma in the 1970s (Pickart 1973, PMID 4349963) and also occurs in saliva and urine. The GHK sequence is part of collagen and could be released during its breakdown. For products, it is produced synthetically.
- **Mechanism of action:** supposed to stimulate wound healing and connective tissue remodeling, among other things by transporting copper into cells and stimulating collagen production.
- **Use:** permitted as an ingredient in cosmetics (creams, serums, hair care). In addition, offered on the gray market as a "research peptide" for injection.
- **Approval EU:** no approval as a medicine. Usable as a cosmetic ingredient.
- **Approval USA:** no approval as a drug; sold as a cosmetic ingredient. There are restrictions on the preparation of injectable formulations in pharmacies (see Legal Status).
- **WADA:** not listed by name. When injected as a non-approved substance, category S0 ("non-approved substances") may apply (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **C** – one older randomized wound study and a few very small skin studies; for the uses promoted today (anti-aging cosmetics, injections), no robust controlled human data.

### How It Is Supposed to Work

GHK binds copper so tightly that it can transport this vital trace element into cells. Copper is needed by several enzymes that are important for connective tissue, such as lysyl oxidase, which cross-links collagen and elastin fibers. An early paper suggested as early as 1980 that GHK exerts its effect on cells by facilitating copper uptake (Pickart 1980, PMID 7453802).

In cell cultures, GHK-Cu stimulated collagen production by fibroblasts even at extremely low concentrations; the maximum was at about one nanomole per liter (Maquart 1988, PMID 3169264). The discoverer of GHK, Loren Pickart, has described a large number of further effects in several reviews – on wound healing, hair growth, inflammation, nerve growth and even on the activity of thousands of genes (Pickart 2015, PMID 26236730; Pickart 2018, PMID 29986520). These claims are based mainly on cell and animal experiments and on gene expression data from databases, not on human studies. In addition, many of these reviews come from the circle of people who have also developed GHK-Cu commercially – a fact that readers should be aware of when evaluating them.

#### Does the Peptide Get Through the Skin?

For cosmetic use, it is decisive whether GHK-Cu overcomes the skin barrier. The stratum corneum of the skin is poorly permeable to water-soluble, charged molecules such as GHK. A 2025 review concludes that GHK could undoubtedly be considered an anti-wrinkle agent in cell experiments, but that there are strikingly few clinical studies; copper binding and fatty acid attachments improve permeability, and microneedles and other aids are being researched (Mortazavi 2025, PMID 39963574). A study in three volunteers found the related Pal-GHK in all layers of the stratum corneum after application, but only between the cells and in decreasing amounts with depth (Trzaska 2026, PMID 42790608). Whether sufficient amounts reach the deeper layers of the skin where collagen is formed has not been clarified.

### Human Studies

The PubMed search evaluated for this book (GHK-Cu, copper tripeptide or copper peptide GHK in title or abstract, search date October 2026) yielded only **one** human study. On ClinicalTrials.gov, the search yielded 25 entries, but almost all of them concern other copper compounds (such as radioactive copper-64 for imaging). Only one credible entry actually studies GHK or GHK-Cu; another entry on a GHK-Cu wound gel comes from a sponsor whose registry entries, according to our review, are apparently fictitious sample or test entries, and is therefore not taken into account here. An extended search additionally yielded the older wound study mentioned below as well as individual case reports.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mulder 1994, PMID 17147644 | People with diabetic foot ulcers, multicenter, randomized, blinded assessment, GHK-Cu gel vs. vehicle gel | not stated in abstract | Median wound area reduction 98.5% vs. 60.8%; infections 7% vs. 34% |
| Miller 2006, PMID 16847171 | Skin care after CO₂ laser treatment, randomized, 13 evaluable | 12 weeks | No difference in redness, wrinkles or skin quality by measurement and blinded assessment; higher satisfaction on questionnaire |
| Byun 2016, PMID 27064823 | 1 man, mixture of GHK-Cu, hyaluronic acid, plant extract, tranexamic acid and beta-glucan with spray device | 12 weeks | Fewer eye wrinkles; more collagen in skin samples (single case, combination product) |

**The 1994 wound study** is the only controlled study that improved a clinically relevant endpoint. However, the product (developed under the name Iamin) did not obtain approval as a medicine. The abstract states neither the number of participants nor the study duration; later confirmatory studies cannot be found in PubMed.

**The 2006 laser study** is the only randomized study with a cosmetic question recorded in PubMed. With only 13 evaluable patients, it was too small to reliably detect differences – and objectively it found none.

**Cosmetic studies:** Textbooks and advertising materials cite studies according to which GHK-Cu creams improved wrinkles, skin density and elasticity. These have mostly not appeared in journals listed in PubMed or are available only as conference contributions and manufacturer reports, and could not be checked in the original for this book. A 2026 meta-analysis on oral and topically applied peptides against skin aging found that the benefit for wrinkles was mainly driven by *orally* taken peptides; the abstract does not report results specifically for GHK-Cu (Nukaly 2026, PMID 41924746).

**Injections:** There is not a single published controlled study of injected GHK-Cu in humans. A review for orthopedics and sports medicine states that there are no clinical data for use in musculoskeletal disorders and that the dose, frequency and duration of treatment are unknown (Mayfield 2026, PMID 41476424). Another review of non-approved peptides in sports medicine concludes that robust human safety data are lacking and that there is a potential for harm (Mendias 2026, PMID 41966639).

### Animal and Laboratory Studies

The preclinical literature is extensive:

- **Cell culture:** increased collagen production in fibroblasts (Maquart 1988, PMID 3169264); influence on enzymes that build up and break down connective tissue.
- **Wound healing in animals:** In rats with poorly perfused wounds, wound area decreased after 13 days by 64.5% under GHK-Cu gel, by 45.6% under the vehicle gel and by 28.2% without treatment (Canapp 2003, PMID 14648529). In mice, liposomes containing GHK-Cu promoted the formation of new blood vessels in scalded skin and shortened healing time (Wang 2017, PMID 28370978).
- **Other organs (in animals):** In mice, GHK-Cu injected into the abdominal cavity attenuated experimentally induced pulmonary fibrosis (Ma 2020, PMID 31809714); further mouse studies describe effects on lung damage, intestinal inflammation and muscle weakness after cigarette smoke. In rats, it improved healing after cruciate ligament reconstruction only temporarily (Fu 2015, PMID 25731775).
- **Brain (in mice):** A not yet peer-reviewed preprint from 2026 described better learning performance in old mice after eight weeks of intranasal administration, and after short intraperitoneal administration only temporarily and only in males (Mazzola 2026, PMID 42245779).

These findings show biological activity but cannot readily be transferred to humans – least of all to cosmetic creams with unclear skin penetration.

### Risks and Side Effects

**Cosmetic use (established or likely):** GHK-Cu has been used in cosmetics for decades; no relevant side effects were reported in the few studies. As with any cosmetic ingredient, skin irritation and contact allergies are possible. Copper-containing serums may or may not be chemically compatible with other active ingredients (such as vitamin C or acids) – this is a question of formulation.

**Injection (theoretical, but to be taken seriously):**

- **No safety data:** There are no systematic data on tolerability, dosage or long-term consequences in humans.
- **Inconsistent cellular effects:** Even the early laboratory work described that GHK can promote growth but can also be toxic, depending on the cell system (Pickart 1980, PMID 7453802).
- **Copper burden:** Copper is toxic in higher amounts. How much copper remains in the body after repeated injections has not been studied. People with Wilson's disease, a congenital copper storage disorder, would be particularly at risk.
- **Growth signals:** In animal experiments, GHK-Cu promotes the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis). Whether this poses a risk in existing tumors is unknown (theoretical risk).
- **Gray-market quality:** "Research peptides" are manufactured without pharmaceutical quality control. Impurities, wrong amounts or bacterial contamination are possible; injections of non-sterile products can lead to abscesses and infections. A review describes these risks for injectable "biohacking" peptides in general (Moiz 2026, PMID 42757290). Part IV covers the topic in detail.

### Legal Status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** GHK-Cu (Copper Tripeptide-1) may be used as an ingredient in cosmetics; the EU Cosmetics Regulation (Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009) applies. Cosmetics may only be intended for application to the skin and its appendages and may not claim medicinal effects; advertising claims must be substantiated in accordance with Regulation (EU) No 655/2013. GHK-Cu intended for injection is not a cosmetic but an unapproved medicine. Its sale for human use is not permitted; labels such as "for research purposes only" do not change this.
- **USA:** Permitted in cosmetics. To our knowledge, in 2023 the FDA placed GHK-Cu for injection on a list of substances for which it sees significant safety concerns for pharmacy preparation (compounding); the classification of several peptides has since been rediscussed (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Sport:** GHK-Cu is not named on the WADA list. Cosmetic creams are considered unproblematic. Injected GHK-Cu as a non-approved substance could fall under category S0; athletes should check with their National Anti-Doping Agency if in doubt (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing Studies and Outlook

**Established (registered studies, as of October 2026):**

- **Patch:** A study with a planned 100 healthy people is to investigate whether a patch (X39) changes GHK and GHK-Cu levels in the blood; start planned for 2027 (NCT07706361). The sponsor is a company that sells the patch.

**Assessment:** A serious, registered study on a medically relevant endpoint – such as wound healing, where the only positive controlled human data come from – is not currently in sight. For cosmetic use, independent, sufficiently large, placebo-controlled studies with a cream without other active ingredients would be needed. For injections, no serious study is in sight; interest is being fueled mainly on social media and on the gray market.

### Conclusion

GHK-Cu is a copper peptide produced by the body that stimulates collagen production in the laboratory and accelerates wound healing in animal experiments. In humans, there is only one older randomized study on diabetic foot ulcers with a positive result and one tiny cosmetic study without a measurable effect. Whether GHK-Cu from creams reaches the skin in sufficient amounts is unclear; advertising claims are based mainly on laboratory data and manufacturer studies that have not been independently verified. As a cosmetic ingredient, it is legal and considered well tolerated. Injections of gray-market GHK-Cu have been neither studied nor approved and carry avoidable risks.
