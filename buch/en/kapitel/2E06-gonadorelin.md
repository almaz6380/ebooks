## Gonadorelin

### Profile

- **Type/structure:** peptide of ten amino acids, produced synthetically and identical in sequence to the human hormone GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone, also called LHRH or gonadoliberin).
- **Origin:** GnRH was isolated and decoded from pig and sheep brains in the early 1970s; the researchers involved, Andrew Schally and Roger Guillemin, received the Nobel Prize for this in 1977. Gonadorelin is the international nonproprietary name of the synthetic hormone.
- **Mechanism of action:** agonist at the GnRH receptor of the pituitary gland. Given in pulses, it stimulates the release of LH and FSH; given continuously, it suppresses it.
- **Dosage forms:** injection into a vein for function tests; portable pump that delivers a small amount under the skin or into a vein every 60 to 120 minutes (pulsatile therapy); in some countries, a nasal spray for infants with undescended testes.
- **Approval EU:** nationally approved in individual member states, for example as a diagnostic agent and for pump therapy; products and availability differ from country to country.
- **Approval USA:** Former products (Factrel for diagnostics, Lutrepulse for the pump) are no longer on the market. To the author's knowledge, there is currently no marketed, approved human medicine (please check for updates).
- **WADA:** prohibited for men (group S2, where gonadorelin is named explicitly), in and out of competition (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **B** – approved medicine with decades of use; however, the efficacy data come mainly from large case series, cohorts and a few small randomized studies.

### How It Is Supposed to Work

The reproductive hormone axis begins in the hypothalamus. There, specialized nerve cells release GnRH – not evenly, but in pulses roughly every one to two hours. Each pulse causes the pituitary gland (hypophysis) to release the hormones LH and FSH. These act on the ovaries and testes: they control egg maturation and ovulation, sperm production and the production of estrogen and testosterone. The messenger kisspeptin (chapter 2E04) sits one level higher still and switches on the GnRH nerve cells.

The pulse rhythm is decisive. This becomes clear from an apparent contradiction:

- **Pulsatile administration** – a small amount every 60 to 120 minutes – mimics the natural rhythm and **activates** the axis. Pump therapy for people whose hypothalamus produces too little GnRH is based on this.
- **Continuous stimulation**, after a brief rise, causes the receptors of the pituitary gland to switch off. The hormone axis is **suppressed**, down to testosterone levels like those after castration.

The GnRH analogs such as leuprorelin, triptorelin or goserelin are based on the second principle. They are chemically modified, act much longer than gonadorelin and are used as depot injections for prostate cancer, endometriosis, fibroids or precocious puberty. They are not the subject of this chapter, but they considerably distort the data (see below).

Gonadorelin itself is unsuitable for continuous blockade: it is broken down in the blood within a few minutes.

### Human Studies

**Size of the evidence base (search October 2026) – and why the numbers are misleading:** The automated search for this book found **9** human studies in PubMed (`humanstudien_alle`) and, on ClinicalTrials.gov, the maximum number of **1,000** registry entries at which the query stops. Both figures are useless for assessing gonadorelin, and in opposite directions:

- **The registry figure is greatly overestimated.** ClinicalTrials.gov maps the search term "gonadorelin" to the umbrella term "Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone." As a result, all studies with GnRH analogs and antagonists appear. In the titles of the 1,000 hits, the word "prostate" appears 340 times, "androgen deprivation" 57 times, and leuprorelin, goserelin and triptorelin together well over a hundred times. A review of the titles found only around ten entries that actually study native GnRH or gonadorelin – for example as a pump, patch or function test.
- **The PubMed figure is underestimated.** Most studies on the body-identical hormone use the terms "GnRH" or "LHRH" rather than the drug name. Conversely, several of the nine hits do not concern gonadorelin as a treatment at all: one paper studied a GnRH analog in prostate cancer (Steg 1985, PMID 2934695), others used gonadorelin only as a test substance, for example to examine the effect of a cholesterol-lowering drug or of UV light on the hormone axis (Segarra 1996, PMID 8773342; Falkenbach 1997, PMID 9189734).

This chapter therefore relies on a supplementary, targeted search for studies on pulsatile GnRH therapy and the GnRH test.

#### Diagnostics: the GnRH Test

In the GnRH test (also LHRH test), gonadorelin is injected into a vein; then, over one to two hours, the rise in LH and FSH is measured. The test helps above all in children with early puberty: a marked rise in LH suggests that the hormone axis in the brain is already active (central precocious puberty).

In an analysis of 584 tests (314 of them for diagnosis), a single blood sample 40 minutes after the injection was sufficient for diagnosis: at an LH threshold of 5 IU/L, sensitivity was 98% and specificity 100% (Kandemir 2011, PMID 21448328). A review points to limitations, for example in very early stages of puberty and in overweight children, and to inconsistent cutoff values between studies (Ab Rahim 2020, PMID 32871650).

Because native GnRH is expensive and unavailable in many places, a short-acting analog is increasingly used as a substitute. In a study with 341 girls, a test with triptorelin was similarly accurate to the gonadorelin test (Chioma 2025, PMID 41384863).

#### Infertility: the GnRH Pump in Women

In hypothalamic amenorrhea, menstruation stops because the brain produces too little GnRH – often after severe weight loss, intense exercise or stress, less often for congenital reasons. A small, portable pump can replace the missing rhythm.

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Santoro 1990, PMID 2122733 | 109 women with hypothalamic amenorrhea, multicenter study with intravenous pump | several cycles | Ovulation in 91% and 96%; multiples 12% |
| Filicori 1994, PMID 7962297 | 292 women, 600 cycles, various causes | 1984–1993 | Ovulation in 75% of cycles; pregnancy 18% per cycle; multiples 3.8%; miscarriages 30% |
| Christin-Maitre 2007, PMID 17293247 | 248 women, 829 cycles, 24 French centers, retrospective | 3 years | Pregnancy 25% per cycle; multiples 8.8%; no severe hyperstimulation |
| Dubourdieu 2013, PMID 23298894 | 30 women with amenorrhea and PCOS, RCT: pump vs. gonadotropins | one cycle | Clinical pregnancy 46% vs. 0% |
| Quaas 2022, PMID 36378460 | 66 women, 212 cycles, Swiss center, retrospective | 1996–2020 | Live birth per treatment 65.9%; one follicle in 75% of cycles; one twin pregnancy |

The figures show a consistent picture: in women whose problem actually lies in the hypothalamus, the pump leads to ovulation in the vast majority of cycles, often with only one maturing egg. Because the pituitary gland retains its own feedback, hyperstimulation and multiple pregnancies are less common than with directly injected gonadotropins (Quaas 2022, PMID 36378460). In women with polycystic ovary syndrome or overweight, the pump works less well, and miscarriages were frequent in the largest single series (Filicori 1994, PMID 7962297).

The drawbacks are practical: the pump must be worn for weeks. With intravenous administration, complications at the access site occurred in 7% on average (Santoro 1990, PMID 2122733). Today, the pump usually delivers under the skin; local allergic reactions occurred in 1.7% of cycles in the French study (Christin-Maitre 2007, PMID 17293247).

The data are old and mostly not randomized. Large comparative studies with modern gonadotropin protocols are lacking. Nevertheless, experts regard the pump as a particularly physiological and safe method for hypothalamic amenorrhea with a desire to conceive (Quaas 2022, PMID 36378460) – provided it is available.

#### Men with Hormone Deficiency

Men with congenital GnRH deficiency – for example in Kallmann syndrome, in which the sense of smell is also absent – can also be treated with the pump to stimulate testicular growth and sperm production.

In an analysis of 26 men at a German university center, sperm appeared in the ejaculate in 30 of 33 treatment cycles; in 15 of 18 cases with a desire to conceive, the partner became pregnant. The pump and injected gonadotropins were equally effective (Kliesch 1994, PMID 7921222). In Chinese boys of pubertal age, the testes grew more under the pump than under hCG injections (Gong 2015, PMID 25978110). A small randomized study found indications that pretreatment with FSH before the pump might improve sperm production: all seven pretreated men later had sperm, compared with four of six without pretreatment (Dwyer 2013, PMID 24037890).

#### Infants with Undescended Testes

In some European countries, a gonadorelin nasal spray is given before or after surgery for undescended testes to promote maturation of the germ cells. A randomized study with 62 infants reported more favorable ultrasound findings after combined treatment (Spinelli 2014, PMID 25106891). Another randomized study with 36 boys, by contrast, found no difference in penile size, testicular volume or hormone levels at the time of surgery; the treatment did, however, prove safe (Fiala 2022, PMID 35691791). The benefit is disputed; early surgery is the priority.

### Animal and Laboratory Studies

Gonadorelin is one of the best-understood hormones of all; its fundamentals come from animal experiments in the 1970s. Today, animal research plays hardly any role in the assessment in humans. In veterinary medicine, gonadorelin is widely used, above all in cattle to control ovulation. These products are approved for animals and not for humans.

### Risks and Side Effects

#### Established

Gonadorelin is considered well tolerated. In the studies on pump therapy, problems with the access site (inflammation, vein irritation with intravenous administration) and local skin reactions were the main issues (Santoro 1990, PMID 2122733; Christin-Maitre 2007, PMID 17293247). Multiple pregnancies occur, although less frequently than with gonadotropins; severe hyperstimulation is the exception. During the test, temporary headache, nausea or flushing may occur. Allergic reactions have rarely been described. Details are given in the prescribing information; use belongs in medical hands.

#### With Incorrect Use

If gonadorelin is not given in the right rhythm but too frequently or continuously, it can suppress rather than stimulate the body's own hormone axis. This is not a theoretical risk but the mechanism of action of the GnRH analogs.

### Context: Gonadorelin in Bodybuilding

In internet forums and by some providers of "hormone optimization," gonadorelin is promoted as part of so-called post-cycle therapy (PCT). This refers to the phase after a course of anabolic steroids: the artificially supplied hormones have suppressed the body's own testosterone production, and gonadorelin is supposed to "restart" the hormone axis. In the USA, it is reportedly also offered as a substitute for hCG.

What is known about this:

- **There are no studies** on gonadorelin in this situation. The evidence on PCT as a whole is thin; almost only antiestrogens, hCG and aromatase inhibitors have been studied, and even for these the data are mostly observational. From a scientific point of view, PCT is mostly not recommended (Smit 2026, PMID 42644333).
- **Recovery usually happens on its own.** In the Dutch HAARLEM study with 100 strength athletes, testosterone had normalized in most of them three months after the end of the steroid course; after one year, it was still below the normal range in 11%, and sperm count was still reduced in 34% (Smit 2021, PMID 33550376).
- **The mechanism of action argues against naive use.** Single injections produce only brief LH peaks; frequent doses may rather blunt the pituitary gland. Without a pump and laboratory monitoring, no benefit can be expected, and a delay in recovery cannot be ruled out.
- **Anyone who has symptoms after steroid use**, such as lack of drive, loss of libido or unfulfilled desire to have children, should have this evaluated by a doctor, ideally in an andrology or endocrinology clinic.

In sport, gonadorelin is prohibited for men. Anti-doping laboratories have developed their own detection methods; a review describes gonadorelin as an example of a peptide that has been misused in competitive sport in recent years (Torrini 2023, PMID 36858006).

### Legal Status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU:** no central EU approval; national approvals in several member states, among others as a diagnostic agent, for pulsatile pump therapy and as a nasal spray for undescended testes. The products are prescription-only.
- **Germany:** Products for the GnRH test and for pump therapy were or are on the market; current availability fluctuates. Treatment takes place in specialized centers.
- **Austria:** prescription-only; please check the current availability of individual products in the Austrian list of medicinal products (Arzneispezialitätenliste).
- **USA:** The formerly approved human products are off the market. Gonadorelin is reportedly offered by compounding pharmacies; the regulatory assessment of such preparations is in flux. Veterinary medicines containing gonadorelin are not intended for humans.
- **WADA:** S2 – prohibited for men in and out of competition.

### Ongoing Studies and Outlook

**Established (registered studies):** Among the few registry entries that actually concern native GnRH are:

- completed studies by the manufacturer Ferring on a skin patch that delivers GnRH in pulses through the skin via a weak electric current (NCT00296465 with 132 and NCT00796289 with 350 participants). To the author's knowledge, results of these studies have not been published in PubMed, and the patch did not come to market;
- a study withdrawn in 2025 on GnRH administration with a modern tubeless insulin pump (NCT07152730);
- Chinese studies on the pump in congenital hormone deficiency (NCT02310074) and in PCOS (NCT03989024), both with unknown status.

**Author's assessment:** Gonadorelin is an old, well-understood drug whose importance in diagnostics is declining because short-acting analogs are cheaper and more readily available. For pump therapy in hypothalamic amenorrhea and congenital hormone deficiency, it remains a physiological option whose future depends less on new research than on whether manufacturers continue to supply these small markets. Modern tubeless pumps could make treatment more convenient. There is no evidence of benefit in the bodybuilding context.

### Conclusion

Gonadorelin is the synthetic counterpart of the body's own GnRH and has been used for decades for function tests and pump therapy. Given in the right pulse rhythm, in large case series it led to ovulation in most cycles in women with hypothalamic amenorrhea, with fewer multiples than under gonadotropins, and to sperm production in men with congenital hormone deficiency. The evidence is old and mostly not randomized; registry figures are massively distorted by GnRH analogs. Its use in the bodybuilding scene after steroid courses has not been studied, contradicts the mechanism of action and is prohibited in sport.
