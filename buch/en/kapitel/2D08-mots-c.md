## MOTS-c

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** peptide of 16 amino acids. The name stands for *mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA-c* – it is encoded not in the cell nucleus but in the genome of the mitochondria, the "power plants" of the cell.
- **Origin:** described in 2015 by a research group led by Pinchas Cohen at the University of Southern California. The body produces it itself, mainly in skeletal muscle.
- **Mechanism of action:** regarded as a "mitokine" – a signaling molecule of the mitochondria. In mice, it activated the energy sensor AMPK and improved insulin sensitivity and physical performance; it is therefore discussed as an "exercise mimetic."
- **EU approval:** no.
- **US approval:** no. Removed from FDA "Category 2" in April 2026; in July 2026, the advisory committee PCAC voted 7 to 5 for inclusion in the list of permitted compounding substances (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **WADA:** **prohibited** – explicitly named in category S4 (hormone and metabolic modulators) as an example of an AMPK activator, in and out of competition (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level:** **D** for administration as a drug – there are no published results of controlled studies in humans; in humans, only observational studies on endogenous levels exist.

### How it is supposed to work

Mitochondria have their own small genome. For a long time, it was regarded as the "blueprint" for only 13 proteins of energy production. Since the early 2000s, additional short reading frames have been discovered in it that encode small signaling peptides – first humanin (see the next chapter), then MOTS-c.

The first description showed in mice that MOTS-c acts mainly on skeletal muscle: it inhibits a metabolic pathway (the folate cycle and de novo purine synthesis), causing the messenger AICAR to accumulate and the enzyme AMPK to be activated – the same energy sensor targeted by exercise and the diabetes drug metformin. Mice injected with MOTS-c developed less obesity and less insulin resistance on a high-fat diet (Lee 2015, PMID 25738459). Under metabolic stress, MOTS-c migrated into the cell nucleus in cell cultures and influenced genes there (Kim 2018, PMID 29983246).

In a second major paper, MOTS-c increased the physical performance of young, middle-aged and old mice; treatment started only in old age improved performance and "healthy lifespan." In humans, MOTS-c levels in muscle and blood rose after physical exertion (Reynolds 2021, PMID 33473109).

This gave rise to the image of MOTS-c as "exercise in a syringe." But the order matters: the fact that exercise increases MOTS-c does not mean that MOTS-c injections reproduce the effects of exercise in humans.

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence:** The automated PubMed search (October 2026) yielded 5 publications classified as human studies; none of them administered MOTS-c. In total, PubMed lists around 260 papers on "MOTS-c," mostly animal and cell experiments and observational studies on endogenous blood levels. On ClinicalTrials.gov, the search yielded 5 entries. Four of them only measure levels or concern other questions. The fifth, a purported phase 2 trial of MOTS-c in prediabetes, comes from a sponsor under whose name apparently fictitious sample entries appear in the registry. We therefore do not consider it a genuine study and do not count it; a registered study with MOTS-c itself as the active substance is thus not documented. In addition, there is a completed study with the MOTS-c derivative CB4211 (NCT03998514).

For understanding this chapter, one distinction is central:

1. **Observational studies on endogenous levels:** These measure how much MOTS-c people have in their blood and relate it to diseases or behavior.
2. **Studies in which MOTS-c (or a derivative) is given as a drug:** Only these can show whether supplementation has an effect.

Results of the first kind are often presented on the internet as if they proved the effect of the second kind. That is a short circuit.

#### 1. Endogenous levels: observations

| Study | Participants and design | Duration | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Du 2018, PMID 29691953 | 40 overweight children and adolescents, 57 controls, case-control study | cross-sectional | lower levels in overweight boys, not in girls |
| Kirik 2023, PMID 37750635 | 150 people with fatty liver, 84 healthy people | cross-sectional | lower levels in fatty liver and metabolic syndrome |
| Ikonomidis 2020, PMID 32052315 | 121 people with type 2 diabetes after vascular intervention | 2 years | low MOTS-c (below 167 ng/mL) predicted cardiovascular events (relative risk 3.8) |
| von Walden 2021, PMID 34351816 | 30 people, endurance or strength training or control, randomized | single bout | humanin rose after endurance exercise, MOTS-c showed only a trend |
| Dieli-Conwright 2021, PMID 34413391 | 49 women after breast cancer, training or usual care, randomized | 16 weeks | rise in MOTS-c in non-Hispanic white women, not in Hispanic women |
| Elhusseiny 2025, PMID 40674654 | 19 active men with immobilized lower leg, heat treatment or sham | 2 weeks | heat increased MOTS-c in the blood |

These studies show that low MOTS-c levels frequently go hand in hand with overweight, insulin resistance, fatty liver or vascular problems (Du 2018, PMID 29691953; Kirik 2023, PMID 37750635; Qin 2018, PMID 29242099) and that exercise or heat can raise the levels. What they do **not** show: that low levels cause the diseases, or that supplemental MOTS-c prevents them. It could just as well be the other way around – the disease lowers the level.

Another difficulty is the measurement itself. The studies use different assays (ELISA) whose results differ widely: in children, mean values were around 470 to 560 ng/mL (Du 2018, PMID 29691953), whereas in the diabetes study a value below 167 ng/mL was considered low (Ikonomidis 2020, PMID 32052315). In a study of breast cancer patients, even metformin – which acts on the same energy sensor – did not change the levels measured with a commercial assay (Cuyàs 2022, PMID 36490309).

**Genetic evidence:** A variant in the mitochondrial genome found only in Asia changes one building block of MOTS-c (K14Q). In three cohorts with a combined 27,527 people, men with this variant more often had type 2 diabetes, especially if they were physically inactive. In mice, the altered MOTS-c form was less effective (Zempo 2021, PMID 33468709). This is an interesting indication that MOTS-c could play a role in human metabolism – but not proof of the benefit of supplementation.

#### 2. Administration as a drug: CB4211

**CB4211** is a derivative of MOTS-c developed by the company CohBar. The phase 1a/1b trial (NCT03998514) was randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled and included 88 participants: healthy people received single injections or daily subcutaneous injections for seven days, and people with obesity and fatty liver for 28 days. Primary endpoints were safety and tolerability; secondary objectives included liver fat and body weight. The trial was completed in April 2021. No results have been posted in the registry, and our search did not find a publication in a scientific journal. In 2021, the company announced in a press release favorable changes in individual liver and blood glucose values with good tolerability; such announcements are not independently peer-reviewed. Further development in larger trials is not known.

**MOTS-c itself**, according to our research, has not yet been given as a drug in any published or reliably registered human study. A registry entry describing such a phase 2 trial proved dubious (see above).

Until then, the following applies: there are **no published data** on whether MOTS-c injections influence metabolism, performance or aging in humans.

### Animal and laboratory studies

Preclinical research is extensive and growing rapidly:

- **Metabolism:** In mice, MOTS-c prevented diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance (Lee 2015, PMID 25738459).
- **Muscle:** MOTS-c reduced the muscle-wasting messenger myostatin in obese mice; in humans, MOTS-c and myostatin levels in the blood were inversely related (Kumagai 2021, PMID 33554779).
- **Performance and age:** MOTS-c increased the physical performance of mice of various ages (Reynolds 2021, PMID 33473109).
- **Pancreas:** In mice, MOTS-c delayed the aging of insulin-producing cells and the onset of diabetes (Kong 2025, PMID 40855115).
- Numerous other papers, mainly from China, describe protective effects in models of lung, heart, liver, bone and kidney damage.

The abundance of positive animal studies is striking. It shows that MOTS-c is biologically active; it says little about efficacy and safety in humans.

### Risks and side effects

**Established:** There are no published safety data on the administration of MOTS-c to humans.

**Theoretical:**

- **Blood glucose:** A substance that increases insulin sensitivity could promote hypoglycemia in combination with diabetes medications.
- **Cancer:** In laboratory studies, MOTS-c tended to slow cancer cells: it inhibited the growth of ovarian cancer cells (Yin 2024, PMID 39321430) and made liver cancer cells more susceptible to programmed cell death under oxygen deprivation (Shen 2025, PMID 39581216). Whether this applies in humans, and what effects years of supplementation with a metabolically active signaling molecule would have, has not been studied.
- **Immune reactions:** Repeated injections of a peptide can lead to the formation of antibodies; this has not been studied for MOTS-c.

**Gray market and sport:** MOTS-c is one of the most popular "longevity" and fitness peptides on the internet and is sold as powder for injection. Purity and content are unverified. For athletes, its use is explicitly prohibited (WADA S4).

### Legal status

- **EU / Austria / Germany:** not approved; as a substance with a pharmacological effect, MOTS-c is considered a medicine, and selling it for use in humans is not permitted (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **USA:** not approved. Following the positive PCAC vote in July 2026, MOTS-c could in future be used in pharmacy compounding if the FDA follows it; the decision was pending at the time of going to press (as of October 2026, please check for updates).
- **Sport:** prohibited under the WADA list, category S4 (as of 2026, please check for updates).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Established (registered studies, as of October 2026):**

- There is no reliably registered study in which MOTS-c is given as a drug.
- Several observational studies measure MOTS-c levels, for example in kidney transplantation (NCT07678073) or in diabetes under different medications (NCT03878706).

**Assessment:** The central question – does supplemental MOTS-c improve insulin sensitivity in humans? – has not yet been tested by anyone in a published study. That would first require a controlled phase 1/2 trial with published results. Until then, every statement about an effect in humans rests on animal experiments and level measurements.

### Conclusion

MOTS-c is an endogenous peptide encoded in the mitochondrial genome that improved metabolism and performance in mice and rises in humans after exercise. Observational studies show lower levels in overweight, fatty liver and vascular disease but prove neither causation nor a benefit of supplementation. The only completed study with a MOTS-c derivative (CB4211) has not been published in a scientific journal, and there is no published human study with MOTS-c itself. The data are not sufficient to assess benefit or safety in humans; in sport, MOTS-c is prohibited.
