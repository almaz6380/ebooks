## KPV

KPV is the smallest peptide in this book: only three amino acids long. Online it is offered as an anti-inflammatory agent for the gut and skin, often as a capsule or cream, sometimes for injection. The scientific basis for this consists of cell experiments and mouse models – there are no human studies.

### Profile

- **Type and structure:** tripeptide of the amino acids lysine, proline and valine (Lys-Pro-Val, hence KPV).
- **Origin:** corresponds to the last three amino acids (positions 11 to 13) of the endogenous hormone alpha-MSH (alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone), which is involved in skin tanning and the regulation of inflammation, among other things (Cutuli 2000, PMID 10670585).
- **Mechanism of action (presumed, from laboratory and animal studies):** inhibition of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways in gut and immune cells after uptake via the peptide transporter PepT1.
- **Approval EU/USA:** no.
- **WADA:** not explicitly listed; as a non-approved substance, in the author's view it falls under group S0 (as of 2026, please check for updates).
- **Evidence level: D** – no published human study.

### How it is supposed to work

Alpha-MSH is best known as a tanning hormone, but it also has anti-inflammatory effects. As early as the 1990s, researchers noticed that part of this effect depends on the last three amino acids. In the blood of people with HIV, alpha-MSH and its end piece KPV reduced the production of the inflammatory messengers interleukin-1 and TNF-alpha in the test tube (Catania 1998, PMID 9700761).

Unlike the complete hormone, KPV apparently does not act via the classic melanocortin receptors. In mice with peritonitis, KPV inhibited the influx of inflammatory cells without increasing the typical messenger cAMP; the authors suspect an inhibition of the interleukin-1 effect (Getting 2003, PMID 12750433). This has a practical advantage: unlike melanotan or alpha-MSH derivatives, KPV should not tan the skin.

A second mechanism concerns the gut. There sits the transporter PepT1, which normally takes up small protein fragments from food. In chronic inflammatory bowel diseases, PepT1 is also increasingly produced in the colon. In cultures of human gut and immune cells, KPV was taken up via PepT1 and, at very low concentrations, inhibited the inflammatory switches NF-kappaB and MAP kinases (Dalmasso 2008, PMID 18061177). From this arose the idea of using KPV as a tablet against intestinal inflammation.

In addition, alpha-MSH and KPV inhibited the growth of the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus and the yeast Candida albicans in the laboratory (Cutuli 2000, PMID 10670585).

### Human studies

**Scope of the evidence (search October 2026):** The automated search found **0** human studies in PubMed and **0** entries on ClinicalTrials.gov. A targeted follow-up search for pilot studies, case reports or adverse event reports also found no publication in which people received KPV.

What does exist are laboratory experiments with human cells or tissues, for example:

- blood samples from people with HIV, in which KPV reduced the production of inflammatory messengers (Catania 1998, PMID 9700761),
- human skin cell lines (Elliott 2004, PMID 15102092),
- donated human skin, on which it was tested whether KPV passes through the skin with microneedles and weak electric current – without such aids, it did not penetrate measurably (Pawar 2017, PMID 28343991).

These studies say something about mechanisms and dosage forms, but nothing about efficacy or safety in humans.

A close relative, the artificial double molecule (CKPV)2, was studied clinically in the mid-2000s as an agent against fungal infections; a 2006 paper mentions that it was "currently in clinical testing" (Gatti 2006, PMID 16413580). Results of these studies have not been published in PubMed. Moreover, (CKPV)2 is a different substance from the KPV sold online.

### Animal and laboratory studies

The focus of research is on chronic inflammatory bowel diseases. All of the following results come from mouse experiments:

| Study | Model | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Dalmasso 2008, PMID 18061177 | chemically induced colitis in mice, KPV in drinking water | less inflammation, fewer inflammatory messengers |
| Kannengiesser 2008, PMID 18092346 | two colitis models in mice | faster recovery, fewer inflammatory cells; in one model all treated animals survived |
| Viennois 2016, PMID 27458604 | inflammation-associated colorectal cancer in mice | KPV prevented tumor development, but only in mice with functioning PepT1 |
| Xiao 2017, PMID 28143741 | colitis in mice, KPV in nanoparticles | stronger effect than unbound KPV |

Further studies investigate KPV in hydrogels for wounds and mucous membranes, in nanoparticles against vascular calcification or in the metabolism of fat cells – almost exclusively in cell culture or in rodents. It is striking that a large part of the more recent literature develops dosage forms (nanoparticles, gels, patches). This shows that KPV alone, as a tablet or cream, apparently does not reach the site of action well enough – a point that is relevant for the freely sold products.

### Risks and side effects

#### Documented

None – because there are no human studies. The absence of reported side effects is no indication of safety here.

#### Theoretical

- **Dampening of defenses:** A substance that inhibits inflammatory signals could in principle also impair defense against infection. The laboratory findings point rather to its own antimicrobial effect (Cutuli 2000, PMID 10670585), but human data are lacking.
- **Unclear uptake and dose:** How much KPV from a capsule or cream actually becomes effective has not been studied in humans.
- **Gray-market quality:** KPV is sold as a "research peptide" without pharmaceutical control. A sports medicine review explicitly names KPV among the peptide fragments circulating in bodybuilding circles and warns of mislabeled or contaminated products (Coutinho 2026, PMID 41880199).

### Legal status

*As of 2026, please check for updates.*

- **EU, Germany, Austria:** not approved as a medicine. If KPV is offered with claims about alleviating diseases, it is considered a non-approved medicine. As an ingredient of cosmetics, the rules of the EU Cosmetics Regulation apply; health claims are impermissible there.
- **USA:** no FDA approval.
- **WADA:** not listed by name. As a pharmacologically active substance without approval, in the author's view KPV falls under S0 (non-approved substances).

### Ongoing studies and outlook

**Confirmed:** As of October 2026, no trial with KPV is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov.

**Author's assessment:** The mouse data on intestinal inflammation are consistent and come from several independent groups – that is more than many other "research peptides" can show. Without a company developing KPV as a medicine, however, a human study is not in sight. It is more likely that KPV will continue to be researched in the laboratory as a building block in new dosage forms (nanoparticles, gels).

### Conclusion

KPV is a three-amino-acid fragment of the endogenous hormone alpha-MSH that markedly reduced intestinal inflammation in cell cultures and in mice (Dalmasso 2008, PMID 18061177; Kannengiesser 2008, PMID 18092346). KPV has never been studied in humans – neither for efficacy nor for safety. All claims about effects on the gut or skin in humans are therefore assumptions based on animal experiments. Anyone using KPV products from the internet is part of an uncontrolled self-experiment with products of unknown quality.
